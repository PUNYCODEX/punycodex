Name and Meaning (Etymology)

The name means "Thunderbolt (the weapon of Zeus)" and reflects the figure's role in the tradition. Keraunós is the thunderbolt itself, the weapon by which Zeus enforces cosmic law. In Hesiod and the Homeric poems the thunderbolt is not merely a meteorological event but a missile forged by the Cyclopes and entrusted to Zeus after the defeat of the Titans. Its flash marks the boundary between permissible and forbidden action; those struck by it are singled out by divine judgment. The epithet Keraunios attaches Zeus to this fearsome power and distinguishes him from the calmer sky-god of fair weather.

In Greek art the keraunós appears as a double-headed axe or a bundle of smoking thunder-darts with jagged zig-zag ends. Zeus hurls it in Gigantomachy scenes; Roman copies show Jupiter with the same weapon.

The bolt's form communicates instantaneous, irresistible force — the visual signature of cosmic justice.

Epithets & Cult Titles

Zeus bears thunderbolt epithets that pass to his weapon: Κεραύνιος (Keraunios, "of the thunderbolt"), Βρονταῖος (Brontaios, "of thunder"), and Τερπικέραυνος (Terpikeraunos, "delighting in the thunderbolt"). The keraunós is also called αἰθαλόεις (aithaloēis, smoky, blazing).

Each epithet stresses a different aspect of the bolt: its sound, its fire, and the pleasure Zeus takes in its use.

The Homeric Hymns

The keraunós — the thunderbolt — is the weapon the Cyclopes forged for Zeus and gave him in gratitude for their release from Tartaros, as recounted in the Theogony and echoed in the hymnic tradition. The Homeric Hymn to Zeus (no. 23) names him "greatest" and "fulfiller," the god whose will the thunderbolt executes.

The short hymn therefore presupposes the keraunós as the visible sign of Zeus's sovereign power.

Oracle Sites & Sanctuaries

Sanctuaries of Zeus Keraunios dotted the Greek world, especially on mountaintops exposed to lightning. At Olympia the altar of Zeus received thunderbolt-shaped offerings, while at Dodona Zeus spoke through his oak before the storm.

The thunderbolt thus linked oracular centres with the sky: the same god who hurled the keraunós also pronounced judgment through these shrines.
