Original Script

The name in its original Mesopotamian form. Dumuzid (𒀭𒌉𒍣) is attested in the source tradition — “Faithful son”. Its original diacritics and script distinctions carry the full phonetic and orthographic weight of the source tradition.

ASCII Constraint

dumuzid

The plain dumuzid form is identical to the Unicode restoration. Because this name is already written in Latin letters, no diacritics, stress, or script information were lost — only capitalization differs.

Unicode Restoration

Dumuzid

The Unicode restoration does not need to recover lost marks for Dumuzid. Its value is canonical spelling and consistent cataloguing, not the reconstruction of erased orthography. The domain is readable as-is to both DNS and humanity.

Pronunciation

How Dumuzid was spoken

/duˈmu.zid/ Sumerian/Akkadian Reconstruction
Du- Voiced alveolar stop [d] plus close back rounded vowel [u]; the Sumerian word dumu means 'son'.
-mu- Bilabial nasal [m] plus close back rounded vowel [u]; the second syllable carries the reconstructed stress.
-zid Voiced alveolar fricative [z] plus voiceless dental stop [d]; zid means 'true, faithful, right' — the ethical core of the name.

Modern Approximation

'doo-MOO-zid' — the first two syllables are soft and round, the last closes crisply like 'zid'.

Etymological Kin

  • Sumerian 𒌉𒍣 (dumu-zid), 'faithful son', often written with the divine determinative 𒀭
  • Akkadian Tammūzu, the East Semitic form attested from the Old Akkadian period onward
  • Hebrew/Biblical Tammuz (תַּמּוּז), mourned at the Jerusalem temple gate in Ezekiel 8:14

The Accent / Script Rule

Dumuzid is Tier 2: the canonical restoration uses plain Latin letters with no distinctive diacritic or non-ASCII letter. The Sumerian cuneiform signs carry meanings — dumu 'son' and zid 'faithful' — that the Latin form cannot display; it is therefore a readable scholarly transliteration rather than a phonological reconstruction.

05

The Faithful Son

Shepherds, Vegetation, and the Sacred Marriage

Dumuzid is the Sumerian shepherd made divine — the 'faithful son' whose name joins dumu ('son') and zid ('true, faithful, right'). He is the lord of the sheepfold, the provider of milk and wool, and the bridegroom of Inanna in the sacred marriage that guarantees the land's fertility. Unlike the storm-gods who rule by force, Dumuzid reigns through the gentle goods of the pasture: grass, water, flock, and the cyclic return of vegetation.

His myth is also the myth of seasonal loss. In the Descent of Inanna he is chosen to take the goddess's place in the underworld for half the year, so that life and death may alternate on earth. Dumuzid is therefore not only the patron of shepherds but the archetype of the dying-and-rising vegetation god, mourned as Tammuz across the ancient Near East.

Lord of the Sheepfold

Patron of shepherds, milk, cheese, and wool; the pastoral economy of Sumer consecrated in one figure.

Date Palm and Garden

His wedding songs praise the date palm, the garden, and the sprouting reed — the green world as bridal gift.

Sacred Marriage

The union of Dumuzid and Inanna legitimates kingship and assures fertility; every New Year reenacts their cosmic wedding.

Descent and Return

Half the year in the underworld, half on earth with his sister Geshtinanna — the seasonal cycle made myth.

06

Mythology

Stories of Dumuzid

Dumuzid's myths are woven through the great Sumerian literary corpus: courtship songs, sacred-marriage hymns, the Descent of Inanna, and the laments for his death. They turn on the tension between abundance and loss, intimacy and exile, the green world and the dark earth.

Courtship

The Shepherd and the Farmer

In the Sumerian poem Dumuzi and Enkimdu (ETCSL 4.08.33), Inanna must choose between Dumuzid, the shepherd, and Enkimdu, the farmer. The shepherd boasts of milk, cheese, and wool; the farmer counters with grain, bread, and beer. Inanna prefers Dumuzid, and the poem dramatizes the prestige of the pastoral economy alongside agriculture in early Mesopotamia.

Sacred Marriage

The Bridal of Heaven and Earth

The sacred-marriage songs (ETCSL 4.08.01–04) celebrate Dumuzid as the bridegroom of Inanna. The king of Uruk embodies Dumuzid, while the goddess embodies the city and the land. Their union is not merely symbolic: it guarantees the me of fertility, the sprouting of grain, the filling of folds, and the legitimacy of the reigning king.

Descent

Dumuzid in the Underworld

In the Descent of Inanna (ETCSL 1.4.1), Inanna is killed in the underworld and resurrected only on condition that she send a substitute. She chooses Dumuzid, her beloved husband, because he did not mourn her. The galla demons drag him down, but his sister Geshtinanna offers to share his fate: Dumuzid will spend half the year in the underworld, Geshtinanna the other half. This is the Sumerian explanation of summer's parch and winter's return.

Lament

The Weeping for Tammuz

Sumerian city laments (e.g., The Death of Dumuzid, ETCSL 4.08.32) and later Akkadian tradition mourn the shepherd's death. The biblical prophet Ezekiel witnesses women 'weeping for Tammuz' at the Jerusalem temple gate (Ezekiel 8:14), a rite that travelled from Mesopotamia to the Levant. The tears are not only grief; they are a petition for the god's return and the restoration of vegetation.

Extended Lore

Dumuzid is the god who must be lost so that the world can turn. His death is not punishment for a moral fault but the structural price of Inanna's return from the underworld. In choosing him, the goddess does not act out of cruelty; she acts out of the logic that life and death must alternate, that the green world must descend into darkness in order to rise again.
