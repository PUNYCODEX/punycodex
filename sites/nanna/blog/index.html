The many faces of Nanna

No important name has only one face. Nanna appears as a figure of myth, a scholarly reconstruction, a piece of material culture, a memory carried across languages, and — most recently — a Unicode domain. This post looks at each face in turn: the name and its roots, the Younger Futhark original, the reconstructed pronunciation, the mythological record, the symbols and sanctuaries, the cross-cultural afterlife, and the engineering that lets the restored spelling resolve in a browser. Taken together, those faces explain why nanna was never going to be enough — and why the restored form is worth a domain of its own.

At a Glance

Overview

Nanna (nanna) — Joy, Wife of Baldr · Mother, daring — is the wife of Baldr the Good, daughter of the otherwise unknown Nepr, and mother of Forseti: the only figure in the Norse corpus who dies of grief itself, her heart bursting at the sight of her husband's pyre on the ship Hringhorni, and the dead goddess who sends gifts back up the Hel-road — a linen robe for Frigg and a gold ring for Fulla — when Hermóðr comes to ransom the slain god.

The name's etymology is genuinely disputed. One line derives it from Germanic nanþ-, 'daring, striving'; the other takes it as a nursery word (a Lallname) of the 'mother' type — hence the traditional double gloss 'mother, daring'. Snorri gives her the two scenes the tradition never forgot; Saxo Grammaticus gives her a second, Danish life as the wooed daughter of King Gevarus, for whose hand Høtherus and Balderus go to war.

PuniCodex restores the name as Nanna — identical to the ASCII input, because normalized Old Norse writes no diacritic in this name. The mechanical rule therefore places the name in Tier 2: nothing distinctive is lost in transit, a statement about orthography and never about the goddess. The temple is domain-less: the plain-ASCII nanna.com is registered to a third party outside the collection and is watched for release.

The Name

The name is attested in Old Norse as Nanna — the goddess's name and, rarely, a human personal name. Its etymology is one of the honest open questions of the onomasticon. The stronger philological line connects it with Germanic nanþ- 'to dare', the root behind Old English nēþan and Gothic nanþjan — yielding 'the daring one'; the competing reading takes Nanna as a nursery word of the widespread 'mother' type (compare Greek nanna 'aunt' and kindred Lallnamen), a pet-name elevated to a theonym. Cleasby–Vigfusson record the name under both possibilities without settling the question, and neither do Simek and Orchard.

The ASCII form is not a fallback here but the form itself: normalized Old Norse writes five plain Latin letters and a capital. The Unicode restoration Nanna therefore preserves nothing the ASCII alphabet loses, and the mechanical rule classifies the name Tier 2 — an orthographic fact, not a judgment on the goddess.

The letter-by-letter transformation runs:

  • n → N — Same, capitalized
  • a → a — Same
  • n → n — Same
  • n → n — Same
  • a → a — Same

The Original Script

The name is preserved in medieval Icelandic manuscripts in the Latin alphabet as Nanna — Old Norse (North Germanic), attested 13th c. CE in the Codex Regius of the Prose Edda and related codices, recording material centuries older. Before the Latin bookhand, Old Norse names were carved in the Younger Futhark; no runic attestation of this theonym survives, so the manuscript form is the primary witness. The runic rendering is ᚾᛅᚾᛅ (n-a-n-a).

The scholarly rendering proceeds step by step:

  • The name is a simplex, not a compound: five letters, two syllables, a geminate nn the manuscripts write double.
  • Old Norse orthography needs no diacritic here: both vowels are short a, and no distinctive letter (þ, ð, æ, ǫ) occurs in the name.
  • The Unicode restoration Nanna is therefore identical to the ASCII form — the restoration is a matter of canonical reference, not rescued orthography.
  • The name's disputed etymology — Germanic nanþ- 'daring' against a nursery word of the 'mother' type — is registered in the lexica, not settled by the spelling.

Pronunciation

The reconstructed pronunciation of the name is /ˈnɑn.nɑ/ — Old Norse Reconstruction.

Phoneme by phoneme:

  • Na- — Voiced alveolar nasal [n] and short open back unrounded [ɑ], stressed on the first syllable as in every Old Norse word
  • -nna — The geminate nasal [nː] held audibly across the syllable boundary, then the final short open [ɑ], fully sounded rather than reduced to schwa

For the modern speaker, the closest approximation is: 'NAHN-nah' — two equal beats, the double n held a fraction long in the middle, as in modern Icelandic Nanna.

Kindred and historical forms of the name:

  • Old Norse — Nanna, daughter of Nepr, wife of Baldr
  • Younger Futhark — ᚾᛅᚾᛅ (n-a-n-a), the Viking-Age runic rendering
  • Latin — Nanna, unchanged; Saxo Grammaticus writes the name exactly as the Icelanders did
  • Modern Scandinavian — Nanna, an unbroken living given name in Danish and Icelandic

Nanna is Tier 2 because the restoration preserves nothing the ASCII form loses: no diacritic, no distinctive letter, no vowel length. English and Old Norse write the name identically.

Mythology

Nanna's mythology is two scenes in Snorri and a counter-life in Saxo — among the shortest dossiers in the Edda, and among the most devastating.

The Wife of Baldr (Gylfaginning 32)

Snorri names her in the survey of the Æsir's households: Baldr's wife is Nanna, daughter of Nepr, and their son is Forseti. Of Nepr nothing else is told — the father exists only to place the daughter. The son inherits a hall and an office: Forseti dwells in Glitnir, whose pillars are red gold and whose roof is silver, and stills all strife between gods and men.

The Heart That Burst (Gylfaginning 49)

When Baldr fell to the mistletoe thrown by blind Höðr at Loki's guidance, the Æsir bore him to the sea and built his pyre aboard Hringhorni, greatest of all ships, launched by the giantess Hyrrokkin. When Nanna saw her husband's body carried to the ship, her heart burst with grief and she died; she was laid on the pyre beside him. Óðinn laid the ring Draupnir on the flames; Þórr hallowed the pyre with Mjölnir; Baldr's horse was led to the fire with all its gear. No weapon touches her; no enemy wills her death — the one death in the corpus by love alone.

The Gifts from Hel (Gylfaginning 49)

Hermóðr the Bold rides nine nights to Hel and finds Baldr in the high seat — and Nanna beside him, keeping her place in death as in life. Hel sets the condition: all things, living and lifeless, must weep Baldr out. When Hermóðr turns for home, the dead send tokens: Baldr sends Draupnir back to Óðinn, and Nanna sends a robe of linen to Frigg with other gifts, and a gold finger-ring to Fulla. The ransom fails at the giantess Þökk's dry tears — 'let Hel hold what she has' — and Nanna and Baldr remain in Hel together.

Saxo's Counter-Life (Gesta Danorum III)

Saxo Grammaticus gives Nanna the fuller life the Icelanders withheld — and a different husband. His Nanna is daughter of Gevarus, king of Norway; Høtherus, Gevarus's foster-son, sues for her hand, and she answers with a set speech on the ill match between mortal and demigod. But Balderus, son of Odin, desires her too, and the rivalry becomes war: Høtherus, armed with the sword Mimming, defeats the gods' fleet at sea and wins Nanna's hand; only later does he wound the invulnerable Balderus with the one blade that can harm him. Snorri's Nanna is the wife who dies on her husband's pyre; Saxo's is the bride two rivals bleed for — the same woman read as devotion in Iceland and as desire's prize in Denmark.

Symbols & Iconography

The iconography of Nanna is narrative rather than cultic — the corpus gives her no temple regalia, but fixed around her two scenes a small set of objects no retelling can omit:

  • The linen robe — the gift sent from Hel to Frigg, her husband's mother: domestic love that death does not cancel
  • The gold finger-ring — Fulla's keepsake from the dead: friendship remembered across the boundary, small and exact
  • The pyre of Hringhorni — the fire she entered unslain and unbidden: grief as its own cause of death
  • Glitnir — the hall of red gold and silver where her son Forseti dwells, stilling all strife: the household's survival in the world above
  • The high seat in Hel — her place beside Baldr in the hall of the dead when Hermóðr arrives: marriage persisting past the grave

Archaeology & Evidence

Nanna is a wholly textual goddess: no runestone invokes her, no votive deposit names her, and no image can be securely identified with her. Her attestation hangs on the manuscript witnesses of the Baldr cycle — the Codex Regius and related codices of Snorri's Edda for Gylfaginning 32 and 49, and the medieval Danish tradition of Saxo's Gesta Danorum for her second life as Gevarus's daughter.

The nearest material context is indirect. Úlfr Uggason's Húsdrápa (c. 983), quoted in Skáldskaparmál, proves that Baldr's funeral was carved on the panels of Óláfr pái's hall at Hjarðarholt a generation before Iceland's conversion — the procession of gods and the launch of Hringhorni were pictures as well as verses — though the surviving stanzas do not name Nanna herself. The eddic poems that touch her household survive in the Codex Regius: Grímnismál 15 numbers Glitnir, her son Forseti's hall, among heaven's dwellings.

Realm & Domain

Nanna is catalogued under Joy, Wife of Baldr — and the pairing is exact: the sources give her no independent office, no hall of her own, no cult; her whole divine identity is the marriage and what survives it.

Joy

The household of Baldr and Nanna is the corpus's one picture of unshadowed divine happiness — the fairest god, his wife, and their son Forseti, whose hall Glitnir stills all strife. Joy is what the myth establishes so that grief can be measured against it.

Wife of Baldr

Snorri introduces her with genealogical economy — Baldr's wife, Nepr's daughter — and everything the tradition remembers of her flows from the marriage: the death on his pyre, the place beside him in Hel, the gifts sent home to his mother and his household.

Grief

Hers is the only death in the Edda that needs no weapon: seeing Baldr on the pyre, her heart burst. In a corpus of spear-thrusts and poison, the death by love alone is the starkest statement that the gods are not exempt from loss.

Devotion

From Hel, where nothing returns, she answers Hermóðr's errand with household gifts — linen for Frigg, a gold ring for Fulla: devotion keeping its accounts past death's door.

Across Cultures

Nanna's closest kin are inside the corpus itself: she is the divine pattern of which Brynhildr is the heroic echo — the woman who mounts her dead beloved's pyre, as Brynhildr has herself burned beside Sigurðr in the Völsung cycle. The parallel is structural, not borrowed: two tellings of the northern conviction that grief can be a cause of death and a reason to follow.

Cross-culturally, nineteenth-century scholarship reached further afield — to Alcestis dying in her husband's place, and to the debated Indo-European comparisons around the widow on the pyre — but current handbooks are cooler. Simek and Orchard treat Nanna as a figure of the Baldr complex whose attestation is too thin for cult reconstruction and whose etymology is too disputed for safe comparison; what can be said cleanly is that no other northern goddess is defined so entirely by a single act of grief, and none answers from the grave with so domestic a grace as a robe and a ring. Within the corpus, her related figures are the household her story binds together: Baldr, Forseti, Frigg, and Fulla.

Cultural Legacy

Nanna's afterlife runs quietly through the Baldr story's long reception: every retelling of the death of the shining god keeps her two scenes — the burst heart on the shore and the gifts entrusted to Hermóðr. The Kearys' The Heroes of Asgard (1857), the first popular English retelling, gave both moments to generations of children, and every modern handbook of Norse myth follows.

Nineteenth-century romanticism made much of the wife on the pyre; twentieth-century scholarship made her the test case for how little the Edda needs to say. Her sturdiest legacy is the name itself: Nanna never died out in the North and remains a living given name in Danish and Icelandic, borne today by women who inherit the most grief-stricken and most faithful name in the Edda. Modern Heathen and Ásatrú communities remember her in Baldr rites as the goddess of devotion measured past death's door.

A Meditation

Nanna is the Edda's proof that grief is not a lesser fate than battle. Every other death in the Baldr cycle is negotiated — prophesied, sworn against, tricked, ransomed, avenged. Hers alone simply happens, at the sight of the pyre, and the narrative grants it without comment, as if the gods understood this was a death no oath could have prevented.

And then the tradition does something finer still: it lets her act after death. From Hel, where nothing returns, she sends a robe and a ring — not pleas, not prophecy, but the remembered kindnesses of a daughter-in-law and a friend. The ransom fails; the gifts arrive. In a mythology hurtling toward Ragnarǫk, Nanna is the small, exact consolation: that love keeps its accounts even in the land of the dead, and pays them in linen and gold.

The Unicode Restoration

Nanna is classified as Tier 2: the restoration needs no distinctive letters or diacritics its ASCII form would lose. The ASCII fallback nanna still resolves everywhere, but it is the restored form that carries the name's full information. That is the whole thesis of this temple: the marks are the message.

Why This Restoration Matters

Myth, script, sound, cult, legacy, domain: the faces of Nanna add up to a single argument — that a name is a record, and records deserve fidelity. The PuniCodex restoration keeps that record in working order: the temple presents it, the Scholarly Edition footnotes it, the lexicon catalogs it, and the domain makes it addressable. nanna will always exist as a fallback. But fallback is not identity. Nanna is the name; everything else is a convenience.
