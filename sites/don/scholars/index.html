01

Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Don (don) ​— Mother of ‌Gods · Goddess ‌— is the ​matriarch of the ​Mabinogi's magical ​house: sister of ​Math fab Mathonwy, ​lord of Gwynedd, ‌and mother of ​the kindred the ​Welsh tradition called ‌Plant Dôn, the ​Children of Dôn ‌— Gwydion the ​enchanter, Gilfaethwy ‌his brother, Arianrhod ​ferch Dôn, Gofannon ​the smith, and ​Amaethon the ploughman.[1] ​She is the ​only major figure ‌of the Four ​Branches who never ‌speaks, acts, ​or appears: the ‌whole of the ​Fourth Branch, Math ‌fab Mathonwy, unfolds ​among her children ‌and her brother, ​and she is ​present only in ‌the matronymics they ‌carry — ‌uab Dôn, son ​of Dôn; uerch ​Dôn, daughter of ‌Dôn.[1]

The name's ​etymology is genuinely ​unsettled. The lexicon's ‌traditional gloss is ​'Goddess', reflecting the ​standard derivation from ‌Common Celtic ​dēwā, 'goddess' — ‌the same word ​the Welsh language ​preserves in Dyfrdwy, ​its name for ​the River Dee; ‌a river-name origin ‌is also argued, ​and the handbooks ‌leave the ​question open.[2] The ​long-standing comparison with ‌the Irish Danu, ‌mother in name ‌of the Túatha ‌Dé Danann, is ​registered by the ‌reference works as ​typological, not genealogical.[3]

​PuniCodex restores ​the name as ‌Don — identical ​to the ASCII ​input, because the ​Middle Welsh manuscripts ‌write the name ​in plain Latin ​letters and the ​circumflex of ‌modern Welsh Dôn ​is a modern ‌length-mark, not a ​letter the manuscripts ‌carry; the name ​is Tier 2 ‌by the mechanical ‌rule. No domain ​is claimed: the ​plain-ASCII don.com ​is registered to ​a third party ‌outside the collection ‌and is watched ​for release.[4]

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

The name ​is attested in Middle ‌Welsh as Don, matriarch ‌of the northern divine ​house, in the Fourth ​Branch of the Mabinogi ​and in Culhwch ac ​Olwen — always ​in the matronymic: Gwydion ‌uab Dôn, Gilfaethwy uab ​Dôn, Arianrhod uerch Dôn, ​Gofannon uab Dôn, Amaethon ‌uab Dôn.[1] The genealogical ​tradition supplies her own ‌lineage — Dôn ​daughter of Mathonwy — ‌which makes her sister ​to Math fab Mathonwy ​and explains her children's ​standing at his court; ​the later genealogies ​give her Beli Mawr ‌for a husband, a ​marriage the Mabinogi itself ‌never mentions.[2]

The etymology ​behind the lexicon's gloss ‌'Goddess' is the standard ​one, and it ‌is disputed. The traditional ​derivation runs to Common ‌Celtic dēwā, 'goddess', the ​word the Welsh language ​still keeps in Dyfrdwy, ‌the River Dee; the ‌competing reading takes ‌the name as a ​river-name of the Danube–Don ​type. The comparison with ‌Irish Danu — mother ​in name of the ​Túatha Dé Danann, 'the ‌peoples of the ​goddess Danu' — has ​been a commonplace of ‌Celtic studies since the ​nineteenth century, but no ‌Welsh text equates the ​names, and the ​modern handbooks register the ​parallel without endorsing the ​identification.[3]

The ASCII form ‌is not a compromise ‌here: Middle Welsh writes ​the name in plain ‌Latin letters, and ​the normalized temple spelling ​follows the manuscripts as ‌Don. Modern Welsh orthography ‌writes Dôn with a ‌circumflex, but that mark ‌is a modern length-sign ​for the long ‌vowel, not a letter ​any manuscript carries. There ​is therefore no diacritic ​and no distinctive letter ‌for the restoration to ​recover; only the capital ​distinguishes the restored ​form from the input, ‌which places the name ​in Tier 2 by ​the mechanical rule.

The ​letter-by-letter transformation runs:

  • ‌d → D ​— Same, capitalized
  • ‌o → o — ​Same
  • n → ‌n — Same

The ​project holds no domain ‌for this name: don.com ‌is registered to ​a third party outside ​the PuniCodex collection and ​is watched for release. ​The temple is served ‌at /don/ and claims ‌no domain it does ​not hold.

Sources

  1. Davies, Sioned, trans., The Mabinogion (Oxford, 2007), 'Math fab Mathonwy'; Bromwich, Rachel, and D. Simon Evans, eds., Culhwch and Olwen (Cardiff: University of Wales Press, 1992).
  2. MacKillop, James, Dictionary of Celtic Mythology (Oxford, 1998), s.vv. Dôn, Beli Mawr; Koch, Celtic Culture (2006), s.v. Dôn.
  3. Jackson, Kenneth H., Language and History in Early Britain (Edinburgh, 1953); MacKillop, Dictionary (1998), s.v. Danu; Koch, Celtic Culture (2006), s.vv. Dôn, Danu, Dee.

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The pronunciation ​of the ‌name is ‌/doːn/ — Modern ​Welsh.[1]

Phoneme ​by phoneme:

​- D- ​— Voiced alveolar ​stop [d], ‌fully voiced ​as in English ​'door'

  • ‌-ô- — ​Long close-mid ‌back rounded [oː], ​the vowel ‌modern Welsh ​orthography marks with ​the circumflex; ​a pure ​sustained 'oh', never ​the diphthong ‌of English ​'doe'
  • ‌-n — Final ​alveolar nasal ‌[n], closing ​the single syllable ‌cleanly

For ​the modern ‌speaker, the closest ​approximation is: ​'DOHN' — ‌one syllable, ‌one long pure ‌'oh' held ​steady, like ​English 'dawn' with ‌the vowel ​kept clean.

​Kindred and ‌historical forms of ​the name:

​- Middle ‌Welsh — Don, ​the manuscript ‌form in ​the White Book ​of Rhydderch ​and the ​Red Book ‌of Hergest, written ‌without any ​length mark
‌- Modern Welsh ​— Dôn, ​the same ‌name with ‌the circumflex that ‌modern orthography ‌uses to ​mark the long ‌vowel

  • ​Welsh river-name ​— Dyfrdwy, the ​Welsh name ‌of the ​River Dee, ​from *Dēwā 'the ​goddess'
  • ‌Irish comparison ​— Danu (or ​Dana), the ​standing handbook ‌comparison, never an ​equation[2]

Don ‌is Tier ​2 because ‌the restoration preserves ​nothing ASCII ‌loses: the ‌letters of the ​manuscript form ​are the ​letters of ​the keyboard. The ‌capital alone ‌marks the ​temple form.

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

The name ​is preserved ‌in Middle Welsh ‌manuscripts in ​the Latin alphabet ​as Don ​— Middle Welsh ​(Brythonic Celtic), ​attested in the ‌White Book ​of Rhydderch (c. ​1350) and ‌the Red Book ​of Hergest ‌(c. 1375–1420), recording ​material composed ‌centuries earlier.[1] The ​script is ​written left-to-right in ​the medieval ​Welsh bookhand.

The ​scholarly rendering ‌proceeds step ​by step:

  • ‌Middle Welsh ​writes the name ‌in three ​plain Latin letters; ‌the manuscripts ​carry no mark ‌of any ​kind on the ​vowel.
  • ‌Modern Welsh orthography ‌writes Dôn ‌with a circumflex, ​but that ​mark is a ‌modern length-sign ​for the long ​vowel /oː/, ‌not a letter ​any manuscript ​carries; the temple ‌form therefore ​follows the manuscript ‌spelling.[2]
  • ​The etymology is ​unsettled — ​Common Celtic dēwā ​'goddess' (the ‌reading behind the ‌lexicon's gloss) ​beside a river-name ‌origin — ​but no variant ​spelling of ‌the name is ‌attested in ‌the manuscripts.[3]
  • ‌The Unicode ​restoration Don therefore ‌changes nothing ​but the capital; ​the name ​stands in Tier ‌2 by ​the mechanical ​rule, and the ​plain-ASCII domain ‌don.com — held ​by a ​third party outside ​the PuniCodex ‌collection and watched ​for release ‌— is the ​philologically complete ‌form.

One caution ​attaches to ‌the record: the ‌gloss 'Goddess' ​is the traditional ​etymological rendering, ​not a settled ​philological result, ‌and this edition ‌presents it ​as such.[3]

Sources

  1. Davies, Sioned, trans., The Mabinogion (Oxford, 2007), introduction, on the manuscript witnesses.
  2. Geiriadur Prifysgol Cymru / A Dictionary of the Welsh Language (Cardiff, 1950–2002), s.v. Dôn.
  3. Jackson, Kenneth H., Language and History in Early Britain (Edinburgh, 1953); Koch, Celtic Culture (2006), s.vv. Dôn, Dee.

Domains & Attributes

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sphere of influence, titles, epithets, domain cards.

Don is ​catalogued under ‌the domain 'Mother ‌of Gods' — ​a domain ​that is a ​genealogy, and ​the genealogy is ​the whole ‌record.[1]

The ​Sister-Sons

Gwydion and ​Gilfaethwy, sons ‌of Dôn, are ​Math's sister-sons ‌and the animating ​pair of ‌the Fourth Branch: ​Gwydion 'the best ​teller of ​tales in the ​world', the ​foremost enchanter of ‌Welsh prose, and ​Gilfaethwy, whose ‌desire for Goewin ​his brother ‌turns into a ​war. Every ‌introduction names them ​through the mother.[1]

‌### The ​Daughter

Arianrhod ferch ​Dôn is ‌proposed as Math's ‌footholder, tested ‌by the wand, ​and revealed as ​the mother ‌of Dylan Eil ​Ton and ​of the boy ‌who becomes Lleu; ​her three ​tynghedau against her ‌son drive ​the branch's second ‌half. She ​is the Fourth ​Branch's proudest and ​most wounded ​figure.[1]

The ‌Craftsman-Sons

Culhwch ‌ac Olwen adds ​the older arts: ‌Gofannon fab ​Dôn the smith ​and Amaethon ‌fab Dôn the ‌ploughman, each ‌indispensable to Ysbaddaden's ‌impossible tasks — ​the forge ‌and the furrow ​lodged in ​the matriarch's house.[2]

​### The ‌House Named for ​the Mother

Plant ​Dôn, the ​Children of Dôn, ‌stands beside ​Plant Llŷr as ​one of the ​two great ‌kindreds of the ​Mabinogi world ‌— a divine ​house organized ‌around a mother ​who never appears ‌in it. ‌The parallel with ​Ireland's Túatha ​Dé Danann, the ​peoples of ​the goddess Danu, ‌is the standard ‌comparison of ​the handbooks.[3]

Symbols

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Iconography, attributes, and their meanings.

Dôn ​has no ‌regalia of ‌her ​own — ​the ​sources give ​her none. ​Her ‌symbolic record ​is ​carried by ‌her house:[1]

​- ‌The matronymic ​(uab ‌Dôn) — ​'Son of ​Dôn': ​the mother-name ​standing ​at the ‌head of ​every ‌child's name, ​her ‌whole presence ​in the ‌text
​- Math's ‌wand ​— Her ​brother's instrument ‌of ‌testing and ‌transformation, ​laid across ​her children's ‌fates: ​the wand ​that ‌tested Arianrhod ​and punished ​Gwydion ‌and Gilfaethwy
​- ‌Plough and ​anvil — ​Amaethon's ​plough and ​Gofannon's ‌smithy, the ‌crafts of ​cultivation ‌and making ​her ​sons carry ‌into the ‌oldest ‌tale[2]

  • ‌The ​Dee (Dyfrdwy) ‌— The ​river ​the Welsh ​still ‌call 'the ​goddess' — ​*Dēwā, ​the name's ‌closest ​living relative ​in the ​landscape[3]

‌- The ​star-castles ‌— Caer ​Arianrhod and ‌Caer ​Gwydion, her ‌daughter's ‌and her ​son's halls ​written ​into the ​Welsh ‌night sky ‌by later ​tradition[4]

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

Dôn has no ​myth of her own — no ‌tale, no speech, no deed the ‌sources record. What she has is ​a family, and the family is ​the mythology.[1]

The Sister-Sons at ​Math's Court (Math fab Mathonwy)

The ​Fourth Branch opens at the court ​of Math fab Mathonwy, lord of ‌Gwynedd — who cannot live unless ​his feet rest in a virgin's ​lap, except in time of war ‌— and introduces its great men ​by their mother's name: Gwydion and ‌Gilfaethwy, sons of Dôn, Math's sister-sons. ​Gilfaethwy desires Goewin, the maiden of ‌the foothold; Gwydion engineers a war ​against Pryderi of Dyfed with conjured ​stallions, hounds, and shields that vanish ​after a day; and while the ​king is in the field, the ​brothers force Goewin. Math's justice turns ‌the brothers through three years of ​animal shapes — stag and hind, ‌boar and sow, wolf and she-wolf ​— and takes them back into ‌honour when the debt is paid. ​Through all of it the mother ‌never appears: she is present exactly ​as a name inside other names.[1]

‌### Arianrhod ferch Dôn and the ​Wand (Math fab Mathonwy)

When Math ​needs a new footholder, Gwydion proposes ‌his sister, Arianrhod daughter of Dôn. ‌Stepping over the wand to prove ‌her virginity, she drops a yellow-haired ​boy — Dylan, who makes at ​once for the sea — and ‌'something small' that Gwydion hides in ​a chest: the boy who becomes ​Lleu. Shamed, Arianrhod lays the three ‌tynghedau on her son — no ​name until she names him, no ​arms until she arms him, no ‌wife of any race then on ​earth — and the rest of ‌the branch is Gwydion undoing each ​destiny by craft, down to the ​making of Blodeuwedd from flowers.[1]

​The Ploughman and the Smith (Culhwch ​ac Olwen)

The oldest Arthurian tale ‌names the anoethau, the impossible tasks ‌set by Ysbaddaden Bencawr, and two ​sons of Dôn stand inside the ‌list: Amaethon fab Dôn, without whom ​the giant's ground cannot be tilled, ​and Gofannon fab Dôn the smith, ‌who must be brought to the ‌work of the headlands. Agriculture and ‌smithcraft, the two oldest arts, belong ‌to the matriarch's sons.[2]

The ​Futile Battle (Welsh Triads; Kat Godeu)

‌The triad of the Three Futile ​Battles of the Island of Britain ​gives one to the house of ​Dôn: Cad Goddeu, the Battle of ‌the Trees, fought on account of ​a bitch, a roebuck, and a ​lapwing that Amathaon fab Dôn brought ​away from Annwn. The Book of ‌Taliesin's poem Kat Godeu remembers the ​same war from the other side ​of the family — Gwydion enchanting ​the forest into an army against ‌Arawn. One son's theft begins the ​quarrel; another son's enchantment fights it.[3]

‌### Plant Dôn (the genealogical tradition)

​The Welsh tradition organized its divine ‌world by the mother's name: Plant ​Dôn beside Plant Llŷr. The genealogies ‌make Dôn a daughter of Mathonwy ‌— sister to Math — and ​later ones supply the husband the ​tales omit, Beli Mawr. The Mabinogi ​gives her neither husband nor speech: ​the only major figure of the ‌Four Branches who never appears, and ‌the only one whose name all ​the others carry.[4]

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

Dôn's great ​comparison is ‌Irish, and ‌it is ​as old as ​modern Celtic ​studies: Danu ​(or Dana), the ​equally shadowy ‌mother after ​whom the ​Túatha Dé Danann ‌— the ​peoples of ‌the goddess Danu ​— are ‌named. A ​Welsh divine ​kindred called the ​Children of ​Dôn and ​an Irish one ‌called the ​peoples of ‌Danu look ​like two faces ‌of one ​inheritance, and ‌MacKillop and Koch ​both register ‌the comparison ​as standard. ​But it is ‌a comparison, ‌not an ‌equation: no Welsh ​text identifies ​the two ‌names, and ​the philology is ​genuinely disputed ‌— dēwā ​'goddess' beside a ​river-name of ‌the Danube–Don ​type, with ‌the handbooks leaving ​the question ​open.[1]

Within ​the Welsh corpus ​her closest ‌kin are ‌the tradition's ​other mothers and ‌queens: Rhiannon, ​the Great ​Queen of Dyfed, ‌whose name ‌the handbooks ‌reconstruct as ‌Rīgantonā; and, in ​the later ‌genealogical frame, ​her pairing with ​Beli Mawr ​as father ‌of her ​children — asserted ​by the ​genealogies, unmentioned ‌by the Mabinogi.[2] ​Comparative scholarship ​sets her ​beside the ‌Irish sovereignty mothers, ​Macha above ‌all, but ​the parallel is ‌structural rather ​than verbal. ‌Her husbandless, ‌speechless sovereignty has ​no exact ​parallel anywhere ​in Celtic literature: ​she is ‌the matriarch ‌reduced to ​pure name.[3]

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

Dôn rode ​into English ‌on the ‌same wagon ​as the rest ​of the ​Mabinogi: Lady ​Charlotte Guest's ​translation (1838–49) first ‌carried the ​Fourth Branch ​into print, and ‌the antiquarian ​mythography that ‌followed made ​Plant Dôn the ‌standard label ​for the ​northern divine ​kindred — the ​house of ​Gwydion, set ‌beside the ​house of Llŷr, ‌in every ​Victorian account ‌of the ​Welsh gods.[1] Twentieth-century ‌scholarship fixed ​her place ‌with precision: ​Ifor Williams's Pedeir ​Keinc y ‌Mabinogi (1930) ‌established the text, ‌Mac Cana's ​The Mabinogi ​read the ‌Fourth Branch as ​the masterpiece ​of Welsh ‌prose, and ​the great encyclopedias ​— Koch's ‌Celtic Culture ​and MacKillop's ‌Dictionary — gave ​the matriarch ​her standing ​head-words, Danu ​comparison and all.[2]

‌Her widest ‌modern audience ​came through ‌a children's classic: ​Lloyd Alexander's ​Chronicles of ‌Prydain (1964–68) made ‌'the Sons ‌of Don' ‌the ruling ​house of Prydain, ‌with Math ​son of ​Mathonwy and ​Prince Gwydion at ‌Caer Dathyl ​— her ​name on ​the throne of ‌the best-loved ​Welsh-derived fantasy ​ever written.[3] ​Modern Druid and ‌pagan practice ​honours her ‌as the ​Welsh mother-goddess and ‌mother of ​the magical ‌line, and the ‌Welsh sky ​keeps her ​children's names: ​Caer Gwydion, the ​Milky Way, ‌and Caer ‌Arianrhod among ​the stars.[4]

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

Dôn is ​a textual ‌figure: no ‌temple, votive ​deposit, or inscription ​names her, ​and the ​epigraphic record ​of early Wales ‌— the ​Latin and ​ogham memorial ‌stones of roughly ​the fifth ‌to seventh ​centuries — ‌records personal names ​in funerary ​formulae, not ​theonyms.[1] Her ​evidence is manuscript ​evidence. The ‌Fourth Branch ​survives complete ‌in the White ​Book of ‌Rhydderch (Aberystwyth, ​NLW Peniarth ‌MSS 4–5, c. ​1350) and ‌the Red ​Book of ​Hergest (Oxford, Jesus ‌College MS ‌111, c. ‌1375–1420), with ​earlier fragments in ​Peniarth 6; ‌Culhwch ac ​Olwen is ​preserved in the ‌same two ​codices, and ​the Book ‌of Taliesin (NLW ​Peniarth MS ‌2, early ​fourteenth century) ​holds Kat Godeu. ​The tales' ​date of ‌composition is ‌debated, most often ​placed in ‌the later ​eleventh or ​twelfth century.[2]

The ‌geography of ‌her house ‌is real ‌— Gwynedd and ​Arfon in ‌the north-west ​of Wales, ​where Math's court ​and Arianrhod's ‌castle stand ​in the ​tale — but ​no sanctuary ‌of hers ​is known.[3] ​The closest thing ​to a ‌material trace ​of the ‌name is a ​river: Dyfrdwy, ‌the Welsh ​name of ‌the Dee, continues ‌Common Celtic ​*Dēwā, 'the ​goddess', and ​keeps the matriarch's ​name-element flowing ‌through the ‌landscape to ​this day.[4]

Irish Mythological Cycles

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Dôn has no ​place in the Irish ‌cycles — no Irish text ‌names her — and ​yet her standing comparison ​is the most Irish comparison ​in all of Celtic ​studies: Danu, the shadowy mother ​after whom the Túatha ‌Dé Danann, 'the peoples ​of the goddess Danu', take ​their name. The parallel ‌is direct and structural. Irish ​tradition organizes its divine ‌race as the children or ​descendants of a mother ‌who barely appears in ​its texts; Welsh tradition organizes ​the divine house of ​the Mabinogi as Plant Dôn, ​the Children of Dôn, ​after a mother who never ‌appears at all. Mac ​Cana made the structural ‌comparison precise: two pantheons, each ​reckoned as a matronymic ‌kindred, each named for a ​matriarch who is present ‌only as a name ​inside other names — the ‌Irish mother embedded in ​the corporate title Túatha Dé ​Danann, the Welsh in ‌the matronymics uab Dôn and ‌uerch Dôn that introduce ‌her children scene by ​scene.[1]

The etymological debate decides ​how much weight the ‌parallel can bear. The traditional ​derivation runs to Common ​Celtic dēwā, 'goddess' — the ‌word Welsh preserves in ​Dyfrdwy, its name for ​the River Dee — which ‌would make Dôn and ​Danu near-kin in sense as ‌well as in structure; ​the competing reading takes ​Dôn as a river-name of ​the Danube–Don type, which ​would dissolve the kinship into ‌coincidence. The Irish side ‌of the equation is itself ​unsettled, and no Welsh ‌text ever equates the ​two mothers.[2] What the Irish ​record can and cannot ‌witness is also uneven: the ‌Túatha Dé Danann of ‌Cath Maige Tuired are fully ‌dramatized — kings, craftsmen, ​and battle-mothers in a ‌complete mythic cycle — while ​Plant Dôn remain a ​kindred sketched inside someone else's ​tale; the comparison therefore ‌joins a dossier of ​actions to a dossier of ​names.[3] Sims-Williams's study of ​Irish influence on medieval Welsh ‌literature supplies the final ​caution: contact and borrowing between ​the two literatures are ​demonstrable, which means a ‌shared matronymic house could reflect ​an inherited inheritance, a ‌borrowing, or an independent solution ​to the same genealogical ‌problem — and the texts ​do not tell us ‌which.[4]

The identification of ‌Dôn with Danu therefore remains ​probable-but-unproven: registered as the ​standard comparison by every modern ​handbook, never endorsed as ​an equation. This edition ‌follows the handbooks exactly — ‌the parallel stands, the ​equation does not.[2]

Welsh Sources

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Dôn's dossier ​is spread across ‌the whole ‌breadth of the ​medieval Welsh record, ​and everywhere ​it is the ​same dossier: the ​mother in ‌the matronymic. The ​Fourth Branch of ​the Mabinogi, ‌Math fab Mathonwy, ​is her spine ‌— Gwydion ​and Gilfaethwy sons ‌of Dôn at ​Math's court, ​Arianrhod daughter of ​Dôn tested by ​the wand, ​and the whole ‌tragedy of the ​branch played ‌out among her ​children.[1] Culhwch ac ‌Olwen, the ​oldest Arthurian tale, ‌adds the craftsman-sons: ​Amaethon fab ‌Dôn the ploughman ​and Gofannon fab ​Dôn the ‌smith, each named ‌among the anoethau ‌without whom ​Ysbaddaden's tasks cannot ​be done.[2]

The ‌triadic tradition ​gives her house ​its war: among ‌the Three ​Futile Battles of ​the Island of ‌Britain stands ​Cad Goddeu, fought ‌on account of ​a bitch, ​a roebuck, and ​a lapwing that ​Amathaon fab ‌Dôn brought from ‌Annwn — the ​quarrel the ‌Book of Taliesin's ​Kat Godeu fights ​from Gwydion's ‌side, with the ‌trees of the ‌forest enchanted ‌into an army.[3] ​The genealogical tracts ‌supply what ​the tales withhold: ​Dôn daughter of ​Mathonwy, sister ‌to Math, and ​in the later ​genealogies wife ​of Beli Mawr.[4] ‌The tales survive ​complete in ​the White Book ​of Rhydderch (c. ‌1350) and ​the Red Book ‌of Hergest (c. ​1375–1420); Lady ‌Charlotte Guest's translation ​(1838–49) first carried ‌them into ‌English, Ifor Williams's ​edition (1930) fixed ​the standard ​Welsh text, and ​Sioned Davies's translation ‌(2007) is ‌the current scholarly ​English version.[5]

Inscriptions & Seals

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

No ​inscription names ‌Dôn, and ‌none was ​to be ​expected. The ​early inscribed ​stones ​of Wales ‌— the ​Latin and ​ogham memorial ‌stones of ​roughly the ‌fifth ​to seventh ‌centuries catalogued ​by Nash-Williams ​— record ​personal names ​in ​funerary formulae, ‌not theonyms; ​votive epigraphy ‌of the ​Welsh kind ‌simply does ​not ‌survive, and ​the theonymic ‌record of ​pre-Christian Wales ​is known ‌from literature ‌and ‌place-lore rather ​than stone.[1]

​There is ‌no continental ​contrast to ​set against ‌the ​silence, as ​there is ‌for some ​Celtic figures: ‌no Gaulish ​altar ​or Romano-British ​dedication has ​ever been ‌proposed as ‌naming Dôn ​or any ‌secure ​antecedent of ​the name. ‌The nearest ‌epigraphic cousin ‌is a ‌river-name: Dyfrdwy, ​the ‌Welsh Dee, ​which continues ​*Dēwā, 'the ​goddess' — ‌hydronymy, not ​votive stone, ​is ​where the ‌name's element ​survives outside ​the books.[2] ​The matriarch ‌of ​the Mabinogi ‌is a ​creature of ‌the Welsh ​bookhand, and ‌her dossier ‌— ​manuscript, triad, ​genealogy, and ​folklore — ​is complete ‌without a ‌single carved ​letter.

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

Dôn is ​the matriarch ‌who never speaks. ‌Every other ​great figure ​of the Four ​Branches is ​given a scene, ​a speech, ‌a deed ​— Gwydion his ​enchantments, Arianrhod ‌her curses, Math ​his wand ‌— and Dôn ​is given ‌nothing but ​the space before ​her children's ​names. Yet the ​whole architecture ​of the tale ‌leans on ​that silence: ‌the northern house ​of Welsh ‌mythology is not ​the house ‌of Mathonwy ​her father, or ‌of Math ​her brother, but ​the Children ‌of Dôn, the ‌kindred named ‌for the ​mother.[1]

The sources ​are telling ‌us something about ​where authority ​lived in the ‌world behind ​the tales ​— that the ‌line itself ​could be reckoned ‌through the ​woman, and ​the name that ​organized a ​pantheon could be ‌hers alone. ‌Her restoration is ​like her: ‌three plain ​letters, hidden in ​plain sight. ‌Don is an ‌English word, ‌an English river, ‌a Spanish ​title — ‌and underneath all ​of them ​the Welsh mother ​of gods, ‌carrying a ​name the tradition ​glossed as ​'goddess' itself. The ‌project rescues ​diacritics from the ​address bar; ​it cannot ‌rescue a scene ​the manuscripts ‌never wrote. What ​it can ‌do, and does ​here, is ‌keep the ‌mother-name at the ​head of ​the house, spelled ​exactly as ​the White ‌Book and the ‌Red Book ​spell it.
