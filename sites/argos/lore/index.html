The Authentic Name

Unicode restoration and ASCII comparison

Original Script

Ἄργος

The name in its original Greek form. Árgos (Ἄργος) is attested in the source tradition — “Shining, swift”. Its acute accents carry the full phonetic and orthographic weight of the source tradition.

ASCII Constraint

argos

Reduced to plain argos, the name loses everything that made it specific: acute accents. What remains is an ASCII string that machines can parse but that no longer speaks with its original voice.

Unicode Restoration

Árgos

The Unicode restoration recovers what ASCII flattened. Árgos restores acute accents, returning the name to its original written dignity. The domain encodes to Punycode, but the browser displays the truth.

Punycode Encoding
Árgos.com → xn--rgos-4na.com

The non-ASCII characters in Árgos are encoded while the ASCII remains visible. To the DNS, it is Punycode. To humanity, it is Árgos.

Pronunciation

How Árgos was spoken

/ár.gos/ Attic Greek Reconstruction
Á- Alpha with acute pitch [á] — a bright, open attack; the whole name flashes on its first syllable.
-r- Rolled alveolar rho [r] — the quick turn at the name's center.
-gos Voiced velar gamma with short omicron and final sigma [gos] — clipped, unlenited, watchful.

Modern Approximation

'AR-goss' — two short syllables, stress forward, like a light snapping on.

Etymological Kin

  • Greek adjective ἀργός, 'shining, white, gleaming' — and, by the flash of motion, 'swift'
  • Greek noun ἄργυρος, 'silver', from the same root
  • PIE *h₂erǵ-, 'white, shining', whence also Latin argentum
  • Epithet Πανόπτης (Panoptēs), 'the All-Seeing', his fixed title in the myth

The Accent / Script Rule

Árgos is Tier 2 because the Greek Ἄργος carries only the acute stress; both vowels are short, so a single feature separates the restoration from ASCII. The name belongs to the giant Argos Panoptes, Hera's watcher — not to the city of the Argolid, though the two share the shining root.

05

The All-Seeing Watcher

Vigilance, Service, and the Hundred Eyes

Árgos Panoptes is the perfect sentinel of Greek myth: a giant whose body was set with eyes that slept only in relays, so that some were always open. Hera chose him to guard the cow that had been Io. He is the myth's meditation on watchfulness — its power, its cost, and its strange afterlife in feathers.

The Hundred Eyes

Ovid gives him a hundred eyes ringed about his head; the tragedians make him many-eyed, and later art covers his whole body with them. Two slept while the others watched.

Hera's Guard

Chosen by the queen of heaven herself to watch Io — the one servant whose vigilance could be trusted absolutely.

The Sleepless Watch

His eyes took turns at rest; there was no hour, no dusk, no dawn in which the prisoner was unseen.

The Peacock's Tail

Slain by Hermês, he was memorialized by his mistress: Hera set his eyes on the tail of her bird, where they watch still.

Sacred Symbols

The hundred eyes Vigilance that never wholly sleeps
The peacock Hera's bird; the eyes on its tail are his memorial
The white cow Io transformed, the charge he was set to guard
The herdsman's staff His gear as a common cowherd on the roads of the Argolid
The curved sword The harpē of Hermês, by which the watcher was finally put to sleep
06

Mythology

Stories of Árgos

Árgos exists within a single great story — the wanderings of Io — but it is one of the oldest stories the Greeks told, already old when Homer gave Hermês the epithet Argeiphontēs, 'Slayer of Árgos'.

The Appointment

Hera Sets the Watch

Zeús loved Io, daughter of the river-god Inachos, and to hide her from Hera — so the common telling runs — she was changed into a white cow. Hera was not deceived. She asked for the cow as a gift, and set over it Árgos Panoptes, the all-seeing giant, who tethered his prisoner to an olive tree in the grove of Mycenae and watched her without rest.

The Watch

The Herdsman Who Never Sleeps

Árgos kept Io as a cowherd keeps a beast: by day she grazed under his eyes, by night she was bound. The poets dwell on the horror of it — a princess eating grass, a guard with nowhere to look away. Aeschylus makes him the many-eyed herdsman whose vigil not even the earth could bury.

The Slaying

Hermês Argeiphontēs

Zeús sent Hermês to end it. Disguised as a shepherd, the god played the syrinx and told, in Ovid's account, the very story of how the pipes were made — the tale of Syrinx — until one by one the hundred eyes grew heavy and closed. Then Hermês drew the curved sword and struck off the watcher's head. The epithet Argeiphontēs, 'Slayer of Árgos', clung to him forever after.

The Memorial

The Eyes on the Peacock

Hera took the eyes of her dead servant and set them in the tail of her peacock, where they gleam like jewels. But she did not forgive the prisoner: she sent a gadfly to sting Io into madness and drive her over the world — past the Bosporos, the 'cow's crossing' that bears her name still, to Egypt, where Zeús restored her with a touch.

Extended Lore

There is a peculiar pathos in Árgos. He is the one figure in the Io story without desire: Zeús wants, Hera rages, Io suffers, but Árgos only watches. He is duty itself, a creature made of attention. The myth does not call him wicked; Hera's grief for him is real, and his memorial is beautiful. Yet the story knows that perfect vigilance is a kind of death-in-life — a mind with no interior, no sleep, no dream.
