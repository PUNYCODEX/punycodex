From Greek to Unicode: the journey of Troíā

Long before it was a domain, this name traveled through scripts. Troíā begins in Greek, passes through scholarly transliteration, and ends — for now — inside the punycode machinery of the global DNS. Each stage of that journey preserves some information and loses some, and the craft of restoration is knowing exactly which marks matter. This post follows the name stage by stage: the original script, the reconstructed pronunciation, the mythological record, the material evidence, and finally the Unicode form that carries all of it into the address bar. Think of it as a biography of a name, told through its spelling.

Overview

Troía (troia) — The City That Fell Twice — belongs to the Greek tradition, where it is catalogued under the domain "City of Priam". The name means "Unknown; possibly from Τρωός".

Troíā is the city that fell once and rose forever: ten years of siege made it the template of Western war narrative, and its survivors — in Roman myth — sailed west to found the lineage of Rome itself. Every empire since has wanted to be Troy's heir rather than its conqueror.

The Name

The name is attested in Greek as Τροία. Etymologically it means "Unknown; possibly from Τρωός".

The ASCII form troia survives only because the early domain-name system could not carry diacritics; it is a technological compromise, not an ancient spelling. The Unicode restoration Troía recovers the stress accent of the original directly in the address bar. The original carries both stress and vowel length, and exactly one historically valid Unicode restoration exists, which places the name in Tier 1.

The letter-by-letter transformation runs:

  • t → T — Tau
  • r → r — Rho
  • o → o — Short omicron
  • i → í — Acute on iota
  • a → a — Short alpha

The project holds the domain troíā.com (xn--tro-tma4h.com) as the canonical home of this name.

The Original Script

The name is written in Greek as Τροία. This original script is the form against which the ASCII fallback troia and the PuniCodex restoration Troía are measured: the restoration preserves its pitch accent of the written form, so that a reader typing the modern address still speaks the ancient name.

Pronunciation

The reconstructed pronunciation of the name is /troí.aː/ — Attic Greek.

For the modern speaker, the closest approximation is: TROY-ah, with the stress on the final syllable and a long ā.

Kindred and historical forms of the name:

  • Greek — Τροία
  • Latin — Troia
  • Hittite — Wilusa
  • Owned form — Troíā

Greek Τροία bears the stress on the final syllable with a long diphthongal ending — Troíā — and Latin Troia keeps both the vowels and the fame. The form was already a place-name before it was a story: Hittite records call the land Wilusa, which most scholars now connect to (W)ilios, Troy's other Greek name.

Mythology

The Trojan cycle is the largest story the Greeks told: from the wedding of Pēleús and the Apple of Discord to the Returns of the heroes, it fills eight epics, of which the Iliad and Odyssey are only the surviving heart.

The Apple (The Judgment)

Eris, uninvited to the wedding of Pēleús and Thetis, threw a golden apple inscribed "for the fairest." Zeús delegated the verdict to the Trojan prince Paris, herding on Mount Ida: Hēra offered empire, Athēnā victory, Aphrodítē the most beautiful woman in the world. He chose the woman — Helen, wife of Menelaos — and the war became arithmetic.

Nine Years and a Quarrel (The Siege)

A thousand ships gathered at Aulis; nine years raided the Troad before the Iliad even begins. The poem's subject is a quarrel in the tenth year — Agamemnōn's seizure of Brisēis, Achilleús' withdrawal, and the chain of deaths from Pátroklos to Hektōr — yet behind it stands the city's doom, prophesied and accepted by every combatant.

The Last Night (The Horse)

The Odyssey and the Aeneid tell the end the Iliad never reaches: the Achaeans feign departure, leaving a wooden horse as an "offering to Athēnā." The priest Laokoōn warns against it and is destroyed by sea-serpents; the prophetess Kassandra is disbelieved, as always. At night the hidden warriors open the Scaean Gate, and Troíā burns in a single night after ten years of failure.

The Dispersal (The Survivors)

Greek myth disperses the Trojans across the map: Aeneas to Italy in the Roman telling, Antēnor to the Adriatic, Hektor's son — in the minority tradition — restored in a new Troy. The vanquished city thus colonizes the imagination of its conquerors: to be Troianos, "of Troy," becomes, paradoxically, a title of glory.

Archaeology & Evidence

Hisarlık in northwest Anatolia preserves nine major settlement layers (Troy I–IX), ca. 3000 BCE–500 CE. The candidates for Homer's city are Troy VI (destroyed ca. 1250 BCE, by earthquake or war) and Troy VIIa (burned ca. 1180 BCE, with sling stones and arrowheads in the destruction debris). UNESCO listed the site in 1998. The "Treasure of Priam" Schliemann smuggled out is now split between Moscow and Istanbul, and the Hittite Tawagalawa letter mentioning a king of Ahhiyawa (Achaeans?) at Millawanda places Greek-Anatolian conflict in the right sea at the right century.

Across Cultures

Rome adopted Troy as mother: the Aeneid makes Aeneas' refugees the seed of the Roman people, and Ilium Novum — New Troy — received Roman privileges as a symbolic ancestor. The Middle Ages multiplied the claim: the Franks, the Britons (through Brutus of Troy in Geoffrey of Monmouth), and half the dynasties of Europe forged Trojan genealogies. "Trojan" became a compliment — faithful, brave — even in the languages of the Greeks' heirs.

Within the Greek tradition, closely related names in the corpus include Aígyptos, Asía, Athēnai, Atlantís, Dēlos, and Delphoí.

Cultural Legacy

"Trojan horse" is now the universal term for a payload hidden inside a gift — the computing term is simply the myth recompiled. The question "was Troy real?" launched modern archaeology: Schliemann's excavations at Hisarlık (1870s–90s) proved a fortified Bronze Age citadel exactly where Homer put it. The Hittite archives' Wilusa and Taruisa, matched to (W)ilios and Troia by a century of scholarship, made Troy the test case for how myth and document corroborate each other.

A Meditation

Troíā is the city that teaches what walls cannot do. Gods built hers; a story breached them. Every culture that has traced its ancestry to her fall has understood the lesson: the defeated city outlived all its conquerors because it alone became a story that everyone, including its enemies, needed to tell.

Her deepest lesson is the one the victors learned first: that a story is stronger than a wall. The Greeks won the war and spent three thousand years inside the city's aftermath — and Rome, wiser than all of them, simply claimed to be her. Every culture since has faced the same choice at her gate: to besiege the past, or to inherit it.

The Hittite letter and the burned layer do not prove the Iliad — nothing could — but they do the more valuable thing: they give the song an address. Every retelling since has been a kind of excavation, brushing the myth to find the memory underneath, and every excavator has found what Schliemann found: that a story told long enough and loved enough becomes true in the only way that finally matters — people build on it.

Every autumn the plain floods a little, as the Scamander remembers its old bed, and the mound stands in the water-meadow like the ship of the story it carries — nine cities deep, one name, and still unsinkable.

And the plain is quiet now, the way famous battlefields are — which is its own testimony: the war ended, the song did not.

A Closer Look at the Marks

A restored name is a small map. In Troíā, the journey from the ASCII fallback troia to the Unicode form can be read letter by letter, and each letter tells a story. Of the 5 characters, 3 remain unchanged; they anchor the name to its modern shape. The remaining adjustments recover marks the source tradition used to distinguish this name from every other word built from the same letters.

The t stays as it is — Tau.

The r stays as it is — Rho.

The o stays as it is — Short omicron.

The i becomes í — Acute on iota.

The a becomes ā — Long final alpha.

Together these changes are not decoration. They are the minimum set of marks needed to keep the name legible in its own tradition. Remove them and you still have a string of letters; you no longer have the name as it was written.
