01

Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Pryderi (pryderi) ​— Son of ‌Pwyll, King · ‌Care, anxiety — ​is the ​one figure who ​appears in all ​Four Branches of ​the Mabinogi: the ‌son of Pwyll ​Pen Annwn and ​Rhiannon, stolen ‌on the night ​of his birth, ‌found outside Teyrnon ​Twrf Liant's stable ‌wrapped in silk ​brocade, raised as ​Gwri Wallt ​Euryn, and named ​at his homecoming ​from the word ‌of relief his ​mother spoke — ‌her pryder, her ​care, lifted at ‌last.[1]

His ​life is the ‌thread the cycle ​is strung on. ‌In the First ​Branch, Pwyll Pendefig ​Dyfed, he is ‌born, stolen, ‌fostered, and restored; ‌in the Second, ​Branwen ferch Llŷr, ​he is named ‌first among the ​seven survivors of ​the Irish ‌war who bear ​Brân's head home; ​in the Third, ‌Manawydan fab Llŷr, ​he rules Dyfed, ‌is emptied out ​of it by ​enchantment, and ​is caught fast ​to the golden ‌bowl until Manawydan's ‌patient bargaining frees ​him; in the ‌Fourth, Math fab ​Mathonwy, Gwydion's ​illusory wealth cheats ‌him of the ‌swine of Annwn, ‌and he dies ‌in single combat ​at Y Felenrhyd, ‌buried at ​Maen Tywynog above ​it.[1]

PuniCodex restores ​the name as ‌Pryderi — identical ​to the ASCII ​input, because Middle ​Welsh writes the ‌name in ​plain Latin letters ​with no diacritic ​for the restoration ‌to recover; the ​name is Tier ‌2 by the ​mechanical rule. ‌The plain-ASCII pryderi.com ​is held by ‌a third party ‌outside the PuniCodex ​collection and is ​watched for release; ​no form ​of the name ‌is currently owned, ‌and this temple ​claims none.[3]

02

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

The name ​is attested in Middle ‌Welsh as Pryderi, Pryderi ‌uab Pwyll, in ​the Four Branches of ​the Mabinogi. The First ​Branch supplies its ​own etymology, and it ​is one of the ‌most explicit aetiological ​namings in medieval literature: ​when Teyrnon brings ‌the lost boy home, ​Rhiannon says that if ‌the news is ​true her pryder — ‌her care, her anxiety ​— is lifted ​from her, and Pwyll ​declares that the child ​should take his ​name from the word ‌his mother spoke when ​the news was ‌brought to her.[1] The ​Welsh common noun pryder, ‌'care, anxiety, worry', ​is a living word, ‌and the lexicon's ​gloss 'Care, anxiety' follows ‌the tale's own derivation.[2]

​Older scholarship also ​noted the name's resemblance ‌to Prydain, 'Britain', and ‌to the Pritan- ‌names of the island's ​peoples, but no derivation ​beyond the tale's ‌own commands consensus, and ​honest scholarship says so.[3] ​His foster-name is ‌part of the record: ​Teyrnon's household called the ​golden-haired foundling Gwri ‌Wallt Euryn, 'Gwri of ​the Golden Hair', ‌and the triads remember ​him as the swineherd ​of his foster-father ​Pendaran Dyfed.

The ASCII ​form is not a ‌compromise here: Middle ‌Welsh writes the name ​in plain Latin letters, ‌and the normalized ​spelling is already Pryderi. ​There is no diacritic ‌and no distinctive ‌letter for the restoration ‌to recover; only the ‌capital distinguishes the ​restored form from the ‌input, which places ​the name in Tier ​2 by the mechanical ​rule.

The letter-by-letter ‌transformation runs:

  • p ​→ P — Same, ​capitalized
  • r ​→ r — Same

‌- y → y ​— Same

  • ​d → d — ​Same
  • e → ‌e — Same

​- r → r ‌— Same

  • i ​→ i — ‌Same

The project holds ​no domain for ‌this name: pryderi.com is ‌registered to a third ​party outside the ​PuniCodex collection and is ​watched for release. The ​temple is served ‌at /pryderi/ and claims ‌no domain it does ​not hold.

Sources

  1. The Mabinogi, First Branch (Pwyll Pendefig Dyfed), trans. Sioned Davies (Oxford University Press, 2007) — the naming scene at Arberth.
  2. Geiriadur Prifysgol Cymru / A Dictionary of the Welsh Language (Cardiff: University of Wales Press), s.vv. Pryderi, pryder.
  3. Koch, John T., ed., Celtic Culture: A Historical Encyclopedia (ABC-CLIO, 2006), s.v. Pryderi; MacKillop, James, Dictionary of Celtic Mythology (Oxford, 1998), s.v. Pryderi.
03

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The pronunciation ​of the name ‌in Modern ‌Welsh is /prəˈdɛr.i/ ​— Modern Welsh; ​the Middle Welsh ​value is held ​to be ​substantially the same.[1]

‌Phoneme by phoneme:

​- Pr- — ​Voiceless bilabial stop ‌[p] released ​directly into a ‌trilled [r]: a ​clean initial cluster, ‌as in ​English 'prince'

  • ​-y- — The ​Welsh obscure vowel ​[ə], the 'clear ​y' of ‌non-final syllables: a ​soft central 'uh', ‌never the close ​[i] the ‌letter has in ​final position
  • ‌-der- — Voiced ​[d], open-mid [ɛ], ‌tapped [r], ​under stress: Welsh ​words are stressed ‌on the penultimate ‌syllable, so the ‌weight of ​the name lands ​here
  • -i ‌— Short close ​[i], the ​unstressed final vowel

‌For the modern ​speaker, the closest ​approximation is: 'pruh-DEH-ree' ‌— the ​y a soft ‌'uh', the stress ​on 'der', the ​Welsh penult.

Kindred ​and historical ​forms of the ‌name:

  • Middle ‌Welsh — Pryderi ​uab Pwyll, ‌son of Pwyll ​Pen Annwn and ​Rhiannon
  • Foster-name ‌— Gwri Wallt ‌Euryn, 'Gwri ‌of the Golden ‌Hair'
  • Welsh ​common noun — ‌pryder, 'care, anxiety, ​worry', the ​word the First ​Branch names him ‌from
  • Comparative ​reflex — ​Mabon ap Modron ​and Gaulish Maponos, ‌the divine-son figure-type ​he is read ​against; a ​parallel, not a ‌cognate

Pryderi is ​Tier 2 by ‌the mechanical ​rule: the Middle ‌Welsh form is ​already plain ASCII, ‌and the restoration ‌preserves nothing ​the ASCII form ​loses. The name's ​information-richness is narrative, ​not orthographic: it ‌carries the ‌story of its ​own naming.

Sources

  1. Morris-Jones, John, A Welsh Grammar, Historical and Comparative (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1913), phonology and orthography.
  2. Geiriadur Prifysgol Cymru / A Dictionary of the Welsh Language (University of Wales Press), s.v. Pryderi.

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

The name ​is preserved ‌in Middle ‌Welsh manuscripts ​in the Latin ​alphabet as ​Pryderi — ​Middle Welsh (Brythonic ​Celtic), attested ‌in the ​White Book ​of Rhydderch (c. ‌1350) and ​the Red ‌Book of Hergest ​(c. 1382–1410), ‌recording material ​composed centuries ​earlier.[1] The script ​is written ​left-to-right in ​the medieval Welsh ‌bookhand.

The ​scholarly rendering ‌proceeds step ​by step:

  • ‌Middle Welsh ​writes the ‌name in plain ​Latin letters; ‌no diacritic ​attaches to ​the name in ‌any witness, ‌and normalized ‌Middle Welsh orthography ​and the ​ASCII spelling ‌are identical.

​- The letter ​y represents ‌two Welsh ​values — the ​obscure [ə] ‌of non-final ​syllables, as ‌here in Pry-, ​and the ​close vowel ​of final syllables ​— but ‌the orthography ‌writes a ​single plain letter; ‌nothing distinctive ​is lost ​in ASCII.

  • ‌The derivation ‌from pryder, ‌'care, anxiety', ‌is the First ​Branch's own ‌aetiological naming, ​not a modern ​conjecture: Rhiannon's ​word of ‌relief is ​the name's documented ​source.[2]
  • ​The Unicode ‌restoration Pryderi therefore ​changes nothing ​but the ​capital; the ‌name stands in ​Tier 2 ‌by the ​mechanical rule, and ‌the plain-ASCII ​domain pryderi.com ‌— held ‌by a third ​party outside ​the PuniCodex ​collection and watched ​for release ‌— is ‌the philologically ​complete form.

Sources

  1. Williams, Ifor, ed., Pedeir Keinc y Mabinogi (University of Wales Press, 1930); Davies, trans., The Mabinogion (Oxford University Press, 2007).
  2. Geiriadur Prifysgol Cymru / A Dictionary of the Welsh Language (University of Wales Press), s.vv. Pryderi, pryder; Morris-Jones, John, A Welsh Grammar, Historical and Comparative (Clarendon Press, 1913).
05

Domains & Attributes

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sphere of influence, titles, epithets, domain cards.

Pryderi is ​catalogued under the ‌domain 'Son of ‌Pwyll, King' — ​a filial ​domain and a ​royal one, and ​the Four Branches ​test both halves ‌of it.[1]

​The Son

His ​story begins as ‌a theft: the ​heir of ‌Dyfed taken on ​his birth-night, found ‌at a stable ​door, and restored ​to his mother ​by a foster-father's ​honesty. The bond ​with Rhiannon is ‌the axis ​of the First ‌Branch — her ​penance lasts exactly ‌as long as ​his absence, and ‌his name is ​coined from its ‌ending.[1]

The ​King

He ​rules Dyfed after ‌Pwyll, gives his ‌mother to Manawydan ‌in marriage, and ​holds a kingdom ​that enchantment can ‌empty in a ​night. The Third ​Branch measures ‌his kingship by ​what survives it: ​not force — ‌his own grasping ​at the golden ‌bowl is the ​blunder the tale ​records — ​but the patience ​and good faith ‌of the company ‌around him.[1]

​The Stricken Lord

‌Twice the Otherworld ​takes from him ​what force cannot ‌recover: the ‌emptied land and ‌his own captivity ‌in the Third ​Branch, and in ‌the Fourth the ​swine of Annwn ​— a gift ​from Arawn he ‌was bound ​not to sell ​— won from ​him by Gwydion's ‌conjured gold. His ​demand for single ​combat, 'for it ​was because of ‌me that the ​hosts came', ‌is the Mabinogi's ​plainest statement of ‌a lord answering ​for his quarrel.[1]

‌### Care Itself

‌The name is ​the domain: pryder, ​the care a ​kingdom and ​a mother both ‌exact, lifted only ‌by the truth ​walking home.

Sources

  1. The Mabinogi, First, Third, and Fourth Branches, trans. Sioned Davies (Oxford University Press, 2007).

Symbols

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Iconography, attributes, and their meanings.

The iconography ​of Pryderi ‌is narrative rather ‌than cultic ​— medieval Welsh ​prose preserves ​no temple regalia ​— but ​the Four Branches ‌fixed a ​small set of ​objects around ‌him, each one ​a turn ‌of his story:[1]

​- The ‌silk brocade wrapping ​— What ​the foundling at ​Teyrnon's stable ​door was swaddled ​in: royal ‌blood declaring ​itself where no ‌witness could
​- The clawed ‌hand — ​The great claw ‌reaching through ​the stable window ‌for the ​newborn foal on ​May-eve: the ‌unseen power that ‌took the ‌child as it ​took the ​foals

  • The ‌golden bowl ​on its chains ​— Hanging ‌above the marble ​fountain in ​the fortress that ‌was never ​there: grasp it, ‌and hands ​stick, feet root, ​and speech ​dies — the ​bait at ‌the heart of ‌the enchantment

​- The gate-hammer ‌about his ​neck — What ​he wore ‌when the spell ‌released him, ‌as Rhiannon wore ‌the collars ​of asses: the ‌humiliation of ​the Third Branch, ​borne home ​in silence

  • ‌The swine ​of Annwn ​— The hobeu, ​first pigs ‌in Wales, Arawn's ​gift with ​a condition upon ​them: the ‌treasure a poet's ​illusion could ‌buy, and the ​war it ‌purchased
  • The ​grave at ‌Maen Tywynog — ‌Above Y ​Felenrhyd, where the ​single combat ​was fought: the ​one fixed ‌point of a ‌life lived ​under enchantment

Sources

  1. The Mabinogi, First, Third, and Fourth Branches, trans. Davies (2007).
07

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

Pryderi is the only ​character who appears in all Four Branches ‌of the Mabinogi, and his life is ‌the cycle's spine.[2]

The May-Eve ​Vanishing (First Branch, Pwyll Pendefig Dyfed)

Born ​to Pwyll and Rhiannon after the nobles ​of Dyfed pressed for an heir, he ​is taken on the night of his ​birth while the six women set to ‌watch all sleep; their false witness ​— hound's blood and bones — sends ​Rhiannon to her penance at the horse-block. ‌The child falls into the strangest rescue ​in Welsh prose: Teyrnon Twrf Liant, a ‌lord of Gwent Is Coed, loses a ​foal every May-eve; keeping watch, he strikes ‌off the great clawed hand that ​reaches through the stable window for the ​newborn foal, and outside the door finds ​a baby wrapped in silk brocade. He ​and his wife rear the boy as ​Gwri Wallt Euryn, Gwri of the Golden ‌Hair.[1]

The Naming at Arberth ​(First Branch)

Gwri grows with uncanny speed ‌and bears, year by year, the very ​likeness of Pwyll. When Teyrnon hears of ‌Rhiannon's penance, he and his wife bring ​the boy to Arberth; Rhiannon says that ‌if this is true her pryder ​is lifted from her, and Pwyll names ‌the child from his mother's word. The ​penance ends; Teyrnon takes no reward; and ​Pryderi is fostered with Pendaran Dyfed until ‌he comes of age and Pwyll gives ‌him the realm.[1]

One of the ‌Seven (Second Branch, Branwen ferch Llŷr)

​The Irish war fought for Branwen's sake ​destroys both hosts, and of the men ‌of the Island of the Mighty only ​seven come home bearing Brân's head: the ​tale names Pryderi first, with Manawydan, Glifieu, ‌Taliesin, Ynawg, Gruddieu, and Heilyn. With ​them he feasts seven years at Harlech, ​where the Birds of Rhiannon sing, and ‌eighty years at Gwales in the Assembly ​of the Wondrous Head.[1]

The Enchantment ‌of Dyfed (Third Branch, Manawydan fab Llŷr)

​Ruling Dyfed after Pwyll's death, Pryderi ​gives his mother to Manawydan and comes ​home to Arberth with Rhiannon, Manawydan, and ​his wife Cigfa. Thunder and mist fall, ‌and Dyfed is emptied of every person, ‌dwelling, and beast. After two years of ​hunting and three trades driven out ‌of the towns of England, a shining ​white boar leads Pryderi and Manawydan to ​a fortress that was never there; against ‌counsel Pryderi enters, grasps a golden bowl ‌hanging by chains above a marble fountain, ‌and is rooted and struck dumb; Rhiannon, ‌going in after him, is caught ​the same way, and fort and prisoners ‌vanish. Manawydan undoes it by patience — ​the wheat, the mice, the condemned mouse ​the bishop begs off — and names ​his price: the captives freed, the enchantment ‌lifted, and no vengeance taken. The ​spell was Llwyd fab Cilcoed's vengeance for ​Gwawl fab Clud, the suitor Rhiannon refused; ​Pryderi comes home with a gate-hammer about ‌his neck.[1]

The Swine and the ​Fatal Combat (Fourth Branch, Math fab Mathonwy)

​Among his treasures are the hobeu, ​the first swine in Wales — Arawn ‌of Annwn's gift, which he had sworn ​neither to sell nor to give until ‌the herd had doubled. Gwydion fab Dôn ​comes as a poet, is refused, and ‌buys the herd with conjured stallions, greyhounds, ​and golden shields — apparitions gone ‌by morning. Pryderi comes north with an ‌army; at the peace-talks he demands single ​combat, for it was because of him ​the hosts had come; and at Y ​Felenrhyd Gwydion slays him 'by strength and ​valour, and by magic and enchantment'. ‌They buried him at Maen Tywynog above ‌Y Felenrhyd, and his grave, the Fourth ​Branch says, is there.[1]

Sources

  1. The Mabinogi, all Four Branches, trans. Sioned Davies (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2007); Welsh text ed. Ifor Williams, Pedeir Keinc y Mabinogi (Cardiff: University of Wales Press, 1930).
  2. Mac Cana, Proinsias, The Mabinogi, 2nd ed. (Cardiff: University of Wales Press, 1992); Gruffydd, W. J., Rhiannon: An Inquiry into the Origins of the First and Third Branches of the Mabinogi (Cardiff: University of Wales Press, 1953).

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

Pryderi's great ​comparative frame ‌is the ‌Celtic divine son. ​The handbooks ​set him ​beside Mabon ap ​Modron — ​'Son, son of ‌Mother' — ​the youth ​of Culhwch ac ‌Olwen taken ​from his ‌mother when he ​was three ‌nights old ​and sought until ​no living ​creature remembers his ​loss, and ​behind Mabon ‌the Gaulish Maponos, ​the 'Divine ‌Son' equated ​with Apollo in ‌Romano-Celtic dedications. ​A child ‌stolen at birth ​and restored, ‌whose name carries ​his story: ​Mac Cana ‌and Koch both ‌register the ‌figure-type, while ​insisting the names ​are not ‌cognate and ​no identity is ​claimed.[1]

The ‌second frame is ​the stricken ​sovereignty Pryderi ‌shares with the ​Otherworld-touched kings ‌of the ​insular tradition: a ​ruler whose ​land a ​mist can empty, ‌rescued not ‌by his own ​strength — ‌his grasp ​at the golden ​bowl is ‌the Third ‌Branch's recorded blunder ‌— but ‌by Manawydan's ​patience. Gruffydd built ‌his reading ​of the First ​and Third ​Branches around ‌this Pryderi complex, ​and later ​scholarship has ​kept the debate ‌alive.[2]

Within ​the corpus, ​closely related figures ​include Rhiannon ‌(his mother, whose ​lifted care ‌names him), ​Manawydan (his stepfather ‌and rescuer), ​Arawn (the ‌king of Annwn ‌whose gift ​of swine ​shapes his end), ​and Gwydion ​(the enchanter who ‌cheats him ‌and finally ​slays him).

Sources

  1. Mac Cana, Proinsias, The Mabinogi, 2nd ed. (University of Wales Press, 1992); Koch, John T., ed., Celtic Culture: A Historical Encyclopedia (ABC-CLIO, 2006), s.vv. Pryderi, Mabon, Maponos.
  2. Gruffydd, W. J., Rhiannon: An Inquiry into the Origins of the First and Third Branches of the Mabinogi (University of Wales Press, 1953).

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

Lady Charlotte ​Guest's translation ‌of the ‌Mabinogion (1838–49) ​carried Pryderi ​out of ​the Welsh ​manuscripts and ​into English, ‌and the ​Victorian Celtic ​revival made ‌the vanished ​child of ‌the First ​Branch one ‌of its ​set-pieces.[1] Evangeline ​Walton's twentieth-century ​retellings of ​the Four ​Branches, completed ‌in the ​Mabinogion tetralogy, ‌kept his ​theft, naming, ‌and death ​before modern ‌readers.[2]

Lloyd ​Alexander's Chronicles of ‌Prydain (1964–68) ​built a ​five-novel world ‌openly on ‌the Mabinogi's ‌matter — ​though Pryderi ​himself never ‌appears under ​his own ​name — ‌and carried ​the cycle's ​landscape to ‌a vast ​young readership.[3] ‌In scholarship, ​his centrality ​is axiomatic: ​Ifor Williams's ​edition fixed ‌the Welsh ‌text, Gruffydd's ​Rhiannon (1953) ‌built its ​argument around ​the Pryderi ‌complex, and ‌Mac Cana's ‌study made the ‌boy who ​spans all ‌four branches ​the structural ​key to ​the cycle. ‌In modern ​Druid and ​pagan practice ​he is ‌honoured as ​the lost-and-found ​divine son; ​and the ‌name stands ​in the ‌handbooks as ​the standing ‌example of ​the Mabinogi's ‌own method ‌— a ​person named, ​deliberately and ​explicitly, out ​of a ‌single spoken ‌word of ​grief relieved.[4]

Sources

  1. The Mabinogion, trans. Lady Charlotte Guest, 3 vols. (London, 1838–49).
  2. Walton, Evangeline, the Mabinogion tetralogy (Ballantine, 1970–74).

  4. Mac Cana, Proinsias, The Mabinogi, 2nd ed. (University of Wales Press, 1992); Gruffydd, W. J., Rhiannon (University of Wales Press, 1953).

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

No cult ​site, votive ‌deposit, or ‌inscription of Pryderi ​is known; ​his attestation ​is entirely literary, ​and the ​early inscribed ‌stones of Wales ​— Latin ​and ogham ‌memorial stones ​of roughly the ‌fifth to ​seventh centuries ‌— record personal ​names in ​funerary formulae, ​not the heroes ​of story.[1] ​His evidence ‌is manuscript evidence. ​The Four ‌Branches survive ​complete in the ‌White Book ​of Rhydderch ‌(Aberystwyth, National Library ​of Wales, ‌Peniarth MSS ​4–5, c. 1350) ​and the ‌Red Book ‌of Hergest ‌(Oxford, Jesus College ​MS 111, ​c. 1382–1410), ‌with earlier fragments ​in Peniarth ​6; the ‌tales' date of ​composition is ​argued rather ‌than documented — ​Ifor Williams ‌placed them ​in the later ​eleventh century, ​and current ​scholarship generally holds ‌a range ‌around 1060–1120.[2]

​The geography of ‌his story ​is real: ​Arberth, the ‌chief court of ‌Dyfed, stands ‌by modern ‌Narberth in Pembrokeshire; ​Y Felenrhyd, ‌where the ​fatal combat was ​fought, lies ​toward the ‌border country of ​Gwynedd and ​Dyfed; and ​the Fourth Branch ‌points to ​his grave ​at Maen Tywynog ​above it.[2] ‌The nearest ​epigraphic horizon is ‌comparative, not ​his: the ‌Gaulish Maponos ​of the Chamalières ‌tablet and ‌the northern ​British dedications to ​Apollo Maponus ​— the ​divine son whose ‌figure-type the ‌handbooks read ​him against.[3]

  3. Koch, John T., ed., Celtic Culture: A Historical Encyclopedia (ABC-CLIO, 2006), s.vv. Maponos, Chamalières.

Irish Mythological Cycles

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Pryderi belongs ​to Welsh ‌rather than Irish ‌literature, but ​the Irish cycles ​supply the ​closest mythic economy ​to his, ​and the comparison ‌has been ​a fixture of ​Mabinogi scholarship for ‌over a ​century. The nearest ‌analogue to ​his birth-tale is ‌Irish fosterage-and-concealment: ​in Tochmarc Étaíne, ​the infant ​Óengus — son ​of the ​Dagda and Bóand ‌— is ​carried off the ‌day he ​is born and ‌fostered in ​secret with Mídir ‌of Brí ​Léith, hidden from ‌Elcmar, and ​grows up not ​knowing his ‌own parentage until ‌a taunt reveals ‌it. A ​divine child removed ​at birth, ‌reared by another ​house, and ​restored to his ‌name: the ​shape is Pryderi's ​own, and ‌the handbooks set ​the Welsh ‌and Irish birth-tales ​side by ​side as reflexes ​of one ​insular story-pattern.[1]

The ‌second Irish ‌bridge is the ​stricken king. ‌Irish literature knows ​the ruler whose ​prosperity the ‌Otherworld can cancel ‌in a ‌night — the ‌hollowed-out kingship ​of the síd ‌tales, where ​a realm's substance ​is hostage ​to a bargain ‌or a ​spell. Pryderi's emptied ​Dyfed runs ​on that economy, ‌and his ​rescue by Manawydan's ​patience rather ​than by arms ‌is the ​Third Branch's answer ‌to it.[2] ​Sims-Williams's survey of ‌Irish influence on ​Welsh literature ‌registers the comparative ‌frame while ​warning against over-reading ​borrowing into ​parallel inheritance — ​the safest ‌summary of where ‌the Pryderi ​material stands.[3]

Sources

  1. Bergin, Osborn, and R. I. Best, ed. and trans., 'Tochmarc Étaíne', Ériu 12 (1938), 137–196; Mac Cana, Proinsias, Celtic Mythology (London: Hamlyn, 1970), on the divine-child pattern.
  2. Mac Cana, Proinsias, The Mabinogi, 2nd ed. (Cardiff: University of Wales Press, 1992).
  3. Sims-Williams, Patrick, Irish Influence on Medieval Welsh Literature (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2011).

Welsh Sources

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Pryderi's dossier ​is the ‌Four Branches ‌of the ​Mabinogi — ​Pedeir Keinc y ​Mabinogi — ​and he ​is the ‌only figure ​who appears in ​all four: ‌born and ​named in ‌the First, ​Pwyll Pendefig ‌Dyfed; first-named of ​the seven ​survivors in ​the Second, ​Branwen ferch ​Llŷr; lord and ‌captive of ​the Third, ‌Manawydan fab ​Llŷr; and ‌slain in the ​Fourth, Math ‌fab Mathonwy.[1] ​The triads ‌add one ​more witness: the ​Trioedd Ynys ‌Prydein count ‌him among ‌the Three ​Powerful Swineherds of ​the Island ‌of Britain, ​tending the ​swine of ‌Pendaran Dyfed his ​foster-father — ​the same ‌herd the ​Fourth Branch ‌kills him ​over.[2]

The tales ​survive complete ​in two ​manuscripts: the ‌White Book ‌of Rhydderch (c. ​1350) and ‌the Red ​Book of ​Hergest (c. ‌1382–1410), with earlier ‌fragments in ‌Peniarth 6. ‌Their date ​of composition ‌is argued rather ​than documented ​— Ifor ​Williams placed ‌them in ​the later eleventh ​century, and ​current scholarship ‌generally holds ​a range ​around 1060–1120 — ​and their ‌author is ​unknown.[3] Lady ‌Charlotte Guest's ​translation (1838–49) ‌first carried them ​into English; ‌Ifor Williams's ‌edition (1930) ​fixed the ​standard Welsh text; ​Sioned Davies's ​translation (2007) ‌is the ‌current scholarly ​English version.[4]

Sources

  1. The Mabinogi, trans. Sioned Davies (Oxford University Press, 2007), all Four Branches.
  2. Bromwich, Rachel, ed. and trans., Trioedd Ynys Prydein: The Triads of the Island of Britain, 4th ed. (Cardiff: University of Wales Press, 2014), the Three Powerful Swineherds.
  3. Williams, Ifor, ed., Pedeir Keinc y Mabinogi (University of Wales Press, 1930); Davies, The Mabinogion (2007), introduction, on date and manuscripts.
  4. The Mabinogion, trans. Lady Charlotte Guest, 3 vols. (London, 1838–49).

Inscriptions & Seals

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

No ​inscription names ‌Pryderi. The ‌early inscribed ​stones of ​Wales — ​the Latin ​and ogham ​memorial ‌stones of ​roughly the ​fifth to ‌seventh centuries ​— record ‌personal names ​in funerary ‌formulae, ​not the ​heroes of ​story; votive ​epigraphy of ​the Welsh ‌kind simply ​does not ‌survive, ​and the ‌pre-Christian name-record ​of Wales ‌is known ​from literature ‌and place-lore ​rather than ​stone.[1]

‌The epigraphic ‌contrast that ‌matters is ​comparative and ​close to ‌home: Maponos, ​the Gaulish ​'Divine ‌Son' whose ​figure-type the ​handbooks set ‌beside Pryderi, ​is genuinely ‌attested — ​invoked on ​the ​lead tablet ​of Chamalières ‌from the ‌Auvergne and ​honoured in ‌dedications to ​Apollo Maponus ​from ‌the north ‌of Roman ‌Britain. Precisely ‌the epigraphic ​abundance that ‌Pryderi's dossier ​lacks belongs ​to ​his comparandum; ‌scholars read ​the Welsh ​boy against ​that record, ‌but they ​do not, ​and ​cannot, cite ‌an inscription ​for him.[2] ‌The honesty ​of the ‌record is ​the point: ‌'Care, ‌anxiety' is ​the tale's ​own derivation ​of the ​name, not ‌a title ‌recovered from ​stone.

Sources

  1. Jackson, Kenneth H., Language and History in Early Britain (Edinburgh University Press, 1953), on the early inscriptional record.
  2. Koch, John T., ed., Celtic Culture: A Historical Encyclopedia (ABC-CLIO, 2006), s.vv. Maponos, Chamalières.

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

Pryderi is ​named out ‌of relief. ‌His mother ​has carried ​a false ​charge for ​years — sitting ​by the ‌horse-block, offering ​her back ​to strangers ‌— and ​when the ‌lost boy ​is set before ‌her she ​does not ​say vengeance, ​or justice, ​or even ​joy: she ‌says her care ​is lifted, ‌and the ​court builds ‌the child's ​name out ‌of that ​word. Anxiety, made ‌into a ​name and ​then, astonishingly, ‌into a ‌king.[1]

The ‌rest of ​his story ​keeps faith with ‌the naming. ​His is ​the kingdom ‌a mist ​can empty ​between one ‌hour and the ​next; his ‌is the ​hand that ​sticks to ​the golden ​bowl; his ‌is the herd ‌of otherworld ​pigs a ‌poet's illusion ​can buy; ​his is ‌the grave ‌above Y ‌Felenrhyd. Nothing in ‌the Four ​Branches is ‌safe, and ​Pryderi is ​the figure ​through whom ‌the Mabinogi says ​so — ​the lord ​who loses ‌everything except ​the thread ​of the ​tale, and who ‌walks through ​all four ‌branches carrying ​it. Care, ‌the Welsh ​word means; ‌and also ‌what care is ​for: the ​watch kept, ​the debt ​counted, the ‌mother who ‌waited at ​the gate.

Sources

  1. The Mabinogi, First Branch, trans. Davies (2007).
