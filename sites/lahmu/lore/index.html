The Authentic Name

Unicode restoration and ASCII comparison

Original Script

𒀭𒌓𒈬

The name in its original Mesopotamian form. Lahmu (𒀭𒌓𒈬) is attested in the source tradition — “The hairy one”. Its original diacritics and script distinctions carry the full phonetic and orthographic weight of the source tradition.

ASCII Constraint

lahmu

The plain lahmu form is identical to the Unicode restoration. Because this name is already written in Latin letters, no diacritics, stress, or script information were lost — only capitalization differs.

Unicode Restoration

Lahmu

The Unicode restoration does not need to recover lost marks for Lahmu. Its value is canonical spelling and consistent cataloguing, not the reconstruction of erased orthography. The domain is readable as-is to both DNS and humanity.

Punycode Encoding
Lahmu.com → lahmu.com

The non-ASCII characters in Lahmu are encoded while the ASCII remains visible. To the DNS, it is Punycode. To humanity, it is Lahmu.

Pronunciation

How Lahmu was spoken

/ˈlax.mu/ Akkadian Reconstruction
La- Open syllable with short [a], from the Akkadian root lahāmu, 'to be hairy'.
-ḫ- Voiceless velar fricative [x], written with the cuneiform ḫu sign — the consonant that separates Lahmu from a simple English h.
-mu Bilabial nasal [m] plus close back vowel [u], the masculine nominative ending.

Modern Approximation

'LAKH-moo' — the middle consonant is a dry, breathy 'kh' like the ch in Scottish 'loch', not a soft English h.

Etymological Kin

  • Akkadian 𒀭𒆷𒄷𒈬 (dlaḫmu), 'the hairy one' — protective deity and primordial figure
  • Akkadian feminine Lahamu (dlaḫmu + feminine -atu), his paired counterpart at the threshold
  • Akkadian verb lahāmu, 'to be hairy, to be covered with hair'

The Accent / Script Rule

Lahmu is Tier 2 because the registrable canonical form is plain ASCII: it preserves no distinctive diacritic or non-Latin letter. Scholarly Assyriological transliteration often writes Laḫmu with an underdot ḫ to mark the voiceless velar fricative, but the PuniCodex flagship temple displays the unadorned Latin form Lahmu.

05

LAHMU — The Hairy Guardian

Primordial Protector · Gatekeeper · Silt and Threshold

Lahmu is the Mesopotamian primordial being whose name simply means 'the hairy one'. In the Enuma Elish he is the firstborn of Apsû and Tiāmat, the earliest divine generation before sky and earth were fixed. Beyond cosmogony, he is a protective spirit: a bearded, hair-covered guardian posted at doorways, gates, and thresholds to turn aside demons and pollution.

He stands at the border between chaos and order — born from the mingled primordial waters, then stationed at the doors that keep the ordered world safe.

Primordial Firstborn

First child of Apsû and Tiāmat; the divine line that leads to Anshar, Kishar, Anu, and Ea begins with him.

Guardian of the Gate

In ritual and iconography Lahmu protects temple and palace doorways, the hairy sentinel at the threshold.

The Hairy One

His name and form declare the power of hair: the beard, the mane, the shaggy pelt that marks the apotropaic demon.

Paired with Lahamu

Lahmu and Lahamu appear together as a twin protective pair, male and female guardians of the entrance.

06

Mythology

Stories of Lahmu

Lahmu is not a protagonist of long narrative cycles; his power is concentrated at origins and thresholds. He appears at the very beginning of the Babylonian cosmos, and he endures at the edges of the ordered world as a guardian.

Cosmogony

Firstborn of the Primordial Waters

The Enuma Elish opens with Apsû, the freshwater abyss, and Tiāmat, the salt sea. Their waters mingled and produced the first generation of gods: Lahmu and Lahamu, then Anshar and Kishar, then Anu, and finally Ea (Enuma Elish I:1–10). Lahmu is therefore the first divine body to emerge from chaos, the earliest shape the cosmos takes after the waters meet.

Apotropaic cult

The Hairy Doorkeeper

In ritual texts and iconography Lahmu is a protective demon stationed at doorways. His shaggy body and beard were believed to frighten away evil. Clay figurines and glazed reliefs show him as a muscular, bearded male with curled hair, often flanking an entrance. To pass his gate was to move from dangerous outside into protected inside.

Iconography

The Six-Curled Guardian

Lahmu is recognizable in Mesopotamian art by his long beard arranged in six or seven rounded curls, a naked or lightly clad hairy body, and an aggressive stance. He sometimes grasps a weapon or a gatepost. These attributes made him instantly readable as an apotropaic figure: the hair that named him became the visual sign of his power.

Paired cult

Lahmu and Lahamu

Lahmu is paired with Lahamu, a feminine counterpart whose name shares the same root. Together they guard entrances and thresholds, male and female aspects of a single protective function. In the Enuma Elish the pair is born together; in ritual art they are often depicted as matching figures on either side of a door.
