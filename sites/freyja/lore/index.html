The Authentic Name

Unicode restoration and ASCII comparison

Original Script

ᚠᚱᛅᚢᛁᛅ

The name in its original Norse form. Freyja (ᚠᚱᛅᚢᛁᛅ) is attested in the source tradition — “Lady (from *frawjōn)”. Its original diacritics and script distinctions carry the full phonetic and orthographic weight of the source tradition.

ASCII Constraint

freyja

The plain freyja form is identical to the Unicode restoration. Because this name is already written in Latin letters, no diacritics, stress, or script information were lost — only capitalization differs.

Unicode Restoration

Freyja

The Unicode restoration does not need to recover lost marks for Freyja. Its value is canonical spelling and consistent cataloguing, not the reconstruction of erased orthography. The domain is readable as-is to both DNS and humanity.

Punycode Encoding
Freyja.com → freyja.com

The non-ASCII characters in Freyja are encoded while the ASCII remains visible. To the DNS, it is Punycode. To humanity, it is Freyja.

Pronunciation

How Freyja was spoken

/ˈfrœy.jɑ/ Old Norse Reconstruction

Modern Approximation

FRAY-ya — the first syllable is the diphthong ey, close to English 'ay' in 'say' with rounded lips; stress falls on the first syllable, and the -ja sounds like English 'ya'.

Etymological Kin

  • Old Norse Freyja, 'the Lady' — both common noun and theonym
  • Proto-Germanic *frawjǭ, 'lady', feminine of *frawô, 'lord' — the root of Freyr's name
  • Modern German Frau, 'woman, lady' — the direct descendant of the same word
  • Modern Icelandic Freyja, unchanged in spelling and still in use as a given name

The Accent / Script Rule

Old Norse Freyja is the common noun freyja, 'lady', used as a proper name — the feminine counterpart of her brother's name Freyr, 'lord'. Both descend from Proto-Germanic *frawjǭ, and the cognate line runs through Old High German frouwa to the modern German Frau. Freyja is Tier 2 by rule: the normalized Old Norse spelling is plain Latin letters, so the ASCII form loses nothing — there is no diacritic, no thorn, no eth to restore. The temple form differs from the lowercase entry only by capitalization.

05

The Lady of the Vanir

Brísingamen, Seiðr, and the Choice of the Slain

Freyja is the preeminent goddess of the Norse pantheon: a Vanir among the Æsir, mistress of seiðr magic, owner of the necklace Brísingamen, rider of a chariot drawn by cats, and chooser of half the slain — for she keeps a hall, Sessrúmnir in the field Fólkvangr, and divides the battle-dead with Óðinn himself.

Brísingamen

The necklace of the Brísings, Freyja's great treasure — the jewel Loki steals and Heimdallr wins back, fighting for it in seal-shape.

The Cat-Drawn Chariot

Freyja drives a wagon pulled by two cats — the only chariot-team of its kind in the Eddas, and the image her cult made famous.

The Falcon Cloak

Her valshamr, the falcon-feather coat that lets the wearer fly between worlds — borrowed twice by Loki, and coveted by the giant Þjazi.

Fólkvangr

'The field of the host', Freyja's domain among the slain: her hall there is Sessrúmnir, 'seat-roomy', and half the battle-dead are hers.

Sacred Symbols

Brísingamen The necklace that is worth any price — beauty, desire, and the things a goddess will not be commanded to trade
The cats Her chariot-team: the household animal elevated to divine harness, love and independence in one pair
The falcon-feather cloak The garment of flight — the power to cross worlds that others must borrow from her
Tears of red gold The weeping for the absent Óðr — the myth the skalds built into the kenning 'Freyja's tears' for gold
Hildisvíni 'Battle-swine', the boar she rides in Hyndluljóðr — the Vanir animal of fertility under a warrior's name
06

Mythology

Stories of Freyja

Freyja is everywhere in the Norse corpus: the Poetic Edda, Snorri's prose, and the opening chapters of Heimskringla all circle her — her hall among the dead, her magic, her necklace, her lost husband, and the flyting in which Loki names her last.

Gylfaginning 24

Fólkvangr and the Half of the Slain

Snorri places Freyja beside Frigg as foremost among the goddesses, and gives her what no other goddess has: a share of the battle-dead. 'Her hall is called Sessrúmnir, and it is large and fair. Whenever she rides to battle, she takes half of the slain, and half belong to Óðinn.' Her field is Fólkvangr, the folk-plain; her hall is 'seat-roomy'. The Grímnismál preserves the same division in verse — Freyja chooses the half of the slain each day, Óðinn the other — so the doctrine is older than Snorri: the Lord of the Slain does not keep them all. The Lady keeps a war-dead of her own.

Ynglinga saga 4

She Taught the Æsir Seiðr

Heimskringla's euhemerized history makes Freyja the magic's transmitter: 'Freyja was a blótgyðja, a sacrificial priestess, and she was the first to teach the Æsir seiðr, which had been customary among the Vanir.' Seiðr — the far-reaching, fate-working magic that let its practitioner know the future and shape events — was Vanir craft, and it crossed into the Æsir's world through her; Ynglinga saga adds that Óðinn himself learned it and wielded it beyond anyone. Modern scholarship on seiðr, above all Neil Price's work, treats this notice as the tradition's own memory of a real ritual complex — staffs, seeresses, and performances attested in sagas and graves alike.

Þrymskviða

The Hammer-Ransom

When the giant Þrymr steals Mjölnir and names his price — Freyja as his bride — the gods bring her the offer. Her refusal is one of the Poetic Edda's great moments: 'Freyja was enraged, and snorted, so that the whole hall of the Æsir shook beneath her, and the great necklace of the Brísings burst: “You will know me to be most mad for a man, if I drive with you to the realm of the giants.”' No marriage happens. Instead Þórr is dressed in Freyja's bridal linen, with the necklace Brísingamen at his throat and Loki as his handmaid, and goes to Jötunheimr as the false bride — recovering the hammer under the veil. The poem's joke rests on a fixed point: Freyja cannot be given away; she can only be impersonated.

Hyndluljóðr

Óttarr, Hildisvíni, and the Giantess Hyndla

Freyja protects her devotee Óttarr, who has wagered his inheritance on his ancestry, and carries him to the giantess Hyndla to force out the genealogy he needs. She has transformed Óttarr into her boar Hildisvíni, 'battle-swine', and rides him to the giantess's cave; when Hyndla sees through the shape, Freyja answers without flinching and compels the recitation anyway, demanding the memory-ale that will let Óttarr keep the lore through his lawsuit. The poem also preserves, in its catalogue of Óðinn's gifts, the standing notice that Freyja rides to battle and takes her due of the slain — the prose doctrine of Gylfaginning in older verse.

Skáldskaparmál

Óðr, the Tears of Gold, and the Many Names

Skáldskaparmál assembles Freyja's poetic dossier. Her husband is Óðr; their daughters are Hnoss and Gersimi — 'Treasure' and 'Gem' — names so precious that what is costly is still called by them. Óðr went away on long journeys, and Freyja weeps for him, 'and her tears are red gold' — the myth behind the skaldic kenning that calls gold Freyja's tears. Snorri lists her aliases: Vanadís, 'the dís of the Vanir'; Hörn; Gefn; Sýr, 'Sow'; Mardöll — and she is called Valfreyja, 'Lady of the Slain'. The necklace appears too: gold may be called 'Brísingamen' for her, and Húsdrápa remembers that Loki and Heimdallr once fought for the jewel at Singasteinn, each in the shape of a seal.

Lokasenna

The Flyting

In Loki's feast of insults, Freyja speaks against him and receives the flyting's standard charge — that she has been the lover of all in the hall, Æsir and elves alike. Njǫrðr her father answers for her: 'It is no great harm though a woman gets herself a man, a husband or a lover or both.' The exchange is the Edda's shorthand for what the whole corpus shows: Freyja's sexuality is hers, ungoverned by the marriage bargains others try to make of her — and the tradition itself, in Njǫrðr's voice, declines to call it shame.

Extended Lore

Freyja holds two things the world keeps trying to separate. She is the goddess of love — desire, gold, the necklace, the weeping for Óðr — and she is a commander of the dead, choosing half the slain before Óðinn takes his half. The Edda refuses the split: the same figure who weeps tears of red gold rides to battle; the same name that means 'lady' heads a war-dead of her own. The restoration Freyja needs no diacritics to make the point — the word itself is the argument. In Old Norse, to say 'the lady' was to say her name. The title and the goddess are one word, and the one word is enough.
