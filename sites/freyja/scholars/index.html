01

Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Freyja (freyja) ​— Love, War, ‌Magic · ‌The Lady of ​the Vanir — ​is the preeminent ​goddess of ​the Norse pantheon: ​owner of the ‌necklace Brísingamen, mistress ​of seiðr ​magic, driver of ‌a chariot drawn ​by cats, and ‌chooser of half ​the battle-dead, ‌whom she seats ​in her hall ​Sessrúmnir on the ​field Fólkvangr.[1]

​Old Norse Freyja ​is the common ‌noun freyja ‘lady’ ​elevated to ‌a proper name ​— the feminine ‌counterpart of her ​brother Freyr, ‌‘lord’, and the ​direct cognate of ‌the modern German ​Frau.[2] She ​belongs to the ‌Vanir, the gods ‌of fertility and ‌wealth, but dwells ​among the ​Æsir after the ‌war between the ​two kindreds; Heimskringla ​remembers that ‌it was she ​who first taught ​the Æsir seiðr, ‌‘which had ​been customary among ‌the Vanir’.[3] Her ​mythology is the ​richest of ​any Norse goddess: ​the refusal of ‌the hammer-ransom in ‌Þrymskviða, the ​ride for Óttarr ‌in Hyndluljóð, the ​weeping of red ​gold for the ‌absent Óðr, ‌the necklace won ‌back in seal-shape, ‌and the standing ​doctrine that ‌she takes half ​the slain.[1]

PuniCodex ​keeps the name ​as Freyja ‌— which is ​also the Old ​Norse spelling, letter ​for letter. ‌Unusually among the ​restorations, ASCII loses ​nothing here: the ​normalized form ‌carries no diacritic, ​no thorn, no ‌eth, so the ​name is classified ‌Tier 2, ​plain and complete. ‌The plain-ASCII domain ‌freyja.com is registered ​outside the ​collection and watched ​for release; the ​temple stands canonically ‌at its ‌/freyja/ path in ​the meantime.[2]

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 24 and 35 (Freyja, Fólkvangr, the half of the slain), ed. and trans. Anthony Faulkes (London: Everyman, 1987).
  2. Cleasby, Richard, and Guðbrandur Vigfússon, An Icelandic-English Dictionary, 2nd ed. (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1957), s.v. freyja, freyr.
  3. Snorri Sturluson, Heimskringla, Ynglinga saga 4 (Freyja teaches seiðr), trans. Lee M. Hollander (Austin: University of Texas Press, 1964).

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

The name ​is attested ‌in Old Norse ‌as Freyja (nominative), ​identical with ​the common noun ​freyja ‘lady, ​mistress of a ​household’. It is ‌not a ​compound but a ​transparent title: the ‌feminine formation ​on the same ‌root as ​freyr ‘lord’, her ‌brother's name, both ​descending from ​Proto-Germanic frawjǭ / ​frawô.[1] The ​cognate line is ​one of the ‌clearest in ​Germanic: Old High ‌German frouwa gives ​modern German ‌Frau, and Old ​English frēa ‌‘lord’ preserves the ​masculine side. To ‌name the ​goddess was simply ​to say ‌‘the Lady’.[2]

The ‌name's aliases are ‌part of ​its record: Snorri's ​Skáldskaparmál lists Vanadís ‌‘dís of ​the Vanir’, Hörn, ​Gefn, Sýr ‌‘Sow’, and Mardöll, ​and she is ​Valfreyja, ‘Lady ‌of the Slain’, ​for her ‌share of the ​battle-dead.[3]

Because the ​attested spelling ​is plain Latin ​letters, the ‌ASCII form freyja ‌loses nothing — ​there is ‌no accent, length ​mark, or distinctive ​letter to ‌restore. The name ‌is therefore ‌Tier 2: complete ‌in ASCII, with ​only capitalization ‌separating the temple ​form from ​the dictionary entry.

​The letter-by-letter transformation ‌runs:

  • ​f → F ​— Same, capitalized

​- r ‌→ r — ​Same

  • ​e → e ​— Same
  • ‌y → ​y — Same

‌- j ​→ j — ‌Same

  • a ​→ a ‌— Same

The ‌domain freyja.com is ​registered outside ​the PuniCodex collection; ​the project ​watches it for ‌release and serves ‌the temple ​at /freyja/.

Sources

  1. Cleasby, Richard, and Guðbrandur Vigfússon, An Icelandic-English Dictionary, 2nd ed. (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1957), s.v. freyja.
  2. de Vries, Jan, Altnordisches etymologisches Wörterbuch, 2nd ed. (Leiden: Brill, 1962), s.v. freyja, freyr.
  3. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál (Freyja's names and gold-kennings), trans. Faulkes (1987).

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The reconstructed ​pronunciation of ‌the name ‌is /ˈfrœy.jɑ/ ​— Old ​Norse Reconstruction.[1]

Phoneme ​by phoneme:

​- Frey- ​— Voiceless ‌labiodental [f], trilled ​[r], and ​the Old ‌Norse diphthong ​ey, a ‌rounded front glide ​[œy] that ‌survives in ​modern Icelandic ​as a ​sound close to ​English ‘ay’ ​in ‘say’; ‌the first ​syllable carries the ‌stress, as ​in all ‌Old Norse ​words

  • ‌-ja — Palatal ​glide [j] ‌followed by ​open [ɑ], ​the unstressed second ‌syllable sounding ‌like English ‌‘ya’

For ​the modern ​speaker, the closest ‌approximation is: ​‘FRAY-ya’ — ​the first ‌syllable as ​in ‘fray’, the ​second a ‌light ‘ya’.

​Kindred and ‌historical forms of ​the name:

​- Old ​Norse — ​Freyja, ‘the ‌Lady’, the attested ‌form

  • ​Proto-Germanic — ‌*frawjǭ, ‘lady’, ​the reconstructed ​ancestor
  • Old ‌High German ‌— frouwa, ‌ancestor of ‌modern German Frau

​- Modern ‌Icelandic — ​Freyja, unchanged, ​and still ​a living given ‌name

Freyja ​is Tier ​2 because ​the attested form ‌needs nothing ​ASCII lacks: ​every letter ​of the ‌Old Norse spelling ​is already ‌a plain ​Latin letter, ‌and the ​diphthong ey was ‌never marked ‌with a ​diacritic. English ​‘Freyja’ and Old ​Norse Freyja ​are the ‌same word ‌in the ​same letters.[2]

Sources

  1. Gordon, E. V., An Introduction to Old Norse, 2nd ed. rev. A. R. Taylor (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1957), phonology and grammar.
  2. Zoëga, Geir T., A Concise Dictionary of Old Icelandic (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1910), s.v. Freyja.

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

The name ​is preserved ‌in medieval ‌Icelandic manuscripts ​in the ​Latin alphabet ​as Freyja ​— Old ​Norse (North ‌Germanic), attested ​13th c. ​CE in ‌the Codex ​Regius of ‌the Poetic ​Edda and ‌the Prose ​Edda's codices, ​recording material ​centuries older.[1] ​Before the ​Latin bookhand, ‌Old Norse ​names were ‌carved in ​the Younger ‌Futhark; no ​runic attestation ‌of this ​theonym survives, ‌so the ​manuscript form ​is the ‌primary witness.

‌The scholarly ‌rendering is, ​for once, ​a short ‌story:

  • ​The name ​is the ‌common noun ​freyja ‘lady’, ​feminine counterpart ‌of freyr ‘lord’, ​used as ‌a theonym.

​- Old ​Norse orthography ​writes the ​diphthong as ‌ey (runic ‌ᛅᚢ, the ​au-rune) and ‌requires no ​accent or ​special letter ‌anywhere in ‌the word.
‌- A ‌hypothetical Younger ​Futhark rendering ‌approximates ᚠᚱᛅᚢᛁᛅ ​(f-r-au-i-a).

  • ​The temple ​form Freyja ‌and the ​ASCII form ​freyja are ​therefore the ‌same letters; ​the restoration ​is complete ​in plain ‌Latin, and ​the name ‌is Tier ​2 by ‌rule.[2]

The ​plain-ASCII domain ‌freyja.com is ‌registered outside ​the PuniCodex ​collection and ​watched for ​release; the ‌temple is ‌served canonically ​at /freyja/.

Sources

  1. Gordon, E. V., An Introduction to Old Norse, 2nd ed. rev. Taylor (Clarendon Press, 1957).
  2. Cleasby & Vigfússon, An Icelandic-English Dictionary, 2nd ed. (Clarendon Press, 1957), s.v. Freyja.

Domains & Attributes

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sphere of influence, titles, epithets, domain cards.

Freyja ​governs the ‌triad her ‌temple ​carries — ​Love, ​War, Magic ​— and ​the ‌sources give ​each ​sphere a ‌concrete, attested ​shape.[1]

‌### Love

​Goddess ‌of desire ​and of ​its ​costs: she ​weeps ​tears of ‌red gold ​for ‌the absent ​Óðr, ‌and the ​skalds call ‌gold ​itself ‘Freyja's ‌tears’ ​in her ​memory.

‌War

‌She rides ‌to ​battle and ​takes half ‌the ​slain, seating ​them ‌in Sessrúmnir ​on Fólkvangr ​— ‌the only ​figure ‌in the ​pantheon who ​divides ​the war-dead ​with ‌Óðinn as ‌an equal.

​### ‌Magic

Mistress ​of ​seiðr: Heimskringla ‌remembers that ‌she, ‌a blótgyðja ‌of ​the Vanir, ‌first taught ​the ​art to ​the ‌Æsir — ​the craft ​Óðinn ​would later ‌wield ​beyond anyone.[2]

​### The ​Vanir

‌She is ​Vanadís, ‌‘the dís ​of the ‌Vanir’: ​fertility, wealth, ‌and ‌desire carried ​into the ​Æsir's ​halls after ​the ‌war between ‌the two ​kindreds.

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 24, 35, and Skáldskaparmál, trans. Faulkes (1987).
  2. Snorri Sturluson, Heimskringla, Ynglinga saga 4, trans. Hollander (1964).

Symbols

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Iconography, attributes, and their meanings.

Freyja's attributes ​are the ‌most concrete ‌of any ​Norse goddess ​— the ​corpus hands ​her a ​full set ‌of regalia:[1]

​- Brísingamen ​— the ‌necklace of ​the Brísings, ‌her great ​treasure: worn ‌by Þórr ​in his ​bridal disguise, ​fought for ​by Loki ​and Heimdallr ‌in seal-shape ​at Singasteinn, ‌and so ​bound to ‌her that ​gold itself ‌takes her ​name in ‌the kennings
​- The ​cat-drawn chariot ‌— she ‌drives a ‌wagon harnessed ​to two ​cats, the ‌only such ​team in ​the Eddas; ‌the household ​animal raised ​to divine ‌harness

  • ​The falcon-feather ‌cloak — ​her valshamr, ​the coat ​that lends ​flight between ‌worlds; Loki ‌borrows it ​twice, and ‌the giant ​Þjazi names ​it as ‌part of ‌her ransom-price

‌- Hildisvíni ‌— ‘battle-swine’, ​the boar ‌she rides ​in Hyndluljóð, ​her devotee ​Óttarr in ‌animal shape ​beneath her
​- Tears ​of red ‌gold — ​her weeping ​for Óðr ​on his ‌long journeys, ​the myth ‌the skalds ​built into ‌the kenning ​‘Freyja's tears’ ‌for gold[2]
‌- Sessrúmnir ​— ‘the ​seat-roomy hall’ ​on Fólkvangr, ​where her ‌half of ‌the slain ​are housed

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 24, 35 and Skáldskaparmál, trans. Faulkes (1987).
  2. Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide to the Gods, Heroes, Rituals, and Beliefs (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. Freyja.

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

Freyja's mythology is ​the fullest of any ‌Norse goddess, spread across ‌both Eddas and Heimskringla.

​The Half of the ​Slain (Gylfaginning 24; Grímnismál)

​Snorri numbers Freyja with ​Frigg as foremost of the ​goddesses and gives her ‌a war-office no other ​goddess holds: ‘whenever she rides ​to battle, she takes ‌half of the slain, ​and half belong to Óðinn’. ‌Her hall is Sessrúmnir, ​‘seat-roomy’, on the field ‌Fólkvangr. Grímnismál preserves the same ​doctrine in older verse ​— she chooses her ​half of the slain ​each day.[1]

She Taught ​the Æsir Seiðr (Ynglinga ‌saga 4)

Heimskringla's euhemerized ​account makes her the transmitter ‌of magic: Freyja, a ​sacrificial priestess of the ‌Vanir, ‘was the first to ​teach the Æsir seiðr, ‌which had been customary ​among the Vanir’. The same ‌chapter says Óðinn learned ​the art and wielded ​it beyond anyone — ‌the Vanir craft passing to ‌the Æsir through her ‌hands.[2]

The Hammer-Ransom ​(Þrymskviða)

When Þrymr steals Mjölnir ​and demands Freyja as ‌his bride, her refusal ​shakes the hall of the ​Æsir and bursts the ‌Brísingamen at her throat: ​‘You will know me to ​be most mad for ‌a man, if I ​drive with you to ‌the realm of the giants.’ ​Þórr goes in her ​stead, dressed in her ​bridal linen with her necklace ​at his throat and ‌Loki as handmaid, and ‌recovers the hammer beneath the ​veil.[3]

Óttarr and ‌Hyndla (Hyndluljóð)

Freyja protects ​her devotee Óttarr, transformed into ​her boar Hildisvíni, and ‌forces the giantess Hyndla ‌to recite the ancestry ‌his inheritance depends on — ‌demanding the memory-ale that ​will carry the lore ‌through his lawsuit.[3]

Óðr, ​the Gold, and the ​Necklace (Skáldskaparmál)

Her husband ​Óðr fares away on long ‌journeys; she seeks him ​among strange peoples and ​weeps tears of red gold. ​Her daughters are Hnoss ‌and Gersimi — ‘Treasure’ ​and ‘Gem’. And the ​necklace: Húsdrápa remembers that Loki ​and Heimdallr fought for ‌the Brísingamen at Singasteinn, ​each in the shape of ‌a seal.[4]

The ​Flyting (Lokasenna)

When Freyja ‌speaks against Loki at Ægir's ​feast, he answers with ‌the flyting's standard charge ‌against her lovers; Njǫrðr her ​father replies: ‘It is ​no great harm though ​a woman gets herself ​a man, a husband or ‌a lover or both.’ ‌The corpus's own verdict ​on her independence.[3]

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 24 and 35; Grímnismál (Poetic Edda), trans. Faulkes (1987) and Larrington (2014).
  2. Snorri Sturluson, Heimskringla, Ynglinga saga 4, trans. Hollander (1964).
  3. The Poetic Edda, trans. Carolyne Larrington, 2nd ed. (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2014): Þrymskviða, Hyndluljóð, Lokasenna.
  4. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál (Óðr, Hnoss and Gersimi, Freyja's tears; Húsdrápa on the necklace), trans. Faulkes (1987).

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

The great ​open question ‌of Freyja ‌scholarship is ​her relationship to ​Frigg. Both ​are chief ​goddesses; both ​own a ‌falcon cloak; both ​weep for ​a wandering ‌husband — ​and Óðr's name ‌is a ​consonant's breadth ‌from Óðinn's. ​Since the ​nineteenth century, scholars ​have argued ​whether the ​two were ‌originally one goddess ​whom the ‌recording traditions ​split in ‌two; the sources ​leave the ‌question open, ​and the ‌modern handbooks ​— Simek, Orchard, ​Lindow — ‌still mark ‌it unsettled ‌rather than resolved.[1]

​The interpretatio ​latina equated ‌her with ​Venus, and ​the Germanic week ‌keeps the ​trace: Friday ​is dies ‌Veneris, ‘the Lady's ​day’ — ‌though whether ​the day ​was named for ​Freyja or ​for Frigg ‌is itself ‌part of ​the same debate.[2] ‌Beyond Germania ​there is ​no borrowing ‌to claim: the ‌Vanir complex ‌— fertility, ‌wealth, seiðr, ​and a ‌share of the ​battle-dead — ​is the ​North's own ‌construction, and the ​goddess who ​taught magic ​to the ‌Æsir has no ​exact counterpart ​in any ​other pantheon. ‌Closely related ​figures in the ‌corpus include ​Freyr (her ‌brother and ​masculine counterpart), Óðinn ‌(who shares ‌the slain ​with her ​and learned ​her craft), and ​Heimdallr (who ‌fought for ‌her necklace ​in seal-shape).

Sources

  1. Simek, Rudolf, Dictionary of Northern Mythology, trans. Angela Hall (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer, 1993), s.v. Freyja, Frigg.
  2. Orchard, Andy, Dictionary of Norse Myth and Legend (London: Cassell, 1997), s.v. Freyja, Friday.

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

Freyja's ​afterlife is ‌the longest ‌of any ​Norse goddess. ​Wagner fused ​her ​with Iðunn ​into Freia, ‌the goddess ​of the ​golden apples ‌whose ransom ​sets ‌the Ring ​cycle in ‌motion, and ​the nineteenth-century ​mythographies cast ​her ​as the ​Venus of ‌the North ​— a ‌comparison older ​than ‌the Romantics, ​since the ‌interpretatio latina ​had made ‌it a ​millennium ​before.[1]

In ‌modern practice ‌she stands ‌at the ​center of ​Heathenry and ‌Ásatrú: ​honored as ​mistress of ‌seiðr, patron ​of the ​half of ‌the ​slain, and ‌model of ​ungoverned desire; ​the contemporary ​revival of ​seiðr ‌work in ‌Scandinavia and ​beyond traces ‌its warrant ​directly to ​her teaching ‌in ‌Ynglinga saga, ‌with Neil ‌Price's scholarship ​as its ‌academic frame.[2] ​Her ​name never ​left Scandinavia: ‌Freyja is ​a living ​— and ​in ‌recent decades ​a strikingly ​popular — ​girls' given ‌name in ​Iceland ‌and across ​the North. ‌And the ​week still ‌keeps her ‌cognate every ​Friday, ​dies Veneris, ​the Lady's ​day, whichever ‌goddess the ‌naming finally ​credited.[1]

Sources

  1. Orchard, Andy, Dictionary of Norse Myth and Legend (London: Cassell, 1997), s.v. Freyja; Simek, Dictionary of Northern Mythology (1993), s.v. Freia, Friday.
  2. Price, Neil, The Viking Way: Magic and Mind in Late Iron Age Scandinavia, 2nd ed. (Oxford: Oxbow, 2019).

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

No ​inscription names ‌the goddess ‌Freyja: like ​most of ​the pantheon, ​her attestation ​is textual, ​and the ‌material record ​offers ​context rather ‌than proofs.[1] ​The theophoric ‌place-names of ​Sweden and ‌Norway are ​dense with ​the Frö-/Frøy- ​element, but ​most ​are assigned ‌to her ​brother Freyr; ‌a smaller ​set — ‌Norway's island ​Frøya among ‌them — ​is ‌argued either ​way, and ​the name's ‌transparency (‘the ‌Lady’) makes ‌secure attribution ​harder, not ​easier.[2]

The ‌strongest material ​witness ​belongs to ‌her craft ​rather than ​her cult. ‌The seiðr ​she taught ‌the Æsir ​is the ​best-attested ritual ​complex ​in the ‌Viking-Age graves: ‌the so-called ​völva burials ‌of Scandinavia, ​furnished with ​iron staffs, ‌high-status goods, ‌and in ‌some ‌cases the ​seeds of ‌mind-altering plants, ​are the ​archaeological frame ​in which ‌Neil Price's ​The Viking ​Way ​reads the ‌whole practice. ​Small silver ​pendants of ​standing female ‌figures, long ​labelled valkyries, ‌have been ​drawn into ‌the ​same reinterpretation ‌— the ‌tradition's seeress ​and its ​Lady overlap ​in the ​metal as ‌they do ‌in the ​verse.[3]

Sources

  1. Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide to the Gods, Heroes, Rituals, and Beliefs (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. Freyja.
  2. Simek, Rudolf, Dictionary of Northern Mythology, trans. Hall (1993), s.v. Freyja; on theophoric place-names.
  3. Price, Neil, The Viking Way: Magic and Mind in Late Iron Age Scandinavia, 2nd ed. (Oxford: Oxbow, 2019), on staff burials and the archaeology of seiðr.

Poetic Edda

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The ​Poetic Edda ‌gives Freyja ‌her widest ​stage. ​Grímnismál preserves, ​in older ​verse, the ​doctrine ‌Snorri would ​repeat in ​prose: Freyja ‌chooses ​half the ‌slain each ​day and ‌seats ​them in ​Sessrúmnir — ​Óðinn takes ​the ​other half.[1]

‌Þrymskviða gives ​her the ‌corpus's ​most quoted ‌refusal: the ​enraged snort ‌that ​shakes the ‌hall and ​bursts the ​Brísingamen, ‌followed by ‌the comedy ‌of Þórr's ​bridal ​disguise — ‌her linen, ​her keys, ​her ‌necklace — ​with Loki ​as handmaid. ‌Hyndluljóð ​shows her ‌as a ​patron in ​action: ​her devotee ​Óttarr transformed ‌into the ‌boar ​Hildisvíni beneath ‌her, the ​giantess Hyndla ​compelled ‌to recite ‌the ancestry ‌his inheritance ‌depends ​on, and ‌the memory-ale ​demanded to ​make ​the lore ‌stick through ​the lawsuit.[2] ​And ​Lokasenna closes ‌the sequence ​with the ​flyting ​— Loki's ‌charge of ​promiscuity answered ‌by ​Njǫrðr's flat ‌refusal to ​call it ‌shame. ‌Together the ​four poems ​sketch the ​whole ​figure: war-share, ‌refusal, patronage, ‌and ungoverned ​desire.[3]

Sources

  1. Grímnismál (Poetic Edda), stanza on Fólkvangr and the half of the slain, trans. Larrington (2014).
  2. Þrymskviða and Hyndluljóð (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).
  3. Lokasenna (Poetic Edda), Freyja's exchange and Njǫrðr's answer, trans. Larrington (2014).

Prose Edda

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The ​Prose Edda ‌is Freyja's ‌systematic ​witness. Gylfaginning ​24 ​places her ​beside Frigg ​as ‌foremost of ​the ​goddesses and ‌states the ​war-office ‌in full: ​the hall ‌Sessrúmnir, ​the field ​Fólkvangr, ​and half ​the slain ​whenever ‌she rides ​to ‌battle; Gylfaginning ​35 repeats ‌her ​standing among ‌the ​ásynjur and ‌her title ​Valfreyja.[1]

​Skáldskaparmál builds ‌her poetic ‌dossier: ‌the husband ​Óðr ​who fared ‌away on ​long ​journeys; the ‌daughters ​Hnoss and ​Gersimi, ‘Treasure’ ‌and ​‘Gem’, whose ‌names became ​bywords ​for what ​is ​costly; the ‌tears of ‌red ​gold that ‌made ​‘Freyja's tears’ ​the skalds' ‌kenning ‌for gold; ‌and the ‌catalogue ​of aliases ‌— ​Vanadís, Hörn, ​Gefn, Sýr, ​Mardöll. ‌The necklace ​runs ​through the ​kennings too: ‌gold ​may be ​called ​Brísingamen for ‌her, and ​the ‌quotation from ​Húsdrápa preserves ‌the ​fight for ‌the ‌jewel at ​Singasteinn, where ​Loki ​and Heimdallr ​contended ‌in the ‌shapes of ​seals.[2]

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 24 and 35, trans. Faulkes (1987).
  2. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál (Óðr, Hnoss and Gersimi, the aliases, Húsdrápa), trans. Faulkes (1987).

Runic Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

No ​runic inscription ‌invokes the ‌goddess ​Freyja: no ​runestone ​asks her ​favour, no ​amulet ‌bears her ​name, ​and no ‌inscription yet ​found ‌links a ​dedicant to ‌her ​cult — ​a ​silence she ​shares with ​most ‌of the ​pantheon.[1]

‌What the ​epigraphic record ‌does ​preserve is ‌the ​name's raw ‌material and ​its ​world. The ‌word's root ‌is ‌among the ​most ​productive in ‌Germanic: the ​masculine ​side, frawô ‌‘lord’, ​stands behind ​Freyr's name ‌and ​the Old ‌English frēa, ​and ​the feminine ​line ​runs unbroken ‌into German ‌Frau. ​A hypothetical ‌Younger ​Futhark rendering ​of the ‌theonym ‌would approximate ‌ᚠᚱᛅᚢᛁᛅ (f-r-au-i-a); ‌the ​futhark's au-rune ‌ᛅᚢ ​would carry ​the diphthong ​the ‌Latin manuscripts ​write ​as ey. ​The material ‌context ​for her ​attested ​craft is ‌firmer: the ​iron ‌staffs of ​the Viking-Age ‌‘völva ​burials’ are ‌the ‌archaeological signature ​of the ​seiðr ​that Ynglinga ​saga ‌says she ‌taught the ​Æsir.[2]

Sources

  1. Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide to the Gods, Heroes, Rituals, and Beliefs (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. Freyja.
  2. Price, Neil, The Viking Way, 2nd ed. (Oxbow, 2019), on staff burials; Cleasby & Vigfússon, s.v. freyja.

Sagas & Medieval Sources

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Beyond ​the Eddas, ‌Freyja's most ‌consequential saga ​appearance is ​programmatic: Ynglinga ​saga, the ​opening ​of Snorri's ‌Heimskringla, makes ​her the ​hinge between ‌the Vanir ​and the ‌Æsir. ​Daughter of ‌Njǫrðr, sister-bride ​in the ​Vanir custom, ​blótgyðja — ​sacrificial ​priestess — ‌she is ​the one ‌who ‘first ​taught the ‌Æsir seiðr, ​which ‌had been ​customary among ‌the Vanir’; ​the same ​passage says ‌Óðinn took ‌the ‌craft up ​and practised ​it beyond ‌anyone.[1]

The ​later legendary ​material keeps ‌the ​necklace in ​play: Sǫrla ‌þáttr, a ​fourteenth-century þáttr ‌attached to ​Óláfs ​saga Tryggvasonar ​in Flateyjarbók, ​tells how ‌Freyja acquired ‌the Brísingamen ​from four ‌dwarf ​smiths and ​how Óðinn ‌set Loki ‌to steal ‌it — ‌a late, ​euhemerized ‌tale, but ​the medieval ​tradition's only ​connected account ‌of the ​jewel's purchase, ​and ​the reason ‌the necklace's ​price became ​proverbial.[2] Across ​the sagas ‌more ​broadly her ‌name does ​quieter work: ‌the kenning ​‘Freyja's tears’ ‌for gold, ‌learned ​from Skáldskaparmál, ​echoes through ​skaldic verse ​wherever poets ‌needed to ‌say wealth ​beautifully.

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Heimskringla, Ynglinga saga 4, trans. Hollander (1964).
  2. Sǫrla þáttr (Flateyjarbók), on the acquisition of the Brísingamen; see Orchard, Dictionary of Norse Myth and Legend (1997), s.v. Brísingamen.

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

Freyja ​holds two ‌things the ‌world keeps ​trying ​to separate. ​She is ​the goddess ​of love ‌— ​desire, gold, ​the necklace, ‌the weeping ​for ‌Óðr — ​and she ‌is a ​commander of ​the ​dead, choosing ​half the ​slain before ‌Óðinn ​takes his ‌half. The ​Edda refuses ‌the ​split: the ‌same figure ​who weeps ‌tears of ​red ​gold rides ‌to battle; ‌the same ‌word ​that means ​‘lady’ heads ‌a war-dead ​of her ​own.[1]

‌Her refusals ​are as ​load-bearing as ‌her ​powers. Offered ‌to Þrymr ​as a ​bride-price, ​she does ​not argue; ‌the hall ‌shakes and ​the ‌necklace bursts. ​Charged by ​Loki with ‌her ‌lovers, the ‌tradition answers ‌through her ​father: it ‌is ​no shame. ​The corpus ​never lets ‌her ​be traded, ​commanded, or ​shamed — ‌only ​impersonated, invoked, ​and followed. ​To say ‌her name ​in ‌Old Norse ​was simply ‌to say ​‘the ‌lady’: the ‌title and ​the goddess ​are one ​word, ​and the ‌one word ‌was always ​enough.

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 24 and Skáldskaparmál, trans. Faulkes (1987); Þrymskviða and Lokasenna, trans. Larrington (2014).
