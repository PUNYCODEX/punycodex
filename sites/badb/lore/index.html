The Authentic Name

Unicode restoration and ASCII comparison

Scholarly Transliteration

Badb

The name survives in scholarly transliteration. Badb is the standard Celtic romanisation, documented in academic sources — “Crow, scald-crow”. Because the spelling uses only Latin letters, the form is the same in both ASCII and Unicode.

No indigenous writing system is securely attested for individual celtic names. The form shown is a modern scholarly transliteration.

ASCII Constraint

badb

The plain badb form is identical to the Unicode restoration. Because this name is already written in Latin letters, no diacritics, stress, or script information were lost — only capitalization differs.

Unicode Restoration

The Unicode restoration does not need to recover lost marks for Badb. Its value is canonical spelling and consistent cataloguing, not the reconstruction of erased orthography. The domain is readable as-is to both DNS and humanity.

Pronunciation

How Badb was spoken

/ˈbaðβ/ Old Irish Reconstruction
B- Voiced bilabial stop [b], broad (velarized) before a; as in English 'bay', with the tongue already set for the wide vowel.
-a- Short open [a], the stressed vowel of the single syllable — the 'ah' of 'father' held brief.
-db Lenited [ð], the voiced dental fricative of English 'this', running into lenited final [β], a soft bilabial v — both consonants melt in speech though the spelling keeps them whole.

Modern Approximation

'BAH-dhuv' — one short syllable; let the d soften into the 'th' of 'this' and the final b melt into a light v. Modern Irish badhbh keeps the softening, and English ears often hear the word as something like 'bave'.

Etymological Kin

  • Old Irish badb, 'scald-crow' — the common noun for the hooded crow and the theonym are the same word; badb catha, 'crow of battle', is her standing title
  • Proto-Celtic *bodwā, 'battle, fighting' — the root the name shares with the war-names of Gaul (Matasović, EDPC)
  • Gaulish Cathubodua, 'battle-crow' — the goddess of the Mieussy altar, her name the exact reverse compound of badb catha
  • Modern Irish badhbh, 'scald-crow, hooded crow' — the unbroken living word

The Accent / Script Rule

Badb is Tier 2 by the mechanical rule: the normalized Old Irish form is already plain ASCII — there is no diacritic and no distinctive letter for the ASCII alphabet to lose. The capital alone distinguishes the temple form from the input; the goddess's standing in the tradition is beside the point.

05

Battle, Death, Crow

The Scald-Crow of the Battlefield

Badb is the Irish war-goddess whose name is her shape: badb, the scald-crow — the grey-and-black hooded crow that walks the battlefield when the spears have done their work. She is one of the three of war whom the Lebor Gabála calls the daughters of Ernmas — Badb, Macha, and Anand, the Morrígan — and the Táin gives her its most terrible sound: the shriek over the marching hosts that kills men with fear before a blow is struck. She never fights; she foretells, she panics, and she gathers what the battle leaves.

Badb Catha

'Crow of battle' — her standing title in the texts: the bird that haunts the field and the goddess who owns it are one word.

The Shriek over the Hosts

At Gáirech and Irgáirech, the Táin says, Badb and Bé Néit and Némain shrieked above the armies until a hundred warriors fell dead of fright.

The Ford

The boundary water where the war-goddess stands washing — the Morrígan at the ford of the Unius, the washer of the doomed in the later Gaelic tradition.

The Daughters of Ernmas

Badb, Macha, and Anand — the triple face of Irish war, three sisters of one mother whom the sources also call by the single name Morrígan.

Sacred Symbols

The scald-crow The hooded crow, grey and black — her name, her shape, and her retinue on every field
The shriek Panic as a weapon: the sound that kills men with fear before the spear is thrown
The ford The boundary between bank and bank, living and dead — the water where the doomed are read
The triad Badb, Macha, Némain: the three of war, one terror in three voices
The washing of spoils The harness and garments of the soon-dead, rinsed in the ford before the battle that will bloody them
06

Mythology

Stories of Badb

Badb's dossier is genealogy, sound, and omen rather than adventure: she is the sister in the list, the voice over the army, the bird on the pillar-stone. The Lebor Gabála fixes her family, the Táin fixes her weapon, and the death-tales fix her posture — and the commentators have spent a century and a half working out where she ends and her sisters begin.

Lebor Gabála Érenn

The Daughters of Ernmas

The Lebor Gabála Érenn, Ireland's medieval book of invasions, gives the war-goddesses their pedigree: 'Badb and Macha and Anand, of whom are the Paps of Anu in Luachair, were the three daughters of Ernmas the she-farmer.' The verse recension sings the same family in another key — 'Badb and Macha, greatness of wealth, Morrígu, springs of craftiness, sources of bitter fighting, were the three daughters of Ernmas' — so that the tradition itself is of two minds: sometimes the Morrígan is the third sister, sometimes the single name for all three.

The same book marries her into the house of the war-god. Neit son of Indui, the god of battle, had two wives — Badb and Nemain — and the pedigree records the slaying of the three of them at Ailech by the Fomorians. Cormac's Glossary had already fixed the marriage in the language of record: Neith, the god of battle among the pagan Gael, and Nemon his wife. Badb is never an isolated figure; she is a sister and a wife in a family whose whole business is war.

Táin Bó Cúailnge

The Shriek over the Hosts

When the men of Ireland march under [[medb|Medb]] for the brown bull of Cooley, the Táin marks the nights of the hosting with the sound of the war-goddesses: 'Badb and Bé Néit and Némain shrieked above them that night in Gáirech and Irgáirech, so that a hundred of their warriors fell dead of fright.' It is the battle's true opening — the killing has begun before a single spear is cast, and its instrument is a voice.

The passage tells you everything about her method. She does not wound; she announces. The shriek over the column is prophecy delivered as noise, the knowledge of the field's end injected into the march at its beginning — and the Irish phrase for the whole business of battlefield terror, badb catha, the crow of battle, is simply her name at work as a common noun.

Cath Maige Tuired

The War-Goddess at Mag Tuired

The Second Battle of Mag Tuired, the war the Túatha Dé Danann fight against the Fomorians, runs on the war-goddess's work from its eve to its aftermath. On Samain night [[dagda|the Dagda]] met the Morrígan at the ford of the Unius in Connacht, where she stood washing with one foot on either bank; they lay together at the ford, and she pledged herself to the Túatha Dé Danann's cause, promising to bring the hearts of the Fomorian host and the blood of their king Indech.

After [[lugh|Lugh]]'s sling-stone felled [[balor|Balor]] and the Fomorians broke, it was the Morrígan who proclaimed the battle and the rout to the royal heights of Ireland and its síd-hosts, and who uttered the great prophecy — 'Peace up to heaven, heaven down to earth, earth beneath heaven' — and then its darker twin, the vision of the world's ending. The commentators note that the texts keep the sisters' voices interchangeable: what the manuscript gives to the Morrígan, the tradition hears also in Badb's register, and the Lebor Gabála had already made them one family.

Aided Con Culainn

The Crow on the Pillar-Stone

Cú Chulainn's death-tale, Brislech Mór Maige Muirthemni, gives the crow-goddesses the last word over the greatest of the Ulster heroes. Mortally wounded at Muirthemne, [[cuchulainn|Cú Chulainn]] lashed himself to a pillar-stone so that he would die on his feet, and for three days his enemies would not close in, uncertain whether he lived. Then the battle-goddess Morrígu came with her sisters in the form of scald-crows and settled on his shoulder — and Erc said, 'That pillar is not wont to be under birds,' and only then did the host dare approach and take the hero's head.

It is the emblematic scene of her whole dossier: the scald-crow is the signature of death itself, the field's first and final reader. The living man keeps her off; the dead man is her perch.

Gaelic Tradition

The Washer at the Ford

The medieval texts give the war-goddess her washing-place — the Morrígan washing at the ford of the Unius on the eve of Mag Tuired — and the later Gaelic tradition gives the figure her full shape: the washer at the ford, the woman seen at the boundary water rinsing the blood-stained garments and harness of those about to die in battle. In Scottish Gaelic folklore she is the bean nighe, the washerwoman whose sight foretells death, and the commentators, Mac Cana among them, treat her as the folk continuation of the Badb's own figure.

The washing is prophecy, not threat. The doom is already written — the garments in her hands belong to men still alive and armed — and she merely launders the evidence. No Irish figure holds the moment before death so patiently: not the blow, not the battle, but the foreknowledge, rinsed clean in running water.

Extended Lore

Badb is the rare theonym that refuses to be a metaphor: the word for the bird and the name of the goddess are one, and the medieval writers never felt the need to choose between them. To name her is already to say what she does. Other gods have attributes; she has a species — the grey scald-crow that comes down to the field when the field is finished, and the texts simply ask us to understand that the bird knew all along.
