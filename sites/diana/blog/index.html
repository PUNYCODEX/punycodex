Why Diāna belongs in your address bar

Every address bar is a choice. When you type Diāna, you are not typing a novelty; you are restoring a name that the early DNS, built for English typewriters, could not carry. The plain ASCII form diana is a leftover of that constraint, not the name itself. This post is the long version of the restoration: where the name comes from, how the Latin (epigraphic capitals) tradition wrote it, how it is pronounced, what the myths and the material record preserve, and why its Unicode form now lives as a working domain. The claim throughout is simple — the original spelling is not decoration. It is the name.

Overview

Diāna (diana) — The Huntress of the Moon — belongs to the Roman tradition, where it is catalogued under the domain "Moon, Hunt, Wilderness". The name means "'The bright one, goddess' (from the root *dyēu-, to shine)".

Diāna is Rome's own goddess before she is Artemis imported: mistress of the wildwood, the moon, and women in labour, whose strangest institution — the priest-king of Nemi who wins his office by murder — haunted anthropology for a century.

PuniCodex restores the name as Diāna and serves its temple at diāna.com. The restoration preserves the long ā of the original — a distinctive feature the ASCII form loses — which places the name in Tier 1. The plain ASCII form diana survives as a modern convenience imposed by the early domain-name system; the restoration, not the fallback, is the form the project defends as philologically complete.

The Name

No indigenous written attestation survives for this name; Diāna is a scholarly transliteration of the reconstructed spoken form. Etymologically the name means "'The bright one, goddess' (from the root *dyēu-, to shine)".

The ASCII form diana survives only because the early domain-name system could not carry diacritics; it is a technological compromise, not an ancient spelling. The Unicode restoration Diāna recovers the vowel length of the original directly in the address bar. The restoration preserves the long ā of the original — a distinctive feature the ASCII form loses — which places the name in Tier 1.

The letter-by-letter transformation runs:

  • d → D — Same letter
  • i → i — Same letter
  • a → ā — Macron marks the long vowel
  • n → n — Same letter
  • a → a — Same letter

The project holds the domain diāna.com (xn--dina-rsa.com) as the canonical home of this name.

Pronunciation

The reconstructed pronunciation of the name is /diˈaː.na/ — Classical Latin.

For the modern speaker, the closest approximation is: dee-AH-nah, with the long ā on the stressed second syllable.

Kindred and historical forms of the name:

  • Latin — Diāna
  • Greek twin — Ἄρτεμις
  • Owned form — Diāna

Latin Diāna scans with a long i and long final ā; the owned restoration Diāna follows the classical spelling exactly. Roman grammarians connected the name to the shining of the moon (from the same root as Iūpiter's Dyēus, "the bright sky"), making her the feminine face of celestial light.

Mythology

Roman myth largely retells Artemis' stories under Diāna's name — but her cult institutions are Italy's own, older than the stories, and stranger.

Actaeon (The Bath)

Ovid tells it as a warning about seeing: the hunter Actaeon, wandering at noon, stumbles on Diāna bathing in a hidden valley. She splashes him with water — "now you may tell that you saw me, if you can" — and he becomes a stag, torn apart by his own hounds who know only the shape of prey, not the voice of their master.

The King of the Wood (The Grove)

At Nemi, in Diāna's lakeside grove, the priesthood was held by an escaped slave who became rex Nemorensis by plucking a bough from the sacred tree and killing his predecessor in single combat — and who then guarded the tree, sword in hand, against the next challenger. The rite, already archaic in Ovid's day, frames Frazer's entire Golden Bough.

Hippolytus Reborn (The Refuge)

Roman tradition made Nemi the retirement of a Greek ghost: Virbius, the twice-man — Hippolytos, dragged to death by his horses and raised again by Aesculapius — hidden by Diāna in her grove, where no horse may enter. The goddess shelters the chaste hunter her Greek sister could not save.

The Aventine Charter (The Alliance)

Servius Tullius founded Diāna's temple on the Aventine as the federal shrine of the Latin cities — the one cult they shared with Rome as equals. A Sabine's marvel of a cow, sacrificed there by a cunning priest, sealed Rome's primacy: Roman historians tell the story as the moment alliance tipped toward empire.

Symbols & Iconography

The iconography associated with Diāna concentrates in a small set of recurring attributes, each a compressed statement about the name:

  • The bow — The hunt and the sudden death of women — her arrows strike gently
  • The crescent — The waxing moon she steers as Luna
  • The deer — Her sacred animal; the wild that cannot be owned
  • The oak of Nemi — The grove whose bough — the Golden Bough — only a runaway slave might break

Epithets & Cult Titles

Her Roman titles map her provinces.

  • Trivia — 'of the Three Ways'; the crossroads goddess, where moon, hunt, and underworld meet.
  • Lucina — 'she who brings to light'; the childbirth power she shares with Iūnō.
  • Nemorensis — 'of the Grove'; her Nemi title, the one Frazer made famous.
  • Opifera — 'bringer of aid'; her title as the plebeians' helper.
  • Luna — the moon itself; her celestial name in the poets.
  • Regina Nemorum — 'queen of the groves'; Horace's title for her.

Oracle Sites & Sanctuaries

Nemi — the sanctuary of Diana Nemorensis in the Alban Hills — was her most famous address: a temple-precinct with theatre and baths above the crater-lake called her Mirror, where the rex Nemorensis kept his murderous watch and where Caligula later floated his pleasure barges. On the Aventine in Rome, her federal temple anchored the Latin League and housed the league's bronze law-pillar, copied by Dionysius. Across Latium her sanctuaries marked the old federal roads — at Aricia, at Tusculum, on Mount Tifata — the network of a goddess older than Rome herself.

Archaeology & Evidence

The sanctuary at Nemi — Diana Nemorensis — preserves its theatre, temple platforms, and the famous votive anatomicals (terracotta wombs and limbs) of her healing cult; the lake itself, a volcanic crater, justified "speculum Dianae." On the Aventine, the federal temple's foundations underlie the modern street. The Nemi ships' remains — bronze heads of wolves and lions, the "Nemi wheels," and lead water pipes stamped CAESARIS — are divided between the Museo delle Navi Romane (partially reconstructed after the fire) and Berlin.

Across Cultures

Her fusion with Artemis was total and early, but Diāna kept Italian features Artemis never had: the slave-priest of Nemi, the Aventine's federal politics, and her patronage of the plebeians (her temple was a traditional asylum). In the Middle Ages she became the witch-goddess: canon law condemned women who claimed to ride at night "with Diana," and Charles Leland's Aradia (1899) rebuilt her as the messiah of witchcraft — the root of modern Wicca's Diana.

Within the Roman tradition, closely related names in the corpus include Iānus, Iūnō, Iūppiter, Neptūnus, Vulcānus, and Ártemis.

Cultural Legacy

She lends her name to the moon in a hundred poems and to Diana's Mirror at Nemi, where Caligula's pleasure barges — raised in 1929–32, burned in 1944 — gave underwater archaeology its founding drama. Frazer's Golden Bough, the book that created comparative religion as a public genre, begins and ends in her grove. The goddess of the wild remains the emblem of every conservation that is also a hunt.

A Meditation

Diāna is the wild that insists on a temple. Her priest wins office by violence in her most peaceful grove; her deer are protected by being hunted only by her. She asks the oldest question of stewardship: whether anything can be preserved without being owned — and answers with a lake that reflects the moon without holding it.

Her question to every managed wilderness is the lake's own: can you look without touching? The Actaeon myth is not about a peeping hunter but about the gaze that consumes what it sees — and the grove at Nemi, where the priest-king guarded the tree with a sword, admits the paradox outright: some things are protected only by being made dangerous to approach. The wild she keeps is the wild that keeps itself.

The restored name is live as a working domain: diāna.com, which the DNS carries in punycode form as xn--dina-rsa.com — an ASCII-compatible encoding that lets a non-ASCII name travel the global network without breaking older infrastructure. The visitor sees Diāna; the machines see the encoding. A Closer Look at the Marks

A restored name is a small map. In Diāna, the journey from the ASCII fallback diana to the Unicode form can be read letter by letter, and each letter tells a story. Of the 5 characters, 4 remain unchanged; they anchor the name to its modern shape. The remaining adjustments recover marks the source tradition used to distinguish this name from every other word built from the same letters.

The d stays as it is — Same letter.

The n stays as it is — Same letter.

Together these changes are not decoration. They are the minimum set of marks needed to keep the name legible in its own tradition. Remove them and you still have a string of letters; you no longer have the name as it was written.
