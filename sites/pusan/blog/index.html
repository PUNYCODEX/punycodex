How Pūṣan got its accent back

The ASCII form pusan is missing something. Pūṣan restores the marks the source language used to distinguish this name from a thousand others — and those marks change how the name is read, pronounced, and understood. This post explains, with the full scholarly record behind it, what each restored mark preserves: the Devanagari evidence, the reconstructed sound, the myths the name carries, and the classification logic that separates Tier 1 restorations from Tier 2. Overview

Pūṣan (pusan) — The Guide of All Roads — belongs to the Sanskrit tradition, where it is catalogued under the domain "Journeys, Nourishment, Roads". The name means "'The Nourisher' (from puṣ, to thrive), Vedic solar guide of roads".

Pūṣan is the Vedic herdsman-sun: guardian of cattle and roads, escort of brides to their weddings and souls to the fathers, the god who finds what is lost on the way. He carries a goad, rides a goat-drawn chariot, and eats gruel — because, the myth insists, he once lost his teeth.

PuniCodex restores the name as Pūṣan and serves its temple at pūṣan.com. The restoration preserves the long ū and the retroflex ṣ — distinctive features the ASCII form loses — which places the name in Tier 1. The plain ASCII form pusan survives as a modern convenience imposed by the early domain-name system; the restoration, not the fallback, is the form the project defends as philologically complete.

The Name

The name is attested in Devanagari as पूषन्. Etymologically it means "'The Nourisher' (from puṣ, to thrive), Vedic solar guide of roads".

The ASCII form pusan survives only because the early domain-name system could not carry diacritics; it is a technological compromise, not an ancient spelling. The Unicode restoration Pūṣan recovers the vowel length of the original directly in the address bar. The restoration preserves the long ū and the retroflex ṣ — distinctive features the ASCII form loses — which places the name in Tier 1.

The letter-by-letter transformation runs:

  • p → P — Same letter
  • u → ū — Macron marks the long vowel
  • s → ṣ — Script-specific diacritic
  • a → a — Same letter
  • n → n — Same letter

The project holds the domain pūṣan.com (xn--pan-50a4918a.com) as the canonical home of this name.

The Original Script

The name is preserved in Devanagari as पूषन्.

The scholarly transliteration is pūṣan.

The rendering proceeds step by step:

  • प = pa
  • ू = ū (long)
  • ष = ṣa
  • न् = na
  • pūṣan is the Vedic solar god who protects travelers and herds

Written पूषन् in Devanagari: pū with its long stroke, then ṣa with the retroflex dot, and the final n. The same root gives poṣa, "nourishment," and puṣṭi, "prosperity" — the god is thriving itself, shepherded into personhood.

Pronunciation

The reconstructed pronunciation of the name is /ˈpuː.ʂɐn/ — Sanskrit (IAST).

For the modern speaker, the closest approximation is: POO-shan, long ū on the first syllable.

Kindred and historical forms of the name:

  • Devanagari — पूषन्
  • Conventional — Pushan
  • Owned form — Pūṣan

Sanskrit pūṣan carries the long ū of the root puṣ, "to thrive, nourish," with the retroflex ṣ that English tongues must learn — written पूषन् with the dot beneath the sibilant. The restoration Pūṣan marks both: the length on the root, the retroflex on the close. The retroflex ṣ curls the tongue back — the sound romanization renders sh, though no English sh truly matches it.

Mythology

Pūṣan is the most companionable of Vedic gods: where others blaze, he accompanies. His myths are all journeys — of cattle, of brides, of the dead — and one memorable injury.

Guide of Souls (The Escort)

Ṛgveda 10.17 speaks to the dead with startling tenderness: "May Pūṣan take your hand and lead you; may the two Aśvins carry you in their chariot; go to the fathers, to the home that is yours." Pūṣan knows the road between the worlds because he drives it daily with the sun — the herdsman who loses no animal is the guide who loses no soul.

The Giving of Sūryā (The Wedding)

At the marriage of the Sun-maiden Sūryā — the Ṛgveda's great wedding hymn, still recited at Hindu weddings — Pūṣan is her kinsman-escort who takes her hand and gives her away. The hymn's phrases ("your hand for good fortune, Pūṣan takes") pass into the living rite: every Hindu bride is, liturgically, escorted by him still.

The Gruel of Dakṣa's Rite (The Teeth)

When Vīrabhadra wrecked Dakṣa's sacrifice, Pūṣan — eating the offering at the moment of ruin — had his teeth smashed. The Brāhmaṇas remember the wound in the liturgy: Pūṣan alone receives karambha, gruel, for he cannot chew. It is the Vedic taste for precise, comic piety: the god's injury written into the menu of his cult.

Seeker of Lost Cattle (The Finding)

The hymns beg him for the oldest pastoral miracle: "Pūṣan, bring back the strayed; Pūṣan, show us the road." He tracks by the sun's light, and the stolen, strayed, and hidden are his speciality — the Sarama legend of the rustled cosmic cattle belongs to his pastoral world, where finding is the sun's daily work.

Symbols & Iconography

The iconography associated with Pūṣan concentrates in a small set of recurring attributes, each a compressed statement about the name:

  • The goad — Guidance by encouragement — the herdsman's gentle compulsion
  • The goat chariot — His humble vehicle among the horse-gods — the commoner's deity
  • The gruel — His toothless fare — karambha, the offering only he receives

Archaeology & Evidence

An aniconic Vedic god has no statues, but his world has strata: the pastoral economy of the Ṛgvedic Punjab — cattle-counts, drove-roads, the archaeology of the Painted Grey Ware and earlier Harappan cattle iconography — is the social ground of his cult. The goat-drawn chariot of his hymns is remembered in later folk vehicles of Rajasthani pastoral gods like Pabuji, whose own oral epic of rustled and recovered cattle keeps Pūṣan's oldest story-pattern alive.

Realm & Domain

The Goad

His attribute: the herdsman's prod that keeps the herd — and the journey — moving.

The Road

Paths of earth and paths of heaven: he knows "all the roads," the hymns repeat.

The Psychopomp

Ṛgveda 10.17 entrusts the dead to him: he conducts souls to the fathers.

The Lost Teeth

At Dakṣa's sacrifice his teeth were struck out — the gruel-eating god of the ruined rite.

Across Cultures

Comparativists have long paired him with Greek Pan — the pastoral guardian with a likely cognate name (Pūṣan ~ Paon ~ Pan from a root of "watching over") — and with Hermês in function: herds, roads, and the escort of souls are Hermês' offices too. The equations are debated, not proven, and the tradition here reports them as scholarship, not scripture. Within the Veda he works in harness with Soma, Bhaga, and the Aśvins — the diplomat of the pantheon's second rank.

Within the Sanskrit tradition, closely related names in the corpus include Aṃśa, Dakṣa, Dhātṛ, Durgā, Gaṇeśa, and Kālī.

Cultural Legacy

His liturgy survives wherever the Ṛgveda's wedding hymn is sung — which is to say, at millions of Hindu weddings every year. Journeys are still opened with his sūkta by the observant, and the "lost and found" of Indian folk prayer descends from his finding-office. In the history of religions he is the type-case of the pastoral solar guide — the comparison with Pan is in every textbook, making his name one of the bridges on which comparative mythology was built.

A Meditation

Pūṣan is the god of the way back. Not the destination — the road; not the feast — the gruel; not the blaze of noon — the light by which a lost thing is found. He asks the traveller's question that is also the shepherd's and the mourner's: what have you lost on the road, and who walks beside you while you look?

And his offices have only multiplied: every search-and-rescue, every lost-and-found, every convoy and escort, every road-prayer murmured at a journey's start is his sūkta continued. The hymns call him the god of what is on the way — not the hearth but the road, not the feast but the drive home. The herdsman with the goad and the goat-chariot remains the patron of the least heroic and most necessary of human acts: going out, and coming back, and bringing everyone along.

The gruel-offering is the Veda's gentlest doctrine: even the gods bear their wounds into worship, and the liturgy feeds them accordingly. Pūṣan's toothless mouth did not end his service — it only changed his menu. So the herdsman-god teaches the road's last lesson, the one every convalescent and every scarred guide knows: keep moving, keep finding, and let the offering fit the mouth that remains.

A Closer Look at the Marks

A restored name is a small map. In Pūṣan, the journey from the ASCII fallback pusan to the Unicode form can be read letter by letter, and each letter tells a story. Of the 5 characters, 3 remain unchanged; they anchor the name to its modern shape. The remaining adjustments recover marks the source tradition used to distinguish this name from every other word built from the same letters.

The p stays as it is — Same letter.

The u becomes ū — Macron marks the long vowel.

The s becomes ṣ — Script-specific diacritic.

The a stays as it is — Same letter.

The n stays as it is — Same letter.

Together these changes are not decoration. They are the minimum set of marks needed to keep the name legible in its own tradition. Remove them and you still have a string of letters; you no longer have the name as it was written.
