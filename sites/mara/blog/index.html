Pronouncing Māra: a guide for the curious

Saying Māra aloud is harder than reading it on a screen, and more rewarding. The restored spelling is a compressed pronunciation guide: every accent and macron is an instruction. This post unpacks those instructions — the reconstructed sound, the phoneme-by-phoneme record, the kindred forms in neighboring languages — and then zooms out to the full record around the name: its Devanagari writing, its mythology, its cult, and its modern life as a Unicode domain. Whether you arrive as a linguist, a reader of myth, or a domainer, you will leave able to say the name the way the evidence suggests it was said — and able to type it the way it was written.

At a Glance

  • Restored name: Māra
  • ASCII form: mara
  • Meaning: "‘death, the tempter’, the personification of death and desire who assailed the Buddha under the Bodhi tree (from √mṛ ‘to die’), MW."
  • Domain of influence: Death, Temptation, Illusion
  • Pantheon: Buddhist
  • Classification: Tier 1
  • Original script: मार (Devanagari)
  • Live domain: māra.com

Overview

Māra (mara) — Death, Temptation, Illusion · The Tempter, Lord of Death — is Buddhism's personification of everything that keeps beings bound: death, desire, and distraction, the Evil One who assailed the future Buddha under the Bodhi tree and has never stopped working since.

The name comes from the root √mṛ, ‘to die’: māra, ‘death, the one who causes death, the killer’. The Pāli texts know him also as Namuci, ‘he who does not let go’, and as Pāpimā, ‘the Evil One’ — three names for one office. His great scene is the night of the awakening, when he rode against the seated sage with his armies and his daughters and lost; but his subtler work is the daily one — the whispered suggestion, the timely fear, the offer of an easier road — and the tradition insists he is at once a real devaputta of the highest sense-sphere heaven and a name for the mind's own resistance to waking.

PuniCodex restores the name as Māra and serves its temple at māra.com. The restoration keeps the long ā of the Sanskrit; the plain ASCII mara is a modern convenience of the early domain-name system, not an attested ancient spelling.

The Name

The name is attested in Sanskrit and Pāli as मार (IAST māra): from the root √mṛ, ‘to die’, the noun māra means ‘death, killing, the killer’ — personified, the god of death and the tempter. His bynames complete the dossier: Namuci, ‘the one who does not let go’ — the old asura name the Pāli texts give him, death as grip rather than blow; Pāpimā (Pāli; Sanskrit Pāpīyas), ‘the Evil One’, the tradition's most common address; and in Pāli also Kaṇha, ‘the Dark One’, pairing him against the Buddha as darkness against light.

The Buddhist analysis later multiplied the name into four Māras: the Māra of the defilements (kleśa), the Māra of the aggregates (skandha), the Māra who is death (mṛtyu), and the devaputta Māra, the deity of the Paranimmitavasavattin heaven who commands the sense-world's delights. The multiplication is the doctrine: most of what ‘Māra’ does, the texts concede, the mind does to itself.

The ASCII form mara survives only because the early domain-name system could not carry diacritics; it is a technological compromise, not an ancient spelling. The Unicode restoration Māra recovers the long ā directly in the address bar. Because the restoration preserves vowel length the ASCII form loses, the name is classified Tier 1.

The letter-by-letter transformation runs:

  • m → M — Same, capitalized
  • a → ā — Long vowel ā
  • r → r — Same
  • a → a — Same

The project holds the domain māra.com (xn--mra-1oa.com) as the canonical home of this name.

The Original Script

The name is preserved in Devanagari as मार — Brahmic abugida, attested Sanskrit and Pāli (via the Brāhmī-derived scripts), in the Buddhist manuscript traditions of India; the script is written left-to-right.

The scholarly transliteration is Māra (IAST), giving the normalized reading /ˈmaː.rə/.

The rendering proceeds step by step:

  • Sanskrit Māra is written मार in Devanagari: मा (mā) + र (ra), two open syllables; the vowel sign ा marks the long ā.
  • The word derives from √mṛ, ‘to die’: māra, ‘death, the killer’ — personified, the Buddhist Tempter.
  • IAST encodes vowel length with the macron (ā).
  • The Unicode restoration Māra is registrable in .com; the Devanagari form is not supported in the .com IDN table.

Pronunciation

The reconstructed pronunciation of the name is /ˈmaː.rə/ — Sanskrit Reconstruction.

Phoneme by phoneme:

  • Mā- — Bilabial [m] with long open [aː]; the root syllable of √mṛ, ‘to die’, stressed and held long
  • -ra — Trilled or tapped [r] with short [ə]

For the modern speaker, the closest approximation is: ‘MAA-ruh’ — the first vowel twice the length of the second.

Kindred and historical forms of the name:

  • Sanskrit / Pāli — मार / Māra, ‘death, the killer’; bynames Namuci, Pāpimā, Kaṇha
  • Sinhala — Māra; Thai — Māra, whence Māra in the idiom ‘defeat Māra’ for any great overcoming
  • Tibetan — bDud (Dü), the standard translation: ‘the Demon’

Māra is Tier 1 because the long ā is the name's very root: māra from mṛ, death itself, lengthened in the dying.

Mythology

Māra's mythology is the counter-narrative of the Buddha's whole career: wherever the Dharma advances, he is on the scene within a verse.

The Assault under the Tree (Padhāna Sutta; Lalitavistara 21)

As the future Śākyamuni sat immovable beneath the Bodhi tree, Māra came in person. The Padhāna Sutta (Sn 3.2) has him name his armies — desire first, then discontent, hunger and thirst, craving, sloth and torpor, fear, doubt, hypocrisy and obstinacy, vainglory, and self-exalting contempt — and the Buddha answer that he would rather die in battle than live defeated. The Sanskrit Lalitavistara expands the night into epic: Girimekhala the elephant, the grotesque host, the weapons falling as flowers, the daughters sent after the rout — and the earth itself called to witness, quaking its testimony until the army breaks.

The Daughters (SN 4.25)

Force having failed, Māra sent Taṇhā, Aratī, and Ragā — Craving, Discontent, Passion. The Buddha saw them as they were, and they withdrew as a crow withdraws from a stone it has pecked.

The Persistent Tempter (Mārasaṃyutta; Bhikkhunīsaṃyutta)

The canon refuses to retire him. For forty-five years Māra returns: to the Buddha, suggesting that the Dharma is too deep to teach; to the nuns, taunting that women cannot awaken (Sister Somā's reply is one of the canon's great retorts); to the monks, as a snake in the grass, a voice in the ear, a sudden doubt. He is, in Ling's phrase, the mythology of evil domesticated into a working psychology.

The Last Invitation (DN 16)

His final recorded success is the gentlest: at the Mahāparinibbāna he comes not with armies but with a courteous request — the Tathāgata has done his work; let him pass away now. The Buddha agrees, setting the date of the parinirvāṇa. The Tempter's last temptation is the one even the Buddha grants: everything compounded decays — and Māra, who is death, is owed his due.

The Ten Armies (Padhāna Sutta (Sn 3.2))

The Sutta Nipāta's Padhāna Sutta gives his order of battle, and it is a psychology textbook in disguise: sensual pleasure is his first army, discontent the second, hunger and thirst the third, craving the fourth, sloth and torpor the fifth, fear the sixth, doubt the seventh, hypocrisy and obstinacy the eighth, gains, fame, and honor the ninth, and the tenth is self-exaltation joined with contempt for others. The Buddha's answer is the template of every later victory: he sees each army for what it is. The tradition's whole art of temptation is this catalog — and its whole defense is recognition.

The Night Under the Tree (Nidānakathā / Buddhacarita)

The full assault is told in the Nidānakathā and versified in Aśvaghoṣa's Buddhacarita, whose canto on the Defeat of Māra is Sanskrit poetry's great set-piece: whirlwinds that cannot stir his robe, a rain of rocks that falls as flowers, darkness that will not spread. Mounted on the elephant Girimekhala, Māra advances his final claim — the seat of awakening is his — and calls his army as witness. The bodhisattva answers with the gesture that defines Buddhist art: he touches the earth, and the earth itself thunders witness to his perfections. The elephant kneels; the army scatters; the dawn comes up on an undefeated man.

The Last Visit (Mahāparinibbāna Sutta (DN 16))

Māra keeps one appointment to the end. In the Mahāparinibbāna Sutta he comes to the aged Buddha and, with perfect courtesy, reminds him of his ancient promise: that he would not pass away until the four assemblies — monks, nuns, laymen, laywomen — were well established. That work is done now; it is time for the Blessed One's parinibbāna. And the Buddha, who once broke his army by seeing him, grants the request without resistance: in three months the Tathāgata will pass away. The tradition's point is exact — the tempter's last success is only the world's impermanence keeping its schedule.

Symbols & Iconography

The iconography of Māra is the negative image of the Buddha's calm:

  • The elephant Girimekhala — His war-mount at the Bodhi tree, the beast of the world's bulk thrown against one seated man
  • The demon army — The grotesque host of Gandhāran reliefs and Thai murals: weapons that turn to flowers in mid-air over the Buddha's head
  • The daughters — Taṇhā, Aratī, Ragā: Craving, Discontent, Passion, his three envoys of charm after force fails
  • The bow and flower-arrows — Later art arms him as a dark inversion of the love-god: arrows that are blossoms, killing by attraction
  • The dark body — Kaṇha, ‘the Dark One’: the iconographic shadow against the Buddha's gold

Archaeology & Evidence

Māra's monuments are the assault scenes of Buddhist art. The Gandhāran schist reliefs (1st–3rd c. CE) established the composition: the serene seated Buddha at the centre, the grotesque host collapsing around him, weapons arrested mid-flight — the visual charter of every later Māravijaya (‘Victory over Māra’) scene.

The type then became Southeast Asia's signature: the great Māravijaya images of Bagan, Sukhothai, and the Thai and Burmese temple traditions, where the earth-touching Buddha — often with the earth-goddess Vasundharā wringing the flood of merit from her hair to drown the demon host — anchors the whole iconographic program. The armies of the murals, half nightmare and half carnival, are among the masterpieces of narrative painting in Asia.

Realm & Domain

Māra is the lord of everything that binds: death first, but also desire, fear, and the ten thousand distractions by which a being talks itself out of waking.

Death

His oldest office: mṛtyu-māra, the mortality that ends every project — the king of the sense-sphere because everything in it dies.

Temptation

Kāma-māra: desire as strategy — the daughters Taṇhā (Craving), Aratī (Discontent), and Ragā (Passion) sent to do what armies could not.

The Armies of the Mind

His ten armies in the Padhāna Sutta are psychological states: sense-pleasure, discontent, hunger and thirst, craving, sloth and torpor, fear, doubt, hypocrisy and obstinacy, self-seeking glory, and self-exalting contempt for others.

The Household of Illusion

As devaputta of the Paranimmitavasavattin heaven he ‘controls what others create’ — the deity of a world where everything delightful is someone else's work, and nothing is free.

Across Cultures

Māra is the closest figure Buddhism has to the Devil, and the comparison has been drawn since the first Western studies — but the tradition itself resists the equation: Māra creates no beings and damns no one, is subject to kamma like every other being, and in some Mahāyāna texts is himself converted and destined for Buddhahood. T. O. Ling's classic study maps the difference precisely: not a principle of evil opposed to God, but the personification of the forces within saṃsāra that resist awakening — a tempter inside the system, not a rebel against it.

The tantric tradition absorbed him into the protector cults (the subjugation of Māra is a standard maṇḍala rite), Tibetan tradition translates his name as bDud, ‘the demon’, and the comparative literature pairs his fourfold analysis — kleśa, skandha, mṛtyu, devaputta — with every psychology that distinguishes the enemy without from the enemy within. Kindred figures in the PuniCodex index include Śākyamuni (the one he could not move) and Thánatos (Greek death, his nearest structural cousin).

Cultural Legacy

Māra's deepest legacy is therapeutic: the Buddhist analysis of temptation — the ten armies as states of mind — is a working psychology of distraction that modern meditation teaching uses daily, and ‘Māra’ has become the standard shorthand, in Dharma halls worldwide, for whatever shows up to pull the mind off the cushion. The Dhammapada's victory verses — ‘seeking the builder of this house I wandered through countless births... now you are seen, O builder; you shall not build again’ — remain the most quoted lines in the Pāli canon, the winner's speech over the Tempter.

His image built the iconography of the Buddha's greatest moment: the bhūmisparśa earth-touching gesture — the right hand down, calling the earth to witness against Māra — is the single most reproduced pose of the Buddha in Southeast Asia, the Tempter's defeat fixed as the teacher's emblem.

A Meditation

Māra is the most psychologically honest figure in Buddhist mythology. The canon never lets him be merely a monster: his armies are moods, his daughters are cravings, his weapons are the ones the mind uses on itself. The night under the tree is everyone's night before any real change — the sudden appetite, the timely fear, the reasonable voice suggesting tomorrow.

What defeats him is not force but recognition. The Buddha does not fight the armies; he names them, touches the earth, and stays seated. The tradition's whole counsel on temptation is in that gesture: you do not have to win the argument with the voice — you have to know whose voice it is. Namuci, they called him: the one who does not let go. The answer to a grip is not a blow. It is not needing to pull free.

The Unicode Restoration

Māra is classified as Tier 1: the restoration preserves at least one distinctive feature — a diacritic or a distinctive letter — that the ASCII form loses. The ASCII fallback mara still resolves everywhere, but it is the restored form that carries the name's full information. Across the 4 characters of the name, the restoration adjusts 1: 1 mark of length (ā). That is the whole thesis of this temple: the marks are the message.

The Domain Name

The restored name is live as a working domain: māra.com, which the DNS carries in punycode form as xn--mra-1oa.com — an ASCII-compatible encoding that lets a non-ASCII name travel the global network without breaking older infrastructure. The visitor sees Māra; the machines see the encoding. That duality is the engineering compromise on which the entire restoration rests, and it is why a name written the way its own tradition wrote it in Devanagari can now be typed into any browser on earth.

Why This Restoration Matters

Pronunciation turns out to be the heart of the matter. The marks in Māra are instructions for the voice, and a web that strips them is a web that mispronounces the past at scale. The restoration hands the instructions back: say it as the evidence suggests, type it as the tradition wrote it, and let the punycode machinery do the quiet translation in between.
