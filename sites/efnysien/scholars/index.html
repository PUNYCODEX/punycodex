01

Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Efnysien (efnysien) ​— Trouble, Mischief ‌· Double enemy ‌— is ​the destroyer of ​the Second Branch ​of the Mabinogi: ​the half-brother of ​Brân the Blessed, ‌Branwen, and ​Manawydan, whose one ​unprovoked act — ‌the maiming of ​King Matholwch's horses ‌at the wedding ​feast of ‌Aberffraw — sets ​Britain and Ireland ​on the road ​to a war ​that leaves Ireland ​wasted and ‌only seven men ​of Brân's host ‌alive.[1]

The tale ​introduces him in ‌a single ​balanced sentence it ‌never takes back. ​Penarddun ferch Beli ‌bore two sons ​to Euroswydd: Nisien, ​a good ‌youth who could ‌make peace between ‌two armies at ​their most embittered, ​and Efnysien, who ‌would set ​two brothers at ​strife when they ‌loved each other ​most.[1] His dossier ​is one branch ‌of one ​book — the ‌maimed horses, the ​hundred men crushed ​in their flour-bags, ​the child Gwern ​thrown into ‌the fire, and ‌at the end ​the Cauldron of ‌Rebirth burst from ​within at the ​price of ‌his own heart ‌— and it ‌is among the ‌most unflinching portraits ​of malice and ‌its cost ​in medieval literature.[2]

​PuniCodex restores the ​name as Efnysien ‌— identical to ​the ASCII ​input, because Middle ​Welsh writes the ‌name in plain ​Latin letters with ​no diacritic for ​the restoration ‌to recover; the ​name is Tier ‌2 by the ​mechanical rule. The ‌plain-ASCII efnysien.com is ​held by ‌a third party ‌outside the PuniCodex ​collection and is ​watched for release; ​no form of ​the name ‌is currently owned, ‌and this temple ​claims none.[3]

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

The name ​is attested in ‌Middle Welsh as ‌Efnisien (the modern ​standard spelling; the ​temple's form Efnysien ​follows the same ​name with the ​common y-spelling), son ‌of Euroswydd and ​Penarddun ferch Beli, ​in the ‌Second Branch of ​the Mabinogi.[1] Its ‌etymology is debated ​rather than settled, ‌and honest scholarship ​labels it accordingly. ​What is secure ​is the literary ​construction: the branch ​pairs Efnisien with ‌his brother Nisien ​as a named ‌opposition — the ​peace-maker and the ‌strife-maker — and ​the handbooks read ‌the pair as ​an embodiment of ‌contraries, with Efnisien's ​name traditionally glossed ​on the 'enemy, ‌hostile' side ‌of the contrast. ‌The lexicon's gloss ​'Double enemy' follows ​MacKillop's rendering of ‌the name.[2]

The ​ASCII form is ​not a compromise ‌here: Middle Welsh ​writes the name ​in plain Latin ‌letters, and normalized ​Welsh orthography attaches ‌no diacritic to ​it. There is ​nothing distinctive for ​the ASCII alphabet ​to lose; only ‌the capital distinguishes ‌the restored form ​from the input, ‌which places the ​name in ​Tier 2 by ‌the mechanical rule.

‌The letter-by-letter transformation ‌runs:

  • e ‌→ E — ​Same, capitalized
  • ‌f → f ​— Same
  • ​n → n ​— Same
  • ‌y → y ​— Same
  • ​s → s ​— Same
  • ‌i → i ​— Same
  • ​e → e ​— Same
  • ‌n → n ​— Same

The ‌project holds no ​domain for ‌this name: efnysien.com ​is registered to ‌a third party ‌outside the PuniCodex ​collection and is ​watched for release. ​The temple is ​served at /efnysien/ ‌and claims no ‌domain it does ​not hold.

03

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The pronunciation ​of the name ‌in Modern ‌Welsh is /ɛvˈnɪs.jɛn/ ​— Modern Welsh; ​the Middle Welsh ​value is held ​to be ​substantially the same.[1]

‌Phoneme by phoneme:

​- E- — ​Short open-mid front ‌[ɛ], as ​in English 'elf'; ‌light and unstressed
​- -f- — ‌Voiced labiodental [v]: ​Welsh writes ​the v-sound with ​a single f ​(the digraph ff ​writes the f-sound) ‌— the ​name's first trap ‌for English readers
​- -nis- — ‌Alveolar nasal [n], ​short [ɪ] ‌— the value ​Welsh y carries ‌in a non-final ​syllable — and ​[s]; the ‌stressed syllable, since ‌Welsh stresses the ‌penult

  • -ien ​— Palatal glide ​[j] with ‌short open [ɛ], ​closing on [n]; ​the final syllable ‌falls away unstressed

​For the ​modern speaker, the ‌closest approximation is: ​'ev-NIS-yen' — the ‌f sounded ​as v, the ​weight on the ​middle syllable, the ​ending quick and ‌light.

Kindred ‌and historical forms ​of the name:

‌- Middle Welsh ​— Efnisien, son ​of Euroswydd ‌and Penarddun, in ‌the White Book ‌of Rhydderch and ‌the Red Book ​of Hergest
‌- Modern Welsh ​— Efnisien, the ​standard modern spelling
​- The named ‌opposite — ​Nisien, his brother: ​the tale's peace-maker ​set against its ‌strife-maker

  • English ​translations — ​Efnisien (Guest), Efnysien ​— the same ‌name carried into ​English prose

Efnysien ‌is Tier ​2 by the ‌mechanical rule: the ​Middle Welsh form ‌is already plain ‌ASCII, and ​the restoration preserves ​nothing the ASCII ​form loses. The ​weight of the ‌name is ‌semantic and narrative, ​not orthographic.

Sources

  1. Morris-Jones, John, A Welsh Grammar, Historical and Comparative (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1913), phonology and orthography.
  2. Geiriadur Prifysgol Cymru / A Dictionary of the Welsh Language (University of Wales Press), s.v. Efnisien.

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

The name ​is preserved ‌in Middle ‌Welsh manuscripts ​in the ​Latin alphabet ​as Efnisien — ​Middle Welsh ​(Brythonic Celtic), ‌attested in ​the White ​Book of ‌Rhydderch (c. ​1350) and the ‌Red Book ​of Hergest ‌(c. 1382–1410), ​recording material ​composed centuries ​earlier.[1] The script ​is written ​left-to-right in ‌the medieval ​Welsh bookhand.

‌The scholarly ​rendering proceeds step ‌by step:

​- Middle ‌Welsh writes ​the name ‌in plain ​Latin letters; the ​manuscript spelling ‌Efnisien and ‌the temple's ‌form Efnysien ​carry the ​same name ‌with the common ​i/y spelling ​variation of ‌Welsh orthography.
​- No ​diacritic attaches ‌to the name ​in any ‌witness: normalized ​Middle Welsh ​orthography and ​the ASCII ​spelling are identical.
‌- Welsh ‌f writes ​the voiced ‌sound /v/ ​(the digraph ​ff writes ‌/f/), and Welsh ‌y in ‌a non-final ‌syllable writes ​/ɪ/ — ‌values the ​letters carry in ​Welsh, not ​marks the ‌restoration must ​recover.[2]

  • ​The Unicode ​restoration Efnysien therefore ‌changes nothing ​but the ​capital; the ​name stands ‌in Tier ​2 by the ‌mechanical rule, ​and the ‌plain-ASCII domain ​efnysien.com — ‌held by ‌a third ​party outside the ​PuniCodex collection ​and watched ​for release ‌— is ‌the philologically ​complete form.

Sources

  1. Williams, Ifor, ed., Pedeir Keinc y Mabinogi (University of Wales Press, 1930); Davies, trans., The Mabinogion (Oxford University Press, 2007).
  2. Morris-Jones, John, A Welsh Grammar, Historical and Comparative (Clarendon Press, 1913); Geiriadur Prifysgol Cymru, s.v. Efnisien.

Domains & Attributes

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sphere of influence, titles, epithets, domain cards.

Efnysien is ​the Mabinogi's embodiment ‌of trouble and ‌mischief — but ​the branch's words are ​sharper than the ​categories: he is ​the man who ​would set two ‌loving brothers at ​strife, and the ​tale spends its ‌whole length proving the ​sentence.[1]

Strife-Making

‌His defining act ​needs no army ‌and no grievance: ​insulted that his ​half-sister Branwen was ​given to Matholwch ​of Ireland without his ​consent, he maims ‌the Irish king's ​horses in their ‌stable at Aberffraw ​— lips to ‌the teeth, ears ​to the head, ‌tails to the rumps, ​eyelids to the ‌bone. The honour-price ​Brân pays cannot ​bury the insult, ‌and the branch's ‌whole tragedy unfolds ‌from that one ​night.[1]

The Sight ​That Sees Traps

‌In Ireland, it ​is Efnysien alone ​who reads the ‌great house correctly: ​a hundred flour-bags ​on the pillars, ‌an armed man in ​each. He walks ‌the row and ​crushes every skull ​through the sacking ​with his fingers ​— treachery undone ‌by the one ‌mind that thinks in ​treachery.[1]

The ‌Unforgivable Act

At ​the peace feast, ​when the child ‌Gwern — Branwen's ‌son, newly invested ‌as king — ‌goes from lap to ​lap among his ‌kinsmen, Efnysien seizes ​him and throws ​him into the ​fire. After Gwern, ‌the tale permits ​no reconciliation: only ​war.[1]

Destruction Turned ​on Destruction

His ‌last act is ​the only constructive ​one of his ​life: thrown alive ‌into the Cauldron ​of Rebirth among ‌the Irish dead, he ​stretches himself until ‌the vessel bursts ​into four pieces ‌and his own ‌heart breaks. The ​destroyer dies destroying ​the engine of ​endless war — the ​branch offers the ‌act without eulogy, ‌and without calling ​it redemption.[2]

Sources

  1. The Mabinogi, Second Branch (Branwen ferch Llŷr), trans. Sioned Davies (Oxford University Press, 2007).
  2. Mac Cana, Proinsias, Branwen Daughter of Llŷr (University of Wales Press, 1958), on the cauldron-episode and the composition of the branch.

Symbols

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Iconography, attributes, and their meanings.

The iconography ​of Efnysien ‌is narrative ‌rather than ​cultic — medieval ​Welsh prose ​preserves no ​regalia for him, ​but the ‌Second Branch ​fixed a ​small set of ‌objects and ​images no ‌retelling can ​omit:[1]

  • The ‌maimed horses ​— The ​Irish king's horses ​with their ​lips, ears, ​tails, and ‌eyelids cut away: ​pride made ‌visible, the ​insult no ‌payment could erase

​- The ‌Cauldron of ​Rebirth (Pair Dadeni) ‌— The ​vessel Brân ​gave in ‌compensation, which returns ‌the slain ‌to life ​as speechless ​fighting men: war ‌stripped of ​death's limit, ​until the man ‌who breaks ​it breaks ​himself

  • ‌The hundred flour-bags ​— Treachery ‌hung on ​the pillars ​of the great ​house built ​for Brân, ‌read and crushed ‌by the ​only guest ‌whose mind ​ran that way

​- The ‌fire and ‌the child ‌— Gwern cast ‌into the ​flames from ‌his uncle's lap: ​the point ​past which ​the tale ‌permits no return
​- The ​broken heart ​— His ‌only wound: he ​stretched himself ​inside the ​cauldron until it ‌burst, and ​his heart ‌burst with ​it

  • The ‌four-sided grave ​— Not ‌his but ‌Branwen's, dug on ​the bank ​of the ​Alaw for the ​sister whose ‌fate his ‌acts set ​in motion[2]

Sources

  2. Lynch, Frances, 'Bedd Branwen, Anglesey', on the excavation of the cairn by the Afon Alaw traditionally identified with Branwen's grave.
07

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

Efnysien's mythology is one tale ​— the Second Branch of the Mabinogi, Branwen ferch ‌Llŷr — and within it he is the engine ‌of both the catastrophe and its ending.[1]

The ​Two Brothers (Second Branch)

The branch introduces him ​through his mother. Penarddun ferch Beli, daughter of Beli ​Mawr, bore to Llŷr the three great children — ​Brân the Blessed (Bendigeidfran), Branwen, and Manawydan — and ​to Euroswydd two more sons: Nisien and Efnysien. Nisien, ‌the text says, could make peace between two armies ​at their most embittered; Efnysien would set two brothers ​at strife when they loved each other best. The ‌whole branch is the working-out of that sentence.[1]

​### The Maiming of the Horses (Second Branch)

When ‌Brân, the crowned king of Britain and a giant ​no house or ship could hold, gave Branwen to ‌Matholwch, king of Ireland, the wedding feast was held ​at Aberffraw and the Irish horses stabled for the ​night. Efnysien asked whose they were, and was told ​they belonged to the man who had married his ​sister. 'Is this what they have done with ​a maiden as fine as her — given her ‌away without asking me?' He went under the horses ​and cut off their lips to the teeth, their ‌ears to the head, their tails to the rumps, ​and their eyelids to the bone. Matholwch rose in ‌silence and made for his ships; Brân's embassy, Manawydan ​among it, won him back with honour-price: a silver ‌staff as tall as himself, a plate of ​gold as broad as his face, fresh horses, and ‌the Cauldron of Rebirth, which had come to Brân ​from Llasar Llaes Gyfnewid and Cymidei Cymeinfoll, wanderers fled ​out of Ireland.[1]

The Starling and the Crossing ‌(Second Branch)

The compensation did not hold. In Ireland ‌the injury was remembered and Matholwch was shamed by ‌his own nobles; Branwen was sent to the kitchen, ​and the butcher boxed her ear every day. ​She reared a starling, taught it speech, tied a ‌letter under its wing, and sent it to Brân. ​He mustered Britain and waded the sea, for no ​ship could carry him; when the Irish destroyed the ‌bridge over the river, he lay down across it ​himself — 'He who would be a leader, let ​him be a bridge.'[1]

The Hundred Bags (Second ‌Branch)

The Irish sued for terms: the kingship ​would pass to Gwern, Branwen's son, and they would ‌build Brân a house great enough to hold him ​— the first house he had ever entered. But ​in that house they hung a hundred bags on ​the pillars, an armed man in each. Efnysien came ​in ahead of the company, laid his hand on ‌each bag — 'What is in here?' 'Flour.' — ‌and squeezed each head through the sacking until ​the skull gave, a hundred times down the hall.[1]

‌### The Boy in the Fire and the Breaking ​of the Cauldron (Second Branch)

At the feast the ​boy Gwern was invested as king and went, charming ‌everyone, from lap to lap — Brân, Manawydan, Nisien ‌— until he came to Efnysien's lap. 'Why does ‌my nephew not come to me?' Efnysien asked; and ‌when the boy was given to him he ​seized him by the feet and threw him headlong ‌into the fire. Branwen would have leapt in after ​him, but Brân caught her with one hand and ​raised his shield with the other, and the hall ​rose in war. In the battle the Irish could ‌not be beaten: each night they cast their slain ​into the cauldron, and each morning the dead rose ​as fighting men, speechless but whole. Then Efnysien, ​seeing the ruin he had made, lay down among ‌the Irish dead as a corpse, was thrown into ​the cauldron with them, and stretched himself out until ​the vessel burst into four pieces — and his ​heart burst with it, and so he died.[1]

‌The Aftermath (Second Branch)

The branch closes in grief, ​not triumph. Ireland was wasted; of Brân's host only ‌seven men came home — Pryderi, Manawydan, Glifieu, ​Taliesin, Ynawg, Gruddieu, and Heilyn — bearing Brân's head, ‌for the king had taken a poisoned spear in ​the foot and commanded his own beheading. Branwen, looking ‌on the two wasted islands, died of a broken ‌heart at Aber Alaw in Anglesey and was buried ​in a four-sided grave on the riverbank. The head ​went with the seven to Harlech and to Gwales ​and at last to the White Mount in ​London, buried facing France as a ward against invasion ‌— where it stayed, the tradition says, until Arthur ‌dug it up, declaring he would defend the island ​by his own strength alone.[2]

Sources

  1. The Mabinogi, Second Branch (Branwen ferch Llŷr), trans. Sioned Davies (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2007); Welsh text ed. Ifor Williams, Pedeir Keinc y Mabinogi (Cardiff: University of Wales Press, 1930).
  2. Mac Cana, Proinsias, Branwen Daughter of Llŷr: A Study of the Irish Affinities and of the Composition of the Second Branch of the Mabinogi (Cardiff: University of Wales Press, 1958).

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

The comparison ​that follows ‌Efnysien everywhere ‌is with ​Loki: the ​malice inside ​the kin-group, ​the sworn ​relative whose ‌mischief costs ​the community ​its peace, the ‌trickster the ​tale can ‌neither spare ​nor save. ‌MacKillop registers ​the parallel ​as typological ​rather than ​genealogical — ​the Mabinogi's ‌strife-maker is a ​prince of ‌the Children ​of Llŷr, ‌not a ​god, and ‌his story ​answers to ‌Welsh legal ​and social ​concerns: the ‌honour-price, the insult ‌remembered, the ‌compensation that ​fails.[1]

The ​truer Celtic ‌company is ​the cauldron-complex. ​The Cauldron ‌of Rebirth ​belongs to ​the same ‌deep stock as ​the cauldron ‌of the ​Dagda from ​Murias in ​the Lebor ​Gabála Érenn, ‌from which ‌no company ​went away ‌unsatisfied, and ​the Second Branch ​itself gives ‌the vessel ‌an Irish ‌provenance through ‌the fugitive ​Llasar Llaes ‌Gyfnewid — ​Mac Cana's ​study of ​the branch's ‌Irish affinities makes ​the cauldron ​and the ​whole Irish ‌war central ​to its ​composition.[2] Within ​the corpus ‌his closest ​kin are ‌Nisien, the ​brother he was ‌written as ​the opposite ‌of; Matholwch, ‌the king ​his pride ​destroyed; and ​Manawydan, the ​brother who ‌survived to ‌carry the ​head home.

Sources

  2. Mac Cana, Proinsias, Branwen Daughter of Llŷr (University of Wales Press, 1958); Mac Cana, Proinsias, The Mabinogi, 2nd ed. (University of Wales Press, 1992).

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

Lady ​Charlotte Guest's ‌translation of ‌the Mabinogion ​(1838–49) carried ​the Second ​Branch into ​the English-speaking ​world, and ‌Efnysien's maiming ​of the ​horses and ‌death in ​the cauldron ‌became set-pieces ​of ‌the Victorian ​Celtic revival.[1] ​Evangeline Walton ​retold the ​branch at ​full length ‌in The ​Children of ‌Llyr (1971), ​the second ‌volume of ​her Mabinogion ‌tetralogy, giving ​the ‌destroyer a ​modern novel's ​interior view ‌without softening ‌him.[2]

His ‌widest-reaching legacy ​is the ​cauldron itself: ‌Lloyd Alexander ​drew the ​deathless cauldron-born ‌warriors of ​his Prydain ​chronicles — ‌above ​all The ‌Black Cauldron ​(1965), filmed ​by Disney ​in 1985 ​— directly ‌from the ‌Welsh matter, ​in which ‌a vessel ​that returns ​the slain ‌to silent ‌service can ‌be ‌stopped only ​from within, ‌at terrible ​cost.[3] In ​Welsh memory ​the branch ‌left two ​landmarks: the ​proverb 'A ​fo ben, ‌bid bont' ​— he ​who would ​be ‌a leader, ​let him ‌be a ​bridge — ‌and Bedd ​Branwen on ‌the bank ‌of the ​Alaw, the ​grave the ​tradition dug ​for the ‌sister his ‌acts helped ​kill.

Sources

  1. The Mabinogion, trans. Lady Charlotte Guest, 3 vols. (London, 1838–49).
  2. Walton, Evangeline, The Children of Llyr (New York: Ballantine, 1971).
  3. Alexander, Lloyd, The Black Cauldron (New York: Holt, 1965), and the Prydain chronicles' debt to the Mabinogion.

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

No cult ​site, votive ‌deposit, or ‌inscription of ​Efnysien is ​known; his ​attestation is ​entirely literary.[1] ​His evidence ‌is manuscript ​evidence: the ​Second Branch ‌survives complete ​in the ‌White Book ​of Rhydderch ‌(Aberystwyth, National ​Library of ​Wales, Peniarth ​MSS 4–5, ​c. 1350) ​and the ‌Red Book ​of Hergest ‌(Oxford, Jesus ​College MS ‌111, c. ​1382–1410), with ‌earlier fragments ​in Peniarth ‌6; the ​tales' composition ​is argued ‌rather than ‌documented, most ‌often placed ​in the ​later eleventh ‌or early ​twelfth century.[2]

​The geography ‌of his ​story is ​real: Aberffraw, ‌the royal ​court of ‌Gwynedd where ​the horses ​were maimed; ​Harlech, where ​Brân's court ‌sat; and ‌the Anglesey ​shore where ‌the survivors ​landed. The ​tradition's one ‌material anchor ‌is Bedd ‌Branwen, a ‌cairn on ​the bank ‌of the ​Afon Alaw ​identified since ​the Middle ‌Ages with ​Branwen's 'four-sided ​grave'; excavated ​by Frances ‌Lynch in ​1967–68, it ​proved to ​hold Early ‌Bronze Age ​cremations — ‌far older ​than any ‌Mabinogi event. ​The identification ‌is the ‌folklore, not ​the archaeology, ​and the ​record is ​the more ‌valuable for ‌keeping the ​two distinct.[3]

Sources

  2. Davies, Sioned, trans., The Mabinogion (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2007), introduction, on manuscripts and dating.
  3. Lynch, Frances, report on the excavation of Bedd Branwen, Anglesey (1967–68); cf. Koch, Celtic Culture (2006), s.v. Branwen.

Irish Mythological Cycles

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The Second ​Branch of the ‌Mabinogi is the Welsh ‌corpus's Irish-Sea branch, ​and Efnysien is ​the man who ​detonates it. Matholwch, ​king of Ireland, is ​the corpus's standing ‌Irish king: he ​crosses the sea ​to Harlech to ‌ask for Branwen, the ​wedding is held ‌at Aberffraw, and ​Efnysien's maiming of ‌the Irish horses turns ​a marriage alliance ​into an insult ​the honour-price cannot ​bury. In Ireland ​the grievance festers — ‌Branwen is consigned ​to the kitchen ‌and the butcher ​boxes her ear daily ‌— until the ​starling's letter brings ‌Brân's host across ​the Irish Sea, ‌the giant wading beside ​his ships, and ​the war leaves ‌Ireland wasted and ‌only seven men ‌of Britain alive.[1]

The ​branch's Irish provenance ​runs deeper than ‌its plot. The ​Cauldron of Rebirth — ​Pair Dadeni — ‌comes into the ​story already Irish: ​Brân received it ‌from Llasar Llaes Gyfnewid ​and Cymidei Cymeinfoll, ‌fugitives who had ​fled out of ​Ireland, and it ​returns to Ireland as ​compensation, there to ‌raise the slain ‌as speechless fighting ​men until Efnysien breaks ‌it from within. ​Mac Cana's study ​of the branch's ‌Irish affinities made ‌the cauldron-complex and the ‌Irish war central ‌to the Second ​Branch's composition.[2]

Beneath ‌the narrative lies ​a recognizable Irish-Sea political ​economy: marriage alliance, ​honour-price (saraad), the ‌shaming of a ​king by his own ​nobles, hostages exacted ​and the investiture ‌of the child ​Gwern as king ​— a world of ​cross-channel kingship and ‌tribute that the ​branch takes for ‌granted and Efnysien's pride ​ignites. Sims-Williams's study ‌of Irish influence ​on medieval Welsh ‌literature places the ‌Second Branch's Irish matter ​in precisely this ​historical frame of ​contact across the ​sea, and the ‌modern handbooks treat it ‌as the branch's ​defining context.[3][4]

Welsh Sources

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Efnysien's whole ​dossier is ‌the Second Branch ‌of the Mabinogi ​— Branwen ​ferch Llŷr, 'Branwen ​daughter of ​Llŷr' — and ​he appears nowhere ‌else in ​the Four Branches ​except as ‌the son of ​Penarddun and ‌Euroswydd named in ​its kinship lists. ‌Within the ​branch he owns ​four scenes: ​the maiming of ​the horses at ​Aberffraw, the ‌crushing of the ​hundred men ‌in the flour-bags, ​the throwing of ‌Gwern into ​the fire, and ‌the breaking ​of the Cauldron ‌of Rebirth in ​which he ​dies.[1] The neighbouring ‌branches remember ‌his family without ‌him: the ​Third Branch, Manawydan ​fab Llŷr, follows ‌his brother ​Manawydan home from ​the Irish ‌war to the ​enchantment of Dyfed ​and the ‌courtship of Rhiannon, ​and the ‌First Branch's Pryderi ​is among the ​seven survivors ​who carried Brân's ​head.[2]

The ‌tales survive complete ‌in two manuscripts ​— the ‌White Book of ​Rhydderch (c. ​1350) and the ‌Red Book ‌of Hergest (c. ‌1382–1410), with earlier ‌fragments in ​Peniarth 6 — ‌and their ​date of composition ​is argued rather ​than documented, ‌most often placed ​around 1060–1120; ​their author is ​unknown.[3] Lady Charlotte ‌Guest's translation ​(1838–49) first carried ​the Second ​Branch into English; ‌Ifor Williams's edition ​(1930) fixed ‌the standard Welsh ​text; Sioned ‌Davies's translation (2007) ​is the ‌current scholarly English ‌version. Mac Cana's ​Branwen Daughter ​of Llŷr (1958) ​remains the ​foundational study of ‌the branch's composition ‌and its ​Irish affinities.[4]

  2. The Mabinogi, First and Third Branches, trans. Davies (2007).
  3. Davies, The Mabinogion (2007), introduction, on date and manuscripts; Williams, Ifor, ed., Pedeir Keinc y Mabinogi (University of Wales Press, 1930).
  4. Mac Cana, Proinsias, Branwen Daughter of Llŷr (University of Wales Press, 1958).

Inscriptions & Seals

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

No ​inscription names ‌Efnysien. The ‌early inscribed ​stones of ​Wales — ​the ​Latin and ​ogham memorial ‌stones of ​roughly the ​fifth to ‌seventh ​centuries — ‌record personal ​names in ‌funerary formulae, ​not literary ​theonyms, ​and the ​theonymic record ​of pre-Christian ‌Wales is ​known ‌from literature ​and place-lore ‌rather than ​stone.[1]

The ‌epigraphic silence ​is ‌unsurprising and ​instructive in ​equal measure: ‌Efnysien is ‌a human ‌prince ​within the ​tale, not ‌a cult ​figure, and ​no votive ‌practice ​ever attached ​to him. ‌What the ​stone record ‌does preserve ​is ​the world ​his story ​moves in ‌— the ‌genealogical names ​of ‌early Welsh ​dynasties, Beli ​Mawr among ‌them in ‌the literary ‌genealogies ‌— while ​the one ‌monument the ​tradition itself ​names, Branwen's ​'four-sided ‌grave' on ​the Alaw, ​survives as ​Bedd Branwen, ‌a ​Bronze Age ​cairn whose ​excavated cremations ‌are far ​older than ‌any ​Mabinogi event.[2] ‌The honesty ​of the ‌record is ‌the point: ​the ​branch's anchor ​in the ​landscape is ‌folklore's memory, ‌not antiquity's ​proof.

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

Efnysien is ​what a ‌story looks like ‌when it ​refuses to flatter ​anyone — ​including itself. The ​Second Branch never ​explains him. ‌He is not ​possessed, not ​wronged first, not ‌mad; the ​tale simply pairs ‌him with ​Nisien and lets ‌the contrast ​stand: what one ​brother binds, the ​other unbinds, ​and the world ​has both ‌kinds of man ​in it. ‌His evil is ​small in ‌scale and total ​in consequence ‌— a night ​with a knife ‌in a ​stable, and two ​kingdoms bleed ‌for a generation. ‌That is ‌the branch's hardest ​teaching about ​trouble: it does ‌not need ​to be great, ​only timely, and ‌no compensation ​is large enough ​to make ‌an injured king ​forget.[1]

And ‌then the ending, ​which the ​Welsh text declines ​to call ​redemption. Efnysien sees ‌what his mischief ‌has cost ​— the speechless ‌dead rising ​nightly from the ​cauldron — ‌and lies down ‌among the ‌corpses of the ‌enemy to ​be thrown in ‌with them. No ​one forgives ​him in the ​text; no ‌one praises him; ​the branch ​records only that ​the cauldron ‌broke and his ​heart broke. ​The destroyer is ​allowed one act ‌of destruction ​aimed at destruction ‌itself, and ​it costs exactly ‌everything he ​has. The Mabinogi ‌does not ‌promise that the ​man who ​sets the fire ​can put it ​out. It ‌shows him choosing ‌to burn ​with it.

Sources

  1. The Mabinogi, Second Branch, trans. Davies (2007).
