The Authentic Name

Unicode restoration and ASCII comparison

Original Script

PLVTO

The name in its original Roman form. Plūtō (PLVTO) is attested in the source tradition — “"The Rich One" — Roman lord of the underworld and of buried wealth”. Its macron-length vowels carry the full phonetic and orthographic weight of the source tradition.

ASCII Constraint

pluto

Reduced to plain pluto, the name loses everything that made it specific: macron-length vowels. What remains is an ASCII string that machines can parse but that no longer speaks with its original voice.

Unicode Restoration

Plūtō

The Unicode restoration recovers what ASCII flattened. Plūtō restores macron-length vowels, returning the name to its original written dignity. The domain encodes to Punycode, but the browser displays the truth.

Punycode Encoding
Plūtō.com → xn--plt-sxa2m.com

The non-ASCII characters in Plūtō are encoded while the ASCII remains visible. To the DNS, it is Punycode. To humanity, it is Plūtō.

The Accent / Script Rule

Latin Plūtō carries two long vowels, both marked by the restoration. His name is Rome's calque of the Greek Πλούτων — "the Rich One" — the underworld god whose buried wealth made death and riches share a single name. Both vowels are long in the classical scansion; Lewis & Short print the macrons that the restoration keeps.

The Rich One Below

Underworld, Wealth, and the Doors of the Deep

Plūtō is Rome's underworld god in his own right — not merely Hádēs renamed, but the Latin "Rich One" who rules the dead and the buried treasure together: the god in whose name the ground gives up both its wealth and its silence.

06

Mythology

Stories of Plūtō

Plūtō's Roman myths retell the Greek cycle with a merchant's accent: the underworld as a treasury as much as a tribunal.

The Taking

Proserpina in the Meadow

Ovid tells the Roman version of the oldest underworld story: Plūtō rose through the earth of Henna's meadow as Proserpina gathered flowers, and carried her down through the lake's opening to be his queen. Cerēs searched the world with torches; the famine followed her grief; and the settlement — half the year above, half below — made the seasons. Rome read it as agriculture's charter-myth: seed descends, grain returns.

The Wealth

Plūtus the Giver

Under the name Plūtus he was the benign twin: the wealth that comes from under the earth — grain, metal, the harvest itself. Aristophanes' comedy made him a blind old man who, healed, gives riches to the just; Rome's poets used him for the moral that wealth, like the grave, is no respecter of persons. The two names stayed one god: the Rich One below is also the Giver below.

The Gate

The Plutonium of Rome

Rome kept its own door to his kingdom at the Tarentum in the Campus Martius, where, in 249 BCE during the darkest days of the First Punic War, the city staged the first Ludi Saeculares — three nights of sacrifice and games for Dis Pater and Proserpina to turn the war. The famous pit-leaper belongs elsewhere and earlier: Marcus Curtius, who rode armed into the Forum's chasm in 362 BCE (Livy 7.6); and the devotio of the Decii was battlefield self-sacrifice, never pit-hurling. His gate is Rome's nuclear option in story — the real rite was a festival, not a fall.

Extended Lore

Plūtō is the god of what the ground holds: the dead, the ore, the seed, the secret. Rome's insight was to give all four one name and one address — the rich dark below where everything valuable also fears to go. He asks the miner's and the mourner's common question: what will you trust to the deep — and what, in your season, will it give back?
