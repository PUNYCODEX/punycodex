The Authentic Name

Unicode restoration and ASCII comparison

Original Script

𐎀𐎘𐎗𐎚

The name in its original Canaanite form. ʾAṯiratu (𐎀𐎘𐎗𐎚) is attested in the source tradition — “‘Lady of the Sea’ — the Ugaritic mother of the gods (rbt ʾaṯrt ym), consort of ʾIlu and intercessor for Baʿlu, KTU”. Its Semitic pharyngeal letters carry the full phonetic and orthographic weight of the source tradition.

ASCII Constraint

athiratu

Reduced to plain athiratu, the name loses everything that made it specific: Semitic pharyngeal letters. What remains is an ASCII string that machines can parse but that no longer speaks with its original voice.

Unicode Restoration

ʾAṯiratu

The Unicode restoration recovers what ASCII flattened. ʾAṯiratu restores Semitic pharyngeal letters, returning the name to its original written dignity. The domain encodes to Punycode, but the browser displays the truth.

Punycode Encoding
ʾAṯiratu.com → xn--airatu-2jd7287c.com

The non-ASCII characters in ʾAṯiratu are encoded while the ASCII remains visible. To the DNS, it is Punycode. To humanity, it is ʾAṯiratu.

Etymology

From ʾaṯr, “place, sanctuary” — “Lady of the Place/Sanctuary”; the sea-epithet rbt ʾaṯrt ym, “the Great Lady who walks on the Sea”, gave rise to her Greek-era reading as a sea-goddess.

Meaning

Mother of the gods (qnyt ʾilm), consort of ʾIlu, intercessor for Baʿlu, patroness of the royal house; “Lady of the Sea”.

From original to transliteration

  1. The Ugaritic alphabet writes the name in four signs: ʾa-ṯi-ra-tu.
  2. The normalized vocalization ʾAṯiratu follows the KTU convention; the shorter form ʾAṯirat (without final -u) is also common in modern scholarship.
  3. The Greek tradition renders her name Ἀσερά (Philo of Byblos); the Hebrew Bible knows her as ʾăšērāh (Asherah), and the Phoenician form shifts ṯ → š as Ašeratu.
  4. The Unicode restoration ʾAṯiratu preserves the glottal onset and the interdental ṯ; the domain aṯiratu.com carries the registrable ṯ.

Pronunciation

How ʾAṯiratu was spoken

/ʔaθ.i.ˈraː.tu/ Ugaritic Reconstruction

Modern Approximation

ah-thi-RAH-too — the th is the soft interdental of English 'thin'; the stress and the long ā fall together on the third syllable.

Etymological Kin

  • Ugaritic 𐎀𐎘𐎗𐎚 (ʾaṯrt) — alphabetic cuneiform, four signs
  • Phoenician 𐤀𐤔𐤓𐤕 (ʾšrt, Ašeratu) — the ṯ has become š
  • Hebrew אֲשֵׁרָה (ʾĂšērāh) — the goddess and her cult object
  • Owned form ʾAṯiratu

The Accent / Script Rule

Ugaritic writes the name 𐎀𐎘𐎗𐎚 — four signs, a-ṯ-r-t, in alphabetic cuneiform. The second consonant is the letter ṯ: a voiceless interdental fricative /θ/, the 'thin' sound English keeps and most later Semitic languages lost. In Phoenician and Hebrew the sound shifted to š, which is why the same goddess appears as Ašeratu in Phoenician and ʾĂšērāh in the Hebrew Bible. The restoration ʾAṯiratu keeps the Ugaritic consonant exactly where the tablets put it, marks the glottal onset ʾalep, and carries the nominative case-vowel -u; the scholarly Latin convention 'Athirat' inserts an h that no Ugaritic sign writes.

05

The Lady of the Sea

Creatress of the Gods, Consort of ʾIlu, and the Queen Mother's Voice

ʾAṯiratu is the great mother of the Ugaritic pantheon: qnyt ʾilm, 'Creatress of the Gods', consort of the high god ʾIlu, and the one deity who can walk into his tent and change his mind. Her title rbt ʾaṯrt ym — 'the Great Lady who walks on the Sea' — makes her sovereign of the waters that fed, and feared, the city of Ugarit.

Creatress of the Gods

qnyt ʾilm: the whole divine council are 'the seventy sons of ʾAṯiratu' — and she names their kings.

Lady of the Sea

rbt ʾaṯrt ym: she treads the Mediterranean that carried Ugarit's wealth, and her own servant is the divine fisherman.

The Intercessor

When Baʿlu needs a palace, it is ʾAṯiratu who rides to ʾIlu's tent and wins the decree (KTU 1.4 III–V).

The Queen Mother

qnyt ʾilm: the creatress of the gods — the mother whose voice settles the court.

Sacred Symbols

The sea ym, the waters she treads and rules — the title rbt ʾaṯrt ym made permanent
The spindle The queen mother's domestic sovereignty — women's work raised to cosmic office
The saddled donkey The beast her servant harnesses for the ride to ʾIlu's tent (KTU 1.4 iv) — diplomacy's mount
06

Mythology

Stories of ʾAṯiratu

ʾAṯiratu rarely fights; she decides. Her scenes in the Ugaritic tablets show the queen mother at work — approached by the younger gods, saddling her donkey, speaking in the high god's tent, and settling what thunder cannot.

The Baal Cycle

A Palace for Baʿlu

In KTU 1.4 the storm-god Baʿlu has defeated Yammu but has no house — and no standing — among the gods. The queen mother acts. She orders her servant Qōdēšu-wa-ʾAmruru to saddle her donkey, rides to the source of the two rivers, to the tent of ʾIlu, and prostrates herself. ʾIlu sees her coming and is glad; the banquet is spread, the request is made, and the decree is given: let a house be built for Baʿlu like the gods', and let Kôṯāru-wa-Ḫasīsu build it. Without her embassy the storm-god stays homeless.

The Baal Cycle

The Seventy Sons and the Succession

The tablets call her qnyt ʾilm, 'Creatress of the Gods', and the pantheon itself 'the seventy sons of ʾAṯiratu'. When Baʿlu lies dead in the realm of Môtu, it is to her that ʾIlu turns: let one of your sons be made king. She names ʿAṯtaru the Radiant, who climbs Baʿlu's throne on Mount Ṣāpānu — and cannot fill it: his feet do not reach the footstool, his head does not reach the top, and he steps down. Even the succession is hers to attempt.

The Keret Epic

The Vow of King Keret

In KTU 1.14–1.16 the childless King Keret marches on Udum to win the bride Ḫurriyu, and on the road he stops at the shrine of 'ʾAṯiratu of the Tyrians and the goddess of the Sidonians'. There he vows: if he brings the woman home, he will pay her measure in silver and gold. He wins the bride — and forgets the vow. Sickness follows, the land grieves, and the epic's third tablet turns on the queen mother's unpaid debt. Kings swear to her; she collects.

The Lady

She Who Treads the Sea

Her great title rbt ʾaṯrt ym is read 'the Great Lady who walks on the Sea' — and by some scholars, 'she who treads on the sea-dragon'. Her servant Qōdēšu-wa-ʾAmruru is called dgy ʾaṯrt, 'fisherman of ʾAṯiratu', and rows her messages across the deep; and in the Keret Epic the royal heir is promised to 'suck the milk of ʾAṯiratu, the breasts of the Virgin ʿAnatu' — the queen mother's milk is what legitimates a king of Ugarit.

Extended Lore

ʾAṯiratu is the theology of the quiet word. The storm-god wins the battles; she wins the audience. Her power is the intercessor's power — the mother who saddles her own donkey, rides the length of the world to the high god's tent, and comes home with the decree in her hand. She asks the question every house and every institution answers daily: when the thunder fails, whose voice does the king still trust?
