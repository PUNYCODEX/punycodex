01

Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

ʾAṯiratu (athiratu) ​— Lady of ‌the Sea, Mother of ‌the Gods · ​"'Lady of the ​Sea' — the Ugaritic ​mother of the ​gods (rbt ʾaṯrt ​ym), consort of ʾIlu ‌and intercessor for ​Baʿlu" — belongs ​to the Canaanite tradition, ‌where she is ​catalogued under the ‌domain "Lady of the ​Sea, Mother of ‌the Gods". Her ​name is the first ​word of Ugarit's ​royal theology: qnyt ​ʾilm, 'Creatress of the ​Gods'[1].

ʾAṯiratu is ‌the great mother ​of the Ugaritic pantheon ‌— consort of ​the high god ‌ʾIlu, mother of the ​divine council itself ‌('the seventy sons ​of ʾAṯiratu'), and the ‌court's one reliable ​channel to its ​distant king. When the ‌storm-god Baʿlu needs ‌a palace, it ‌is ʾAṯiratu who saddles ​her donkey, rides ​to ʾIlu's tent ‌at the source of ​the rivers, and ​wins the decree ‌(KTU 1.4 III–V); when ​Baʿlu lies dead, ​it is to ‌her that ʾIlu turns ​to name a ‌successor. Her great ​title rbt ʾaṯrt ym, ​'the Great Lady ​who walks on ​the Sea', ties her ‌to the Mediterranean ‌that fed and ​feared the city of ‌Ugarit, and the ​tablets style her ​qnyt ʾilm, 'creatress of ‌the gods'[2]. She ‌is the Ugaritic ‌form of the figure ‌the Hebrew Bible ​knows as [ꜥAsherah](/asherah/) ‌and the Phoenicians as ​[ʾAšeratu](/aseratu/).[3]

PuniCodex restores ​the name as ​ʾAṯiratu and serves its ‌temple at aṯiratu.com. ​The restoration preserves ​distinctive features the ASCII ​form loses — ‌the glottal letter ​ʾ and, above all, ​the underdotted ṯ, ​the interdental consonant ‌/θ/ that Ugaritic writes ​and Phoenician and ‌Hebrew shifted to ​š — which places ‌the name in ​Tier 1. The ‌plain ASCII form athiratu ‌survives as a ​modern convenience imposed ​by the early domain-name ​system; the restoration, ​not the fallback, ‌is the form the ‌project defends as ​philologically complete[2].

Sources

  1. KTU 1.4 and related Ugaritic texts (ʾAṯiratu in the Baal Cycle).
  2. Coogan & Smith, Stories from Ancient Canaan.
  3. Day, Yahweh and the Gods and Goddesses of Canaan.
02

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

The name ​is attested ‌in Ugaritic as ‌𐎀𐎘𐎗𐎚 — four ​signs of ​alphabetic cuneiform, read ​a-ṯ-r-t and ​normalized ʾAṯiratu with ​the nominative case-vowel. ‌Her full ​court style runs ​rbt ʾaṯrt ym, ‌'the Great ​Lady who walks ‌on the ​Sea'[1].

The ASCII ‌form athiratu survives ​only because ​the early domain-name ​system could ​not carry diacritics; ​it is a ‌technological compromise, ​not an ancient ‌spelling. Worse, the ​scholarly Latin ‌convention 'Athirat' inserts ​an h ‌to hint at ​the ṯ — ‌a letter ​no Ugaritic sign ​writes. The ‌Unicode restoration ʾAṯiratu ‌recovers the name's ‌two distinctive ​features directly in ​the address bar: ‌the glottal ​onset ʾ (ʾalep) ​and the ‌underdotted ṯ, the ​voiceless interdental /θ/ ​that English ‌preserves in 'thin' ​and that ‌Phoenician and Hebrew ​shifted to š ​— whence ​the sister forms ​Ašeratu and ‌ʾĂšērāh. The restoration ‌preserves distinctive features ​the ASCII ‌form loses, which ​places the name ​in Tier ‌1.

The letter-by-letter ‌transformation runs:

‌- a → ‌ʾA — Glottal ​onset (ʾalep)
‌- t → ​ṯ — ​Underdotted t: /θ/ ​(Ugaritic ṯ, Greek ‌θ)

  • ​h → — ​— The h ​of the ‌Latin convention, unwritten ​in Ugaritic

​- i → ​i — Same
‌- r ​→ r — ‌Same

  • ​a → a ‌— Same
  • ​t → ‌t — Same

‌- u → ​u — ​Same

The project ​holds the ​domain aṯiratu.com (xn--airatu-w17b.com) ‌as the canonical ‌home of ​this name[2].

Sources

  1. KTU (Ugaritic texts: ʾaṯrt, rbt ʾaṯrt ym).
  2. Coogan & Smith, Stories from Ancient Canaan.
03

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The reconstructed ​pronunciation of the ‌name is /ʔaθ.i.ˈraː.tu/ ‌— Ugaritic Reconstruction.[1]

​Phoneme by ​phoneme:

  • ʾ ​— Glottal stop ​[ʔ], the letter ​ʾalep: a catch ‌in the throat ​before the first ​vowel, as ‌in the Cockney ​'bottle'
  • A ‌— Short open ​vowel [a]
  • ‌ṯ — Voiceless ​interdental fricative [θ], ​the 'th' ​of English 'thin' ​— the sound ​this temple exists ‌to keep
  • ​i — Short ‌close vowel [i]

​- r — ‌Tapped or ​trilled [r]

  • ‌ā — Long ​open vowel [aː], ‌carrying the stress

​- t — ​Voiceless dental stop ‌[t]

  • ‌u — Close ‌back vowel [u], ​the nominative case-ending ​of the divine ‌name

For the ​modern speaker, the ​closest approximation ‌is: ah-thi-RAH-too — ​the th is ​the soft interdental ‌of 'thin', never ​the hard t-h ‌of 'pot-house', and ​the stress and ​the long ​ā fall together ​on the third ‌syllable.

Kindred and ‌historical forms of ​the name:

  • ‌Ugaritic — 𐎀𐎘𐎗𐎚 ​(ʾaṯrt), alphabetic ​cuneiform, four signs

‌- Phoenician — ‌𐤀𐤔𐤓𐤕 (ʾšrt, Ašeratu), ‌the ṯ shifted ‌to š

  • ​Hebrew — אֲשֵׁרָה ‌(ʾĂšērāh), the ​goddess and her ​cult object
  • ​Greek — Ἀσερά, ‌the form Philo ​of Byblos hands ​to Greek readers

​ʾAṯiratu is a ‌Tier-1 restoration: ​the underdotted ṯ ​is the preserved ​non-English feature, a ‌distinctive letter the ​ASCII form erases. ‌The interdental survived ​in Arabic ‌(ث) but shifted ​to š across ‌Phoenician and Hebrew, ‌so that the ​same goddess is ​Ašeratu at Tyre ​and ʾĂšērāh ​in the Bible; ‌only Ugaritic, her ‌own archive, writes ​the ṯ.

Sources

  1. KTU (Ugaritic texts: ʾaṯrt); Day, Yahweh and the Gods and Goddesses of Canaan.
04

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

The name ​is preserved in ‌Ugaritic as 𐎀𐎘𐎗𐎚 ‌— alphabetic cuneiform, ​attested Ugaritic, ​c. 1400–1190 BCE, ​at Ras Shamra ​(ancient Ugarit) on ​the Syrian coast. ‌The script is ​written left-to-right, unusual ​among Semitic scripts, ‌in wedge-impressed ​clay.[1]

The scholarly ‌transliteration is ʾaṯrt ​(with the normalized ‌vocalization ʾAṯiratu), giving ​the reconstructed reading ​/ʔaθ.i.ˈraː.tu/.

Sign by ​sign, the name ​runs:[2]

  • ​𐎀 — ʾa ‌/ʔa/ — the ​first of Ugaritic's ‌three vowel-bearing ʾalep ​signs, a glottal ‌stop carrying the ​vowel a
  • ‌𐎘 — ​ṯ /θ/ — ‌the voiceless interdental ​fricative, the 'th' ​of English 'thin'; ‌the sign this ‌temple exists to ‌keep
  • 𐎗 ​— r ​/r/ — the ‌trilled liquid of ​the stem
  • ​𐎚 — t ‌/t/ — the ​feminine ending of ​the consonantal skeleton

‌The rendering ​proceeds step by ‌step:

  • Ugaritic ​writes thirty cuneiform ​signs for consonants, ​plus three ʾalep ​signs that carry ‌the vowels a, ‌i, u; ​the name's skeleton ‌a-ṯ-r-t is thus ​fully written, while ​the remaining vowels ‌and the nominative ‌case-vowel -u are ‌supplied by the ‌standard vocalization.[3]

​- The interdental ‌ṯ survives in ​Arabic (ث) but ​shifted to š ​in Phoenician and ‌Hebrew, producing the ​sister forms Ašeratu ​and ʾĂšērāh; ​only Ugaritic writes ‌the consonant this ​restoration keeps.

  • ​The scholarly Latin ​convention 'Athirat' signals ‌the ṯ with ​an h that ‌no Ugaritic ​sign writes; the ‌Unicode restoration ʾAṯiratu ​marks it with ‌the underdot instead, ‌and the registrable ​domain aṯiratu.com carries ​the name in ​.com — ​the Ugaritic script ‌itself is not ‌in the .com ​IDN table.

Sources

  1. KTU (the alphabetic corpus of Ras Shamra).
  2. Schniedewind & Hunt, A Primer on Ugaritic: Language, Culture and Literature.
  3. Daniels & Bright (eds.), The World's Writing Systems (Ugaritic).

Domains & Attributes

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sphere of influence, titles, epithets, domain cards.

ʾAṯiratu ​is the ‌great mother ‌of the ​Ugaritic ​pantheon: qnyt ​ʾilm, 'Creatress ​of the ​Gods', ‌consort of ​the high ​god ‌ʾIlu, and ​the one ‌deity who ​can ‌walk into ​his tent ​and change ​his ​mind. Her ​title rbt ‌ʾaṯrt ym ​— ‌'the Great ​Lady who ‌walks on ​the ‌Sea' — ​makes her ‌sovereign of ​the ​waters that ‌fed, and ‌feared, ‌the city ​of Ugarit.[1]

​### Creatress ‌of ​the Gods

​qnyt ʾilm: ‌the whole ​divine ​council are ‌'the seventy ​sons of ‌ʾAṯiratu' ​— and ​she names ​their kings.

​### ‌Lady of ‌the Sea

​rbt ʾaṯrt ‌ym: ​she treads ​the Mediterranean ‌that ‌carried Ugarit's ‌wealth, and ‌her own ​servant ‌is the ​divine fisherman.

​### The ​Intercessor

‌When Baʿlu ​needs a ​palace, it ​is ‌ʾAṯiratu who ​rides to ​ʾIlu's tent ​and ‌wins the ​decree (KTU ‌1.4 III–V).

​### ‌Lamp of ​the Gods

‌qnyt ‌ʾilm: the ​creatress of ​the gods ​— ​the mother ‌whose presence ‌illumines the ​court.

Symbols

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Iconography, attributes, and their meanings.

No ​cult statue ‌of ʾAṯiratu ‌is ​known from ​Ugarit: ​her image, ​like her ​power, ‌is carried ​in ​titles and ‌in the ​furniture ‌of the ​queen mother's ‌house. ​The recurring ​motifs ​are these:[1]

​- The ​sea ‌— ym, ​the ‌waters she ​treads and ‌rules; ​her title ‌rbt ​ʾaṯrt ym, ‌'the Great ​Lady ​who walks ‌on the ‌Sea', ‌is the ​most ​famous line ‌of Ugaritic ​theology[1]
​- The ‌spindle ​— the ​queen mother's ‌domestic ​sovereignty: weaving, ‌nursing, and ​provisioning ​raised to ​cosmic ​office, the ‌imagery that ‌later ​clings to ‌Asherah's ​cult[2]

  • ​The saddled ‌donkey ‌— the ‌beast her ‌servant ​Qōdēšu-wa-ʾAmruru harnesses ‌for ​the ride ​to ʾIlu's ​tent ‌(KTU 1.4 ​iv): ​diplomacy's mount, ​and the ‌picture ​every reader ​of ​the Baal ‌Cycle keeps ​of ‌her[1]
  • ​The lamp ‌— ​her title ‌qnyt ‌ʾilm, 'creatress ​of the ​gods', ​makes her ​the ‌mother of ‌the divine ​household[1]

Sources

  1. KTU (Ugaritic texts: rbt ʾaṯrt ym; qnyt ʾilm; KTU 1.4 iv).
  2. Hadley, The Cult of Asherah in Ancient Israel and Judah.
07

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

ʾAṯiratu rarely fights; ​she decides. Her scenes in ‌the Ugaritic tablets show the ‌queen mother at work ​— approached by the younger ​gods, saddling her donkey, speaking ​in the high god's tent, ​and settling what thunder cannot. ​The Baal Cycle and the ‌Keret Epic are her ​principal witnesses.[1]

A Palace ​for Baʿlu (KTU 1.4 III–V)

‌The storm-god [Baʿlu](/baal/) has defeated ​Yammu but has no house ‌— and no standing — ​among the gods, and the ‌high god [ʾIlu](/el/) cannot ​be moved by force or ​flattery from below. The queen ​mother acts. She orders her ​servant Qōdēšu-wa-ʾAmruru to saddle her ​donkey, rides to the source ‌of the two rivers, ​to the tent of ʾIlu, ‌and prostrates herself; ʾIlu sees ​her coming and is glad. ‌The banquet is spread, the ​request is made, and the ‌decree is given: let ​a house be built for ‌Baʿlu like the gods', and ​let Kôṯāru-wa-Ḫasīsu build it. Without ​her embassy the storm-god stays ‌homeless.[2]

The Seventy Sons ‌and the Succession (KTU 1.4 ‌VI; 1.6 I)

The ​tablets call her qnyt ʾilm, ​'Creatress of the Gods', and ‌the pantheon itself 'the seventy ​sons of ʾAṯiratu'. When Baʿlu ​lies dead in the realm ‌of [Môtu](/mot/), it is ​to her that ʾIlu turns: ​let one of your sons ‌be made king. She names ​ʿAṯtaru the Radiant, who climbs ‌Baʿlu's throne on Mount Ṣāpānu ​— and cannot fill ​it: his feet do not ​reach the footstool, his head ​does not reach the top, ‌and he steps down. Even ‌the succession is hers to ​attempt.[3]

The Vow ‌of King Keret (KTU 1.14–1.16)

​The childless King Keret marches ​on Udum to win the ‌bride Ḫurriyu, and on the ‌road he stops at the ‌shrine of 'ʾAṯiratu of the ‌Tyrians and the goddess ​of the Sidonians'. There he ‌vows: if he brings the ​woman home, he will pay ​her measure in silver and ​gold. He wins the bride ‌— and forgets the ​vow. Sickness follows, the land ​grieves, and the epic's third ​tablet turns on the queen ‌mother's unpaid debt. Kings swear ​to her; she collects.[4]

​She Who Treads the ​Sea

Her great title rbt ‌ʾaṯrt ym is read 'the ​Great Lady who walks on ‌the Sea' — and by ​some scholars, 'she who treads ‌on the sea-dragon'. Her servant ​is called dgy ʾaṯrt, ‌'fisherman of ʾAṯiratu', and rows ‌her messages across the deep; ​and in the Keret Epic ​the royal heir is promised ​to 'suck the milk of ​ʾAṯiratu, the breasts of ‌the Virgin [ʿAnatu](/anat/)' — the ‌queen mother's milk legitimates a ​king of Ugarit.[2]

Sources

  1. KTU 1.4 and 1.14–1.16 (the Baal Cycle and the Keret Epic).
  2. Coogan & Smith, Stories from Ancient Canaan.
  3. Smith, The Ugaritic Baal Cycle, vol. 2 (KTU 1.6).
  4. Wyatt, Religious Texts from Ugarit (the Keret Epic).

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

West ​Semitic speakers ‌carried ‌her under ​shifted ​sounds. In ​Phoenician the ​interdental ​ṯ becomes ‌š, ​and she ​is ‌ʾAšeratu — ​the ‌form Philo ​of ‌Byblos hands ​to Greek ​readers ​as Ἀσερά.[2] ​In ​the Hebrew ‌Bible ​she is ‌ʾĂšērāh, ​Asherah, the ‌most ​contested goddess ‌of Israel ​and ‌Judah: worshipped ​in ​the temple ‌itself, ‌cut down ‌by ​reformers, and ​slowly ‌flattened by ​polemic into ​a ‌wooden pole ​planted ​beside the ‌altar. ​Hittite tablets ‌know ​her as ​Ašertu, ​consort of ​Elkunirša, and ‌Egyptian ‌religion of ​the ‌empire age ​venerates ​a figure ‌many ‌scholars identify ‌with ‌her under ​the title ‌Qudšu, ​'Holiness'. One ​figure, ​three scripts, ‌a ​dozen polemics ​— ​the queen ‌mother ​outlasted every ​alphabet that ​renamed ‌her.[1]

Kindred ​figures ‌in the ​PuniCodex ‌cross-tradition index ​include ‌ꜥAsherah, ʾAšeratu, ‌ʾIlu, ​Baʿlu, ʿAnatu, ​and Šāpšu, ​each ​linked through ‌the ‌Ugaritic divine ​household.

Sources

  1. Day, Yahweh and the Gods and Goddesses of Canaan.
  2. Eusebius, Praeparatio Evangelica 1.10 (Philo of Byblos).

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

Her ​afterlife is ‌an ‌argument the ​Bible could ​not ​win. The ​Deuteronomists ​reduced her ‌to ​the ʾăšērâ, ​a planted ‌pole, ​yet the ‌prophets' ​fury is ‌itself ​the evidence: ​four hundred ​prophets ​of Asherah ​eat ‌at Jezebel's ​table ‌(1 Kings ​18:19), ‌women bake ​cakes for ‌the ​Queen of ‌Heaven ​(Jeremiah 7:18), ​and ‌Josiah must ‌drag her ‌image ​out of ​the ‌temple to ​burn ​it at ‌the Kidron ​(2 ​Kings 23:4–7).[1] ‌Twentieth-century ​archaeology turned ‌the ​polemic into ​attestation — ​the ​painted pithoi ‌of ‌Kuntillet ʿAjrud ​bless ‌a man ​'by Yahweh ​of ‌Samaria and ‌his ‌Asherah', and ‌the ​Khirbet el-Qom ‌tomb inscription ​names ​'Yahweh and ​his ‌Asherah' together.[2] ​Modern ​scholarship (Day, ​Hadley, ‌Wiggins) has ​rebuilt the ​queen ​mother from ‌those ​shards, and ‌feminist ​theology has ‌reclaimed her ​as ‌the divine ‌feminine ​edited out ​of ​the Western ​canon — ‌and ‌read back ​in.[3]

Sources

  1. Hebrew Bible, 1 Kings 18:19; Jeremiah 7:18; 2 Kings 23:4–7 (the Asherah polemics).
  2. Meshel, Kuntillet ʿAjrud (Ḥorvat Teman): An Iron Age II Religious Site on the Judah-Sinai Border.
  3. Day, Yahweh and the Gods and Goddesses of Canaan; Hadley, The Cult of Asherah; Wiggins, A Reassessment of Asherah.

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

The ​tablets of ‌Ras Shamra ‌are ​her archive: ​the palace ​narrative ​of KTU ​1.4, the ‌succession ​scene of ​KTU 1.6, ‌and ​Keret's vow ‌at her ​Tyrian ‌shrine were ​copied in ​the ​scribal school ​of ʾIlimilku ​in ‌the fourteenth–thirteenth ​centuries BCE ‌and ​buried when ‌the city ​fell ‌c. 1190 ​BCE.[1] No ‌temple ​of ʾAṯiratu ​has been ‌securely ‌identified at ‌Ugarit — ​the ​queen mother ‌seems to ​have ​shared the ‌acropolis cult ​of ​Baʿlu and ‌ʾIlu — ​but her ‌worship ​is stamped ​across the ​Iron ​Age Levant: ‌the tree-and-lion ‌iconography ​of the ‌tenth-century Taanach ​cult ​stand and ‌the hundreds ‌of ‌Judean pillar ‌figurines are ​associated ‌with her ​cult by ​many ​scholars.[2] The ‌decisive finds ​are ​the painted ​pithoi of ‌Kuntillet ​ʿAjrud (c. ​800 BCE), ​with ‌their blessing ​'by Yahweh ‌… ​and his ‌Asherah', and ​the ‌Khirbet el-Qom ‌tomb inscription ​— ​the objects ​that made ​the ‌biblical polemic ‌an archaeological ​fact.[3]

Sources

  1. KTU (the Ras Shamra tablets; the ʾIlimilku colophons).
  2. Keel & Uehlinger, Gods, Goddesses, and Images of God in Ancient Israel (Taanach; the pillar figurines).
  3. Meshel, Kuntillet ʿAjrud (Ḥorvat Teman); the Khirbet el-Qom tomb inscription.

Ugaritic Tablets

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The ​queen mother's ‌archive is ‌the ​alphabetic corpus ​of Ras ​Shamra. The ​Baal ​Cycle gives ‌her the ​palace ​narrative (KTU ‌1.4 III–V), ​the ‌banquet of ​'the seventy ‌sons ​of ʾAṯiratu' ​(KTU 1.4 ​VI), and ​the ​succession scene ‌of KTU ​1.6 ‌I, where ​ʾIlu asks ‌her ​to name ‌a king ​and ‌she names ​ʿAṯtaru the ​Radiant. The ‌Keret ‌Epic (KTU ‌1.14–1.16) preserves ​her ​coastal cult-title ‌'ʾAṯiratu of ​the ​Tyrians', the ‌king's vow ​at ​her shrine, ‌and the ​promise that ‌the ​royal heir ​will 'suck ​the ​milk of ‌ʾAṯiratu'.[1] Her ‌titles ​run through ‌the corpus: ​qnyt ​ʾilm, 'Creatress ‌of the ‌Gods'; rbt ‌ʾaṯrt ‌ym, 'the ​Great Lady ‌who ​walks on ​the Sea'; ​rbt ‌ʾaṯrt ym, ​'the Great ​Lady ​who walks ‌on the ​Sea'; and ​her ​servant Qōdēšu-wa-ʾAmruru ‌bears the ​office ‌dgy ʾaṯrt, ​'fisherman of ‌ʾAṯiratu'. ​The tablets ‌were copied ‌in ​the school ​of ʾIlimilku ​and buried ​when ‌Ugarit fell ‌c. 1190 ​BCE.[2]

Sources

  1. KTU 1.4–1.6 (Baal Cycle); KTU 1.14–1.16 (Keret Epic: ʾaṯrt ṣrm, the vow, the suckling oracle).
  2. Wyatt, Religious Texts from Ugarit; Schniedewind & Hunt, A Primer on Ugaritic.

The ​Hebrew Bible ‌knows her ‌as ​ʾĂšērāh — ​some ​forty occurrences, ​nearly all ​hostile. ‌The Deuteronomic ​law forbids ​planting ‌'an asherah, ​any tree', ‌beside ​the altar ‌(Deuteronomy ​16:21); Gideon ​cuts down ​his ​father's asherah ​(Judges 6:25–30); ‌Asa ​deposes the ‌queen mother ​Maacah ‌for her ​image ‌of Asherah ​(1 Kings ‌15:13); ​Elijah's contest ​counts 'the ‌four ‌hundred prophets ‌of Asherah ​who ​eat at ‌Jezebel's ​table' (1 ​Kings 18:19); ‌Manasseh ​sets her ​image in ‌the ​temple (2 ‌Kings 21:7) ​and ​Josiah burns ​it ​at the ‌Kidron (2 ‌Kings ​23:4–7). Whether ‌the biblical ​ʾăšērîm ​are the ‌goddess herself ‌or ‌her wooden ‌cult-object ​is the ‌standing question ​of ​the scholarship.[1] ​The polemics ‌are ​double-edged evidence: ​they prove ​the ‌cult's reach ​from ​the palace ​to the ‌village ​oven, and ‌the women ​who ‌bake cakes ​for the ‌Queen ‌of Heaven ​(Jeremiah ​7:18; 44:15–19) ​testify to ​a ‌devotion the ‌text cannot ​extinguish.[2]

Sources

  1. Hebrew Bible, Deuteronomy 16:21; Judges 6:25–30; 1 Kings 15:13; 18:19; 2 Kings 21:7; 23:4–7.
  2. Day, Yahweh and the Gods and Goddesses of Canaan; Hadley, The Cult of Asherah in Ancient Israel and Judah.

Inscriptions & Seals

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Beyond ​Ugarit, the ‌epigraphic record ‌carries ​her under ​every ​stage of ​the name. ​The ‌Amarna letters ​preserve the ​theophoric ‌name Abdi-Aširta, ​'servant of ‌Aširta', ​borne by ‌the ​ruler of ​Amurru in ​the ​fourteenth century ​BCE — ‌her ​name fossilized ‌in a ​Canaanite ‌chancellery. Hittite ​tablets ‌know her ​as Ašertu, ‌consort ​of Elkunirša, ​'El creator ‌of ‌the earth'; ‌Egyptian texts ​of ​the empire ‌age ​venerate a ​figure many ‌scholars ​identify with ​her as ‌Qudšu, ​'Holiness'.[1] The ‌Iron Age ​record ​is the ​famous ​one: the ‌painted pithoi ‌of ​Kuntillet ʿAjrud ‌bless a ​man ​'by Yahweh ‌of Samaria ‌(or ‌Teman) and ‌his ​Asherah', and ‌the Khirbet ​el-Qom ​tomb inscription ​joins 'Yahweh ‌and ​his Asherah' ​in a ​blessing ‌— whether ​the ​word names ​the goddess ‌or ​her symbol ‌is still ​argued. ‌In Phoenician ​proper she ‌is ‌ʾšrt, Ašeratu, ​and ​Philo of ​Byblos renders ​her ‌for Greek ‌readers as ​Ἀσερά.[2]

Sources

  1. Moran, The Amarna Letters (Abdi-Aširta of Amurru); the Elkunirša myth (Hittite: Ašertu).
  2. Meshel, Kuntillet ʿAjrud (KH1–KH3); Khirbet el-Qom; Eusebius, Praeparatio Evangelica 1.10 (Philo of Byblos).

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

ʾAṯiratu ​is ‌the ‌theology of ​the ​quiet ​word. ​The ​storm-god wins ‌the ​battles; ​she ‌wins ​the ‌audience. Her ​power ‌is ​the ​intercessor's ​power — ​the ​mother ‌who ​saddles ‌her ​own donkey, ‌rides ​the ‌length ​of ‌the world ​to ​the ‌high ‌god's ‌tent, ​and comes ​home ‌with ​the ​decree ‌in her ​hand.[1]

​She ‌asks ​the ‌question ​every house ​and ​every ​institution ‌answers ‌daily: when ​the ‌thunder ​fails, ​whose ‌voice ‌does the ‌king ‌still ​trust? ‌To ​contemplate ʾAṯiratu ​is ​to ‌remember ​that ​influence ​need not ‌be ​loud ​to ​be ‌absolute — ​and ‌that ​the ‌figures ​written ‌out of ‌the ​record ​are ​often ​the ones ‌holding ‌it ​together.[1]

Sources

  1. KTU 1.4 (the intercession for Baʿlu).
