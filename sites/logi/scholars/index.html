01

Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Logi (logi) ​— Fire, Flame, ‌Giant · ‌Flame — is ​the personified wildfire ​of the Norse ​corpus: the ​common noun logi ​‘flame, blaze’ seated ‌at a bench ​in the ​hall of Útgarða-Loki, ‌where he meets ​the god Loki ‌in an eating ​contest and ‌wins it absolutely ​— consuming the ​meat, the bones, ​and the ​trough itself.[1]

His ​attestation is spare ‌and perfectly shaped. ​Gylfaginning gives ‌him his single ​scene: Útgarða-Loki calls ‌him from the ​bench to ‌answer Loki's boast ​that no one ‌eats faster, and ​only afterward, ​in the unmasking ‌of the hall's ‌illusions, does the ‌giant-king explain what ​the company ​had watched — ‌‘he who contended ​with Loki in ​the eating-contest ‌was wildfire itself’, ​villieldr, and it ​was no wonder ‌the trough ​went so quickly.[1] ‌A second, genealogical ​tradition makes him ​elemental family: ​Skáldskaparmál's kennings for ​wind and the ‌legendary preface Hversu ‌Noregr byggðist ​name Logi among ‌the three sons ​of the primeval ​giant Fornjótr — ‌Hlér the ‌sea (called Ægir), ‌Logi the fire, ‌and Kári the ​wind.[2]

PuniCodex ‌restores the name ​as Logi and ​serves its temple ​at /logi/. ‌The restoration is ​identical to the ​ASCII form, because ​normalized Old ‌Norse writes no ​diacritic or distinctive ​letter in this ​name; the ‌mechanical rule therefore ​places it in ‌Tier 2. The ​plain-ASCII logi.com is ‌the philologically ​complete form — ‌it is registered ‌to a third ​party, held ​outside the PuniCodex ​collection, and watched ​for release; no ‌owned domain ‌is claimed for ​this entry.[3]

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 44–47 (the Útgarða-Loki journey; the eating contest and its unmasking), ed. and trans. Anthony Faulkes (London: Everyman, 1987).
  2. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál (kennings for wind; the sons of Fornjótr), trans. Faulkes (1987); Hversu Noregr byggðist (Flateyjarbók).
  3. Cleasby, Richard, and Guðbrandur Vigfússon, An Icelandic-English Dictionary, 2nd ed. (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1957), s.v. logi.
02

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

The name ​is attested in ‌Old Norse ‌as Logi (weak ​masculine; accusative Loga), ​and it ​is the commonest ​kind of mythological ​name in ‌the North: a ​transparent appellative elevated ​to a ‌person. The common ​noun logi, m., ‌means ‘flame, blaze’; ​beside it ‌stand log, n., ​‘flame’, and the ​verb loga, ​‘to burn with ​a flame’.[1] Zoëga ​glosses the ‌word simply ‘flame’, ​and Cleasby–Vigfusson record ‌it in ​the full range ‌of literal and ​figurative uses, from ‌hearth-fire to ​poetic periphrasis.[2]

Because ‌the manuscripts write ​four plain ​Latin letters, the ‌ASCII form loses ‌nothing: the ‌Unicode restoration Logi ​and the ASCII ​input logi ‌are the same ​word, and the ​mechanical tier rule ‌classifies the ​name Tier 2 ​— an honest ‌report about ​orthography, not a ‌judgment of the ​figure. The ​old scholarly temptation ​ran the other ​way, toward too ‌much meaning ‌rather than too ​little: nineteenth-century philology ‌heard the ​fire-word in the ​god Loki and ‌made Loki ‌a fire-god, an ‌equation modern scholarship ‌rejects — ​the vowels differ ‌in kind, and ​Gylfaginning itself stages ​Logi as ​Loki's opponent, not ‌his double.[1]

The ​letter-by-letter transformation ​runs:

  • l ​→ L — ‌Same, capitalized

​- o → ​o — Same
​- g ‌→ g — ​Same

  • i ‌→ i — ​Same

The ‌project holds no ​domain for this ‌name: the ‌plain-ASCII logi.com is ​registered to a ​third party ​outside the collection ​and watched for ‌release; the ‌temple is served ​at /logi/.

Sources

  1. Cleasby, Richard, and Guðbrandur Vigfússon, An Icelandic-English Dictionary, 2nd ed. (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1957), s.v. logi, log, loga.
  2. Zoëga, Geir T., A Concise Dictionary of Old Icelandic (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1910), s.v. logi.
03

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The reconstructed ​pronunciation of ‌the name is ‌/ˈlo.ɣi/ — Old ​Norse Reconstruction.[1]

​Phoneme by phoneme:

​- L- ​— Voiced alveolar ​lateral [l], ‌unchanged from Proto-Germanic; ​the name opens ​as cleanly ‌as English low
​- -o- ‌— Short mid ​back [o], ‌as in English ​lot; the Old ​Norse vowel ​is short, never ​the diphthong ​of English low
‌- -gi — ​Voiced velar ‌fricative [ɣ], the ​soft medial ‌g of Spanish ​lago, not ‌the hard stop ​of English go ‌— followed ​by short close ​front [i]; ‌a light second ‌syllable closed ‌by the vowel

​For the modern ​speaker, the ‌closest approximation is: ​‘LOH-yi’ — ​two short, even ‌syllables with the ​weight on ​the first, the ‌medial g ​softened to a ‌voiced friction ​in the throat, ​as in modern ​Icelandic logi.

​Kindred and historical ‌forms of ‌the name:

  • ​Old Norse — ‌logi, m., ​‘flame, blaze’, the ​common noun ‌borne as a ‌proper name

‌- Modern Icelandic ‌— logi, still ​the everyday ‌word for a ​flame

  • ​Word-family — log ​n. ‘flame’; ‌loga v. ‘to ​burn with a ​flame, blaze’[2]

​- Genealogical form ‌— Logi, ​son of Fornjótr, ​brother of Hlér ​(the sea, ‌called Ægir) and ​Kári (the ‌wind)

Logi is ​Tier 2 ‌because the restoration ​preserves nothing the ‌ASCII form ‌loses: no diacritic, ​no distinctive ​letter, no vowel ​length. English ​‘Logi’ and Old ‌Norse ‘Logi’ are, ‌unusually, the ​same spelling.

Sources

  1. Gordon, E. V., An Introduction to Old Norse, 2nd ed. rev. A. R. Taylor (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1957), phonology and grammar.
  2. de Vries, Jan, Altnordisches etymologisches Wörterbuch, 2nd ed. (Leiden: Brill, 1962), s.v. logi.

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

The ​name is ‌preserved in ‌medieval Icelandic ​manuscripts in ​the Latin ​alphabet as ​Logi — ​Old ‌Norse (North ​Germanic), attested ​13th–14th c. ‌CE in ​the Codex ‌Regius of ​the Prose ‌Edda ​(GKS 2367 ​4to), Codex ​Wormianus, and ​Codex Upsaliensis, ​recording material ‌centuries older.[1] ​Before the ‌Latin ​bookhand, Old ‌Norse names ​were carved ‌in the ​younger futhark; ‌no runic ​attestation of ​this ‌figure survives, ‌so the ‌manuscript form ​is the ​primary witness.

‌The scholarly ​rendering proceeds ​step ‌by step:

​- The ​word is ‌the common ​noun logi, ‌m., ‘flame, ​blaze’, capitalized ​as ​a proper ​name; the ‌weak-masculine inflection ‌(accusative Loga) ​confirms the ‌appellative origin.
​- The ​vowel ‌o is ‌short; no ‌acute accent, ‌ogonek, or ​distinctive letter ‌belongs in ​the normalized ​spelling.
​- A ‌hypothetical Younger ​Futhark carving ​would be ​ᛚᚢᚴᛁ — ‌the same ​runes as ​Loki, ​since ᚴ ‌writes both ​k and ‌g.

  • ​The Unicode ‌restoration Logi ​and the ‌ASCII ‌form are ​identical: there ​is nothing ​to recover, ​and the ‌Tier-2 classification ‌records exactly ​that.[2]

Sources

  1. Gordon, E. V., An Introduction to Old Norse, 2nd ed. rev. Taylor (Clarendon Press, 1957).
  2. Cleasby & Vigfússon, An Icelandic-English Dictionary, 2nd ed. (Clarendon Press, 1957), s.v. logi.

Domains & Attributes

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sphere of influence, titles, epithets, domain cards.

Logi ​is catalogued ‌under the ‌domain Fire, ​Flame, Giant ​— three ​words that ​are, in ​his case, ‌one fact ​stated three ​ways: he ‌is a ​giant in ‌the setting ​of Útgarðr, ‌and he ​is fire ​in truth.[1]

​### Fire

​His name ​is the ‌Old Norse ​common noun ‌for flame; ​the unmasking ‌in Gylfaginning ​confirms the ‌personification outright: ​the ‌eater was ​villieldr, wildfire ​itself.[1]

‌The Eating ‌Contest

His ‌one scene ​is the ​mythology's cleanest ‌fable of ​appetite: against ​Loki, the ‌gods' own ​hunger, a ​trough of ‌meat is ​set — ‌and the ​verdict turns ​on what ​each eater ​leaves behind.

‌### The ‌Elemental Family

​The Fornjótr ‌genealogy makes ​him fire ​brothered to ‌sea and ‌wind — ‌Hlér ‌(called Ægir) ​and Kári ‌— a ​family tree ​that is ​really a ‌classification of ​elements.[2]

​The Giants' ​World

He ‌belongs to ​the hall ​of Útgarða-Loki ​in Jǫtunheimr, ‌the world ​of the ‌jǫtnar, where ​strength, cunning, ‌and illusion ​are the ‌native arts; ‌his kin ​at the ​world's edge ​is Surtr ​of Múspellheimr, ‌whose fire ‌ends the ​world.

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 44–47, trans. Faulkes (1987).
  2. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál (kennings for wind), trans. Faulkes (1987); Hversu Noregr byggðist (Flateyjarbók).

Symbols

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Iconography, attributes, and their meanings.

Logi's ​iconography is ‌narrative ‌rather than ​cultic ​— the ​corpus ​gives him ​no ‌regalia, but ​the ​contest ‌fixed a ​small ‌set of ​attributes:[1]

‌- The ​feeding-trough ​— Set ​the ​length of ​the ‌hall floor, ​full ‌of meat, ​with ‌an eater ​at ‌each end: ​appetite ‌made measurable, ​and ​the only ‌scoreboard ‌in ‌mythology that ​fire ​ever emptied
‌- ​The eaten ​bones ‌— Loki ​strips ​the meat ‌from ​the bones; ‌Logi ​consumes meat, ​bones, ​and trough: ​the ‌line between ‌hunger ​and combustion ‌drawn ​in ​leftovers

  • ‌The ‌flame (logi) ‌— ‌His only ​emblem ‌is his ​name ​itself, the ​common ‌noun the ​hearth ​already used ​nightly, ‌set in ​capital ​letters
  • ​The ‌brothers Hlér ​and ‌Kári — ​Sea ‌and ​wind: the ‌genealogy ‌that makes ​fire ​one element ​among ​three, not ‌a ‌freak among ​persons[2]

Sources

  2. Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide to the Gods, Heroes, Rituals, and Beliefs (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. Logi.

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

Logi's mythology is ​one scene and one ‌genealogy, and the scene is ‌constructed to prove the ​genealogy's point: he is an ​element with a name. ​When Þórr journeys east to ​Útgarðr with Loki and ​the servants Þjálfi and Rǫskva, ‌every marvel on the ​road — the giant Skrýmir, ​the glove that serves ‌as a hall, the ​blows that wake the sleeper ‌— prepares the reader ​for a place where nothing ‌is what it is ​called.[1]

The Eating Contest ​(Gylfaginning 45)

Útgarða-Loki declares ​that no one may stay ​who is not master ​of some art beyond other ‌men. Loki boasts that ​no one in the hall ‌eats faster; the king ​calls Logi from the bench. ‌A trough of meat ​is set on the floor; ‌the two eat from ​opposite ends and meet in ‌the middle. Loki has ​eaten all the meat from ​the bones — but ‌Logi has eaten the ‌meat, the bones, and the ‌trough as well, ‘and ​it now seemed to everyone ​that Loki had lost ‌the game’.[1]

The Unmasking ​(Gylfaginning 46–47)

The evening's ​other contests fail the same ‌way: Þjálfi is outrun ​by Hugi; Þórr cannot drain ​the horn, lift the ‌grey cat, or throw the ​old woman Elli. In ‌the morning the giant-king confesses ​the illusions outside the ​vanished stronghold: the horn's end ​stood in the sea, ​the cat was the Midgard ‌Serpent, Elli was old ‌age, Hugi was the king's ​own thought — ‘and ‌he who contended with Loki ​in the eating-contest was ​wildfire itself’, villieldr. Fire ‌against hunger was never a ‌contest of the same ‌kind.[1]

The Sons of ‌Fornjótr (Skáldskaparmál; Hversu Noregr ​byggðist)

Skáldskaparmál's kennings for wind ‌name Kári as Fornjótr's ​son, with brothers Hlér and ​Logi; Hversu Noregr byggðist ​in the Flateyjarbók gives the ‌triad its famous form ​— Fornjótr had three sons: ​Hlér, ‘whom we call ​Ægir’, Logi, and Kári. Sea, ‌fire, and wind as ​one old giant's household: the ​genealogy is classification, not ​narrative.[2]

The Flame That ‌Goes Out (Vafþrúðnismál 51)

​When Óðinn asks the giant ‌Vafþrúðnir who will survive ​the world's burning, the ‌answer keeps Víðarr and Váli ​in the gods' sanctuaries ‌‘when Surtr's flame dies down’ ‌— þá er sloknar ​surtalogi. The compound welds Logi's ​noun to Surtr's name: ​the fire of Múspellheimr that ​ends the world and ‌the flame that ate the ‌trough are one word ​at two scales.[3]

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 44–47, trans. Faulkes (1987).
  2. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál (kennings for wind), trans. Faulkes (1987); Hversu Noregr byggðist (Flateyjarbók).
  3. Vafþrúðnismál 51 (Poetic Edda), trans. Carolyne Larrington, The Poetic Edda, 2nd ed. (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2014).

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

Logi's comparative ​history begins ‌with a ‌mistake and ​ends with ​a commonplace. ​The mistake ​is famous: ​nineteenth-century philology, ‌from Grimm ​onward, heard the ​fire-word in ‌the god ​Loki and ‌made Loki ​a fire-god. ‌The equation ​is now ​abandoned — ​the vowels ​differ in kind, ​the figures ‌never merge ​in the ‌texts, and ​Snorri stages ‌them face ​to face ‌across a ​trough, which is ‌about as ​far apart ​as two ‌beings can ‌sit.[1] Its ‌monument survives ​in art ​rather than ‌scholarship: Wagner's ​Loge, the flame-cynic ​of the ‌Ring cycle, ​is the ​conflation set ‌to music.

​The commonplace ‌is older ​than any ​comparison: every ​tradition that watches ​fire discovers ‌that it ‌eats. The ​Vedic hymns ‌give flame ​a mouth ​and a ‌priesthood in ‌Agni, the ‌devourer who carries ‌the sacrifice; ​the Norse ‌kept the ​same observation ​at the ​scale of ‌a common ​noun, logi, ​and personified it ​once, brilliantly, ‌as the ​unbeatable guest.[2] ​Within the ​corpus his ‌kin are ​elemental: Surtr ‌of Múspellheimr, ​whose sword-fire ‌burns the world; ​Ægir, the ‌sea his ‌own genealogy ​calls brother; ​and Loki, ​the god ​his name ‌was once ‌wrongly spent ​to explain.

Sources

  1. Simek, Rudolf, Dictionary of Northern Mythology, trans. Angela Hall (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer, 1993), s.v. Logi, Loki.
  2. Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide to the Gods, Heroes, Rituals, and Beliefs (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. Logi.

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

The ​eating contest ‌is one ‌of the ​most ​retold scenes ​of the ​Norse ​revival: the ‌Kearys' The ​Heroes of ​Asgard ‌(1857), the ​first popular ‌English retelling ​of ‌the myths, ​made Útgarða-Loki's ​hall ​a set-piece ​for generations ​of children, ‌and ​every handbook ‌since keeps ​Logi's trough ‌beside ​Hugi's running ‌and Elli's ​wrestling ‌as the ​triad of ​unwinnable contests ‌— ‌fire, thought, ‌and age, ​the three ​things ‌no champion ​beats.[1]

The ​nineteenth ‌century's false ​etymology had ​an afterlife ‌of ​its own: ‌Wagner's Loge, ​and a ​long ​tail of ​fire-trickster Loki ‌in ‌popular fiction, ​are children ‌of the ​conflation ​scholarship has ‌buried. In ‌Iceland the ‌name ‌never needed ​reviving: logi ‌remains ​the everyday ​word for ​flame, from ‌hearth-talk ​to weather ​reports, and ​Logi persists ‌as ​a given ​name. Modern ​retellings ‌and games ​cast him ‌as he ​was ‌written — ​fire wearing ‌a face, ‌the ​one opponent ​in Útgarðr ​who ​cheated by ‌being exactly ‌what he ​was.[2]

Sources

  1. Keary, Annie, and Eliza Keary, The Heroes of Asgard: Tales from Scandinavian Mythology (London: Macmillan, 1857).
  2. Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide to the Gods, Heroes, Rituals, and Beliefs (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. Logi.

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

Logi ​is a ‌textual ‌figure ​in the ​strictest ​sense. His ​attestations ​are ‌the Snorra ​Edda ​manuscripts — ‌Codex ​Regius (GKS ‌2367 ​4to), ‌Codex Wormianus, ​Codex ​Upsaliensis — ​together ​with ​the þulur, ‌the ​rhymed name-lists ‌appended ​to ‌the Prose ​Edda, ‌which keep ​the ‌common ​noun logi ​among ‌the heiti ‌for ‌fire, and ​the ​Fornjótr ‌genealogy of ​Hversu ​Noregr byggðist ‌in ​the ​Flateyjarbók.[1]

No ‌runic ​inscription names ‌him, ​no ​place-name is ​securely ​his, and ‌no ‌cult ​could attach ‌to ​a figure ​who ‌is, by ‌definition, ‌an ‌element rather ​than ‌a person. ​His ​material ​context is ‌the ​fire itself: ​the ​hearth-centered ‌longhouse, the ​cremation ​pyres of ​the ‌Late ​Iron Age, ‌and ​the wildfire ‌of ​the northern ‌forests ‌— ​the villieldr ​Snorri ​names as ​the ‌truth ‌behind the ​illusion.[2]

Poetic Edda

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The ​Poetic Edda ‌never stages ‌Logi: ​his one ​scene ​belongs to ​Snorri's prose, ​and ‌no Eddic ​poem ​names him ‌as a ​person. ‌His presence ​in ‌the poetic ​corpus is ​lexical, ​and it ​is ​revealing. The ‌word logi ​lives ‌in the ​poems ‌as the ​common noun ‌for ​flame — ‌most ​memorably in ​the compound ‌surtalogi, ‌‘Surtr's flame’, ‌the ​fire of ​Múspellheimr that ‌ends ​the world: ​Vafþrúðnismál ‌51 keeps ​Víðarr and ​Váli ‌in the ​gods' ‌sanctuaries ‘when ​Surtr's flame ​dies ​down’ (þá ​er ‌sloknar surtalogi).[1]

‌The þulur, ​the ‌rhymed name-lists ​that ​travelled with ‌the Eddic ‌tradition ‌and were ‌copied ​with the ‌Prose Edda, ​keep ​logi among ​the ‌heiti — ​the poetic ​synonyms ​— for ‌fire. ​The picture ​is consistent ​across ‌the whole ​corpus: ‌Logi the ​person appears ‌once, ​in one ‌hall, ‌while logi ​the word ​burns ​quietly through ​the ‌poetry, from ‌hearth to ​Ragnarök.[2]

Sources

  1. Vafþrúðnismál 51 (Poetic Edda), trans. Carolyne Larrington, The Poetic Edda, 2nd ed. (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2014).
  2. The þulur (fire-heiti), appended to the Prose Edda, ed. Faulkes (1987).

Prose Edda

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The ​Prose Edda ‌is Logi's ‌sole narrative ​witness, ​and it ​gives him ​two distinct ​existences. ‌Gylfaginning 44–47 ​gives him ​his ‌scene: the ​call from ‌the bench, ​the ‌trough, the ​verdict that ​Loki ‘had ​lost ​the game’, ​and the ‌morning-after confession ​that ‌the eater ​was villieldr, ‌wildfire itself. ​Snorri's ‌craft deserves ​note — ‌the contest ​is ​the first ‌of the ‌evening's ‌trials, so ​the reader's ​eye is ‌trained ​early: nothing ​in this ‌hall is ​what ​it is ‌called.[1]

Skáldskaparmál ​gives him ‌his ​family: in ​the kennings ​for wind, ​Kári ‌is named ‌Fornjótr's son, ​‘his brothers ‌Hlér ​and Logi’ ​— the ‌wind ‌kin to ‌the sea ‌and the ​fire. ‌The same ​triad stands ​in the ​legendary ‌preface Hversu ​Noregr byggðist. ​Between the ​scene ‌and the ​genealogy, Snorri's ​sources present ​a ‌complete, if ​miniature, theology ‌of flame: ​an ‌element that ​can be ‌seated ‌at a ​bench, brothered ​to sea ​and ​wind, and ‌sent home ‌with the ​morning.[2]

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 44–47, trans. Faulkes (1987).
  2. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál (kennings for wind), trans. Faulkes (1987); Hversu Noregr byggðist (Flateyjarbók).

Runic Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The ​runic corpus ‌contains no ‌attestation ​of Logi ​— ​no inscription ​names the ​figure, ‌and none ​could ​be expected ‌to: he ​is ‌a literary ​personification, ‌not a ​cult deity. ​What ​the epigraphic ​record ​can offer ‌is orthographic. ​In ‌the Younger ​Futhark ‌of the ​Viking Age, ‌the ​name would ‌be ​carved approximately ​ᛚᚢᚴᛁ (l-u-k-i): ‌the ‌sixteen-rune row ‌writes ​both k ​and g ‌with ​ᚴ, so ​the ‌runes of ​Logi and ​Loki ‌are the ​same ‌— an ​epigraphic curiosity ​that ​has no ​bearing ‌on the ‌rejected etymological ​equation, ‌since runic ​orthography ​could not ‌register the ‌difference ‌between the ‌two ​words' gutturals.[1]

‌The common ​noun ​logi itself ​is ‌at home ​in the ​language ​the runestones ‌record; ​the mythology's ​gesture is ​only ‌to capitalize ​it. ‌No runic ​text, however, ‌preserves ​the personification ‌— ‌the name's ​witness remains ​the ​manuscript tradition ​of ‌the thirteenth ‌and fourteenth ​centuries.[2]

Sources

  1. Gordon, E. V., An Introduction to Old Norse, 2nd ed. rev. Taylor (Clarendon Press, 1957), on the Younger Futhark.
  2. Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide to the Gods, Heroes, Rituals, and Beliefs (Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. Logi.

Sagas & Medieval Sources

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Beyond ​the Eddas, ‌Logi's medieval ‌afterlife ​is genealogical. ​Hversu Noregr ​byggðist ​— ‘How ​Norway Was ‌Settled’, ​the legendary ​preface ‌preserved in ​the fourteenth-century ‌Flateyjarbók ​— sets ‌him at ​the ​head of ​northern legendary ​history: ​the primeval ‌giant Fornjótr, ​whose ‌name the ​poets use ‌as ​a byword ‌for ​the first ‌inhabitants of ​the ​far North, ‌has three ‌sons ‌— Hlér, ​‘whom we ​call ‌Ægir’, Logi, ​and Kári. ​The ‌text then ​unfolds the ​descendants ‌of the ​storm-brothers ‌into the ​legendary dynasties ​of ​Norway; fire ​himself has ‌no ‌further line, ​because fire ‌needs ​none.[1]

The ​euhemerizing histories ‌that ‌so freely ‌turned gods ‌into ​ancient kings ‌— ​Saxo's Gesta ​Danorum, the ​Ynglinga ‌tradition — ​leave Logi ​untouched: ​an element ‌resists euhemerism, ​there ​being no ​kingdom to ‌invent ​for flame. ‌His only ​other ‌medieval currency ​is ‌the word ‌itself, which ​the ​sagas use ​nightly without ​capital ‌letters, whenever ‌a hall ​burns.[2]

Sources

  1. Hversu Noregr byggðist (Flateyjarbók); cf. Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide (Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. Fornjótr, Logi.
  2. Cleasby & Vigfússon, An Icelandic-English Dictionary, 2nd ed. (Clarendon Press, 1957), s.v. logi.

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

Logi ​is the ‌mythology's ‌quietest ​lesson and ​one ​of ​its hardest: ​some ‌appetites are ​animal, ​and ‌some are ​elemental, ‌and ​the two ‌are ​not the ​same ​kind ​of thing. ​Loki, ‌the ​gods' own ‌hunger, ​eats well ‌— ​no ‌shame attaches ​to ‌losing ​as he ​lost. ‌But wildfire ‌does ‌not ​chew and ​does ‌not ​stop; it ​consumes ‌the meat, ​the ​bones, ‌and the ​furniture, ‌and ​it was ​never ​really competing.[1]

​The ‌North ‌kept that ​observation ‌in ​a common ​noun ‌and personified ‌it ‌once, ‌brilliantly, as ​the ‌unbeatable ​guest. Every ​household ​that has ‌watched ​a ​fire take ​the ‌beams ​as readily ​as ​the kindling ‌already ​knows ‌what Útgarða-Loki's ​hall ‌was ​built to ‌demonstrate: ‌you can ​out-eat ​any ​man, and ​you ‌cannot ‌out-eat a ​flame.

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 45–47, trans. Faulkes (1987).
