Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Arawn (arawn) ​— Otherworld, ‌Annwn · ‌Silver-tongued — ​is the king ​of Annwn, ​the Welsh ​Otherworld, in ​Pwyll Pendefig Dyfed, ‌the First ​Branch of ​the Mabinogi: ‌the lord ​whose white, red-eared ‌hounds Pwyll ​drives from ‌their kill ​at Glyn Cuch, ​and whose ​price for ​friendship is ​the strangest exchange ‌in Welsh ​prose — ‌a year ​and a day ‌in which ​each king ‌takes the ​other's shape, throne, ‌and bed, ​so that ​Pwyll may ‌fight Arawn's ‌enemy Hafgan at ‌the ford ​and prove ​his honor ‌where no one ​can see ​it.[1]

The ‌name's etymology ​is genuinely unsettled. ​The lexicon's ‌traditional gloss ​is 'silver-tongued'; ‌the handbooks record ​that the ​derivation is ​unknown — ​Ifor Williams proposed ‌a learned ‌borrowing of ​the biblical ‌Aaron, carried into ​Welsh by ​Latin learning, ‌and native ‌Celtic derivations ‌have also been ‌attempted, none ​commanding consensus.[2]

‌PuniCodex restores ​the name as ​Arawn — ​identical to ‌the ASCII ​input, because Middle ​Welsh writes ​the name ‌in plain ​Latin letters with ​no diacritic ​for the ‌restoration to ​recover; the name ‌is Tier ​2 by ‌the mechanical ​rule. No ‌domain is claimed: ‌the plain-ASCII ​arawn.com is ​registered to ​a third party ​outside the ‌collection and ‌is watched ​for release.[3]

Sources

  1. Davies, Sioned, trans., The Mabinogion (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2007), 'Pwyll Prince of Dyfed'; Welsh text ed. Ifor Williams, Pedeir Keinc y Mabinogi (Cardiff: University of Wales Press, 1930).
  2. Williams, Ifor, ed., Pedeir Keinc y Mabinogi (1930), notes; Koch, John T., ed., Celtic Culture: A Historical Encyclopedia (Santa Barbara: ABC-CLIO, 2006), s.v. Arawn.
  3. MacKillop, James, Dictionary of Celtic Mythology (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 1998), s.vv. Arawn, Annwn.

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

The theonym ​is attested in ‌Middle Welsh ‌as Arawn, king ​of Annwn, in ​the First Branch ​of the Mabinogi ​— a ​king with a ‌kingdom and no ​genealogy: the tale ​gives him ‌no epithet, no ​patronymic, and no ‌ancestry, as if ​the Otherworld's ‌lord needed no ​introduction beyond his ​crown.[1] The lexicon's ​traditional gloss is ​'silver-tongued', but ​the philology behind ‌the name is ​honestly unsettled. Ifor ‌Williams proposed ​that Arawn is ‌a learned borrowing ​of the biblical ‌Aaron, carried into ​Welsh through ‌Latin learning; native ​Celtic derivations have ​also been attempted, ‌and the ‌modern reference works ‌— Koch's Celtic ​Culture and the ​Geiriadur Prifysgol ‌Cymru — record ​the etymology as ​uncertain rather than ‌resolved.[2]

The ASCII ​form is ​not a compromise ‌here: Middle Welsh ​writes the name ‌in plain ​Latin letters, and ​the normalized spelling ​is already Arawn. ​There is no ‌diacritic and ‌no distinctive letter ​for the restoration ‌to recover; only ​the capital ​distinguishes the restored ‌form from the ‌input, which places ‌the name ‌in Tier 2 ​by the mechanical ‌rule.

The letter-by-letter ​transformation runs:

  • ​a → ​A — Same, ‌capitalized
  • r ​→ r — ​Same
  • ​a → a ‌— Same
  • ​w → w ​— Same
  • ​n → ‌n — Same

​The project holds ‌no domain for ​this name: ‌arawn.com is registered ​to a third ‌party outside the ‌PuniCodex collection ​and is watched ​for release. The ​temple is served ​at /arawn/ and ‌claims no ‌domain it does ​not hold.

Sources

  1. Davies, Sioned, trans., The Mabinogion (Oxford, 2007), 'Pwyll Prince of Dyfed'.
  2. Williams, Ifor, ed., Pedeir Keinc y Mabinogi (1930), notes; Geiriadur Prifysgol Cymru / A Dictionary of the Welsh Language (Cardiff: University of Wales Press, 1950–2002), s.v. Arawn; Koch, Celtic Culture (2006), s.v. Arawn.

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The pronunciation ​of the ‌name is ‌/aˈraʊn/ — Modern ​Welsh.[1]

Phoneme ​by phoneme:

​- A- — ​Unstressed short ​open [a], ‌the light first ​syllable; Welsh ​stress has ‌not yet fallen
​- -raw- ‌— Trilled ​or tapped [r] ‌followed by ​the Welsh ​diphthong aw [aʊ], ​the stressed ​penult that ​carries the whole ‌weight of ​the name
‌- -n — ​Final alveolar ‌nasal [n], ​closing the stressed ‌syllable cleanly

​For the ‌modern speaker, the ​closest approximation ​is: 'a-ROWN' ‌— the aw ‌sounds like ‌the 'ow' ​of 'town', and ​the stress ‌falls on ​the second syllable, ​the Welsh ‌penult.

Kindred ​and historical forms ​of the ‌name:

  • ​Middle Welsh ‌— Arawn, king ​of Annwn ​in Pwyll ​Pendefig Dyfed, the ​First Branch ‌of the ‌Mabinogi
  • Possible ​biblical loan ‌— Aaron, ​the derivation proposed ​by Ifor ‌Williams and ‌registered as a ‌possibility by ‌the modern ​handbooks
  • Modern ‌Welsh — ​Arawn, a ​rare but living ​given name

‌- Welsh ​folklore — master ​of the ​Cŵn Annwn, ‌the white red-eared ​hounds of ​the Otherworld, ​a folk elaboration ‌of his ​Mabinogi pack[2]

‌Arawn is Tier ​2 because ‌the restoration ​preserves nothing ASCII ‌loses: the ‌letters of ​the manuscript form ​are the ​letters of ​the keyboard. The ‌capital alone ‌marks the ​temple form.

Sources

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

The name ​is preserved ‌in Middle ‌Welsh manuscripts in ​the Latin ​alphabet as ​Arawn — Middle ​Welsh (Brythonic ​Celtic), attested ‌in the White ​Book of ​Rhydderch (c. ‌1350) and ​the Red Book ‌of Hergest ​(c. 1382–1410), ‌recording material composed ​centuries earlier.[1] ​The script ​is written left-to-right ​in the ​medieval Welsh ‌bookhand.

The scholarly ​rendering proceeds ‌step by ​step:

  • Middle ‌Welsh writes ​the name ‌in plain Latin ​letters; the ‌digraph aw ​represents the diphthong ​[aʊ] that ‌Modern Welsh ‌still keeps.

‌- No diacritic ​attaches to ​the name ‌in any witness: ​normalized Middle ​Welsh orthography ‌and the ASCII ​spelling are ​identical.

  • ‌The etymology is ​unsettled — ‌the biblical ​Aaron (Ifor Williams's ​proposal) and ​native Celtic ​derivations are both ‌on record ‌— but ​no variant spelling ‌of the ​theonym is ​attested in ‌the manuscripts.[2]
  • ‌The Unicode ‌restoration Arawn ‌therefore changes nothing ​but the ‌capital; the ​name stands in ​Tier 2 ​by the ‌mechanical rule, and ​the plain-ASCII ​domain arawn.com ​— held by ‌a third ​party outside ​the PuniCodex collection ​and watched ‌for release ​— is the ‌philologically complete ​form.

One ‌caution attaches ​to the record: ‌the gloss ‌'silver-tongued' is ​the lexicon's traditional ​rendering, not ​a settled ​philological result, and ‌this edition ‌presents it ​as such.[2]

Sources

  1. Davies, Sioned, trans., The Mabinogion (Oxford, 2007), introduction, on the manuscript witnesses.
  2. Williams, Ifor, ed., Pedeir Keinc y Mabinogi (1930), notes; Geiriadur Prifysgol Cymru, s.v. Arawn; Koch, Celtic Culture (2006), s.v. Arawn.

Domains & Attributes

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sphere of influence, titles, epithets, domain cards.

Arawn is ​catalogued under the ‌domain 'Otherworld, Annwn' ‌— one domain ​in two words, ​the place and ​what it is.[1]

​### Annwn

Annwn ​(later Annwfn) is ‌the Welsh Otherworld: ​not a land ​of the dead ‌in the classical ​sense but a ‌world beside this ​one — richer, ‌stranger, and reachable ​at its borders, ​with its own ​kings, its own ​hunts, and its ​own war. The ‌First Branch ​knows it divided ‌into two kingdoms, ​Arawn's and Hafgan's, ‌until Pwyll makes ​them one.[1] The ‌lexicon keeps its ​own temple for ‌the realm itself ​at Annwn.

​The Otherworld Hunt

‌Arawn enters the ‌tale hunting: his ‌pack of hounds ​shining white with ​red ears — ‌the colour the ​Welsh tradition fixed ​on Otherworld creatures ‌— brings down ​a stag in ​Pwyll's own hunting-ground ‌at Glyn Cuch. ​The hounds are ‌Annwn made visible; ​the insult Pwyll ​offers them is ​what sets the ​whole exchange in ‌motion.[1]

Kingship ‌Held in Trust

​Arawn's answer to ‌the insult is ​to lend a ​stranger his entire ‌identity: face, throne, ‌court, and bed ‌for a year ‌and a day. ​Kingship, in his ‌hands, is a ​trust that can ​be handed over ​and returned — ‌and the ​tale measures both ​kings by how ​they carry what ‌is not theirs.[2]

​### Honor

The ​exchange is a ​test with no ‌witness: what Pwyll ​does in Arawn's ‌bed, in Arawn's ​shape, no one ‌in either world ​can see. The ‌First Branch builds ‌its definition of ​honor on exactly ​that darkness — ​and Arawn, who ​devised the test, ‌is the god ‌of the standard ​it sets.[2]

Sources

  1. Davies, Sioned, trans., The Mabinogion (Oxford, 2007), 'Pwyll Prince of Dyfed'.
  2. Mac Cana, Proinsias, The Mabinogi, 2nd ed. (Cardiff: University of Wales Press, 1992), on the First Branch as the proving of Pwyll.

Symbols

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Iconography, attributes, and their meanings.

Arawn's ​regalia are ‌not ‌weapons but ​conditions ​— ​the working ​parts ​of the ‌one ​great test:[1]

​- ‌The white ​hounds ‌with ​red ears ‌— ​The colour ​of ​the Otherworld: ​a ​hunt that ‌arrives ​from ‌the far ​side ‌of the ​boundary
‌- The ​exchanged ‌shape — ​Kingship ​as ‌a trust ‌held ‌for another: ​the ​year Arawn's ‌face ​ruled two ​realms
‌- ​The single ​blow ‌— Restraint ​as ‌the condition ​of ​victory: ​strike once, ​or ‌not at ‌all
​- The ‌untouched ​bed — ​Honor ‌kept ‌in the ‌dark, ‌where no ​one ‌would ever ​have ​known

  • ​The ‌ford ​— The ​meeting-place ​between realms, ‌where ​debts are ​paid ​and blows ‌are ​counted

‌- The ​yearly ‌gifts — ​Friendship ‌renewed in ‌kind: ​horses and ​greyhounds ​and ​hawks, and ‌every ‌kind of ​treasure[1]

07

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

Arawn's whole dossier is ​one story — the opening movement of the ‌Mabinogi — told with the economy and exactness ‌that mark the Four Branches at their best.[1]

​### The Wrong at Glyn Cuch (Pwyll Pendefig ​Dyfed)

Pwyll, lord of Dyfed, goes hunting at ​Glyn Cuch, and there he sees a sight ​he has never seen: a pack of hounds ​shining white with red ears bringing down a ‌stag. He drives them off and sets his ​own hounds on the kill — and ​the stranger who rides up is Arawn, king ‌of Annwn, and it was his hunt. Arawn ​names the discourtesy plainly and refuses revenge; what ‌he asks instead is amends. He has a ​rival, Hafgan, king of the other realm of ‌Annwn, who wars on him without end: let ​Pwyll take Arawn's shape and his place for ​a year and a day, and meet Hafgan ​at the ford at the year's turning.[1]

​The Exchange of Shapes (Pwyll Pendefig Dyfed)

​Each king takes the other's form and goes ‌to the other's court, and neither realm suspects. ​Arawn rules Dyfed so well that there had ‌never been so good a year in it. ​Pwyll enters Annwn in Arawn's likeness, and that ‌first night he is led to the bed ​of Arawn's wife — the fairest and most ‌comely woman he had ever seen. He turns ​his back to her, and he does ‌so every night of the year: courteous by ​day, and in the bed he never once ​turns his face toward her. The queen never ‌knows. The whole test of the tale is ‌here, in the dark, where no one could ‌have seen the difference.[1]

The Single Blow ​at the Ford (Pwyll Pendefig Dyfed)

Arawn's instructions ​were exact: Hafgan — the name suggests 'Summer-White' ‌— can be slain by one blow and ​only one; strike him a second time ​and he will rise as strong as before ‌and fight again the next day. At the ​appointed time the two kings meet at the ​ford, and at the first clash Pwyll strikes ‌Hafgan one blow that shears through shield and ​armour. Hafgan begs for the finishing stroke, and ‌Pwyll refuses: the blow he gave is all ​Hafgan will have from him. Hafgan's nobles declare ​their king lost, and Pwyll takes the submission ​and hostages of Hafgan's realm, so that ​the two kingdoms of Annwn are made one ‌under Arawn's crown.[1]

The Restoration and the ‌Title (Pwyll Pendefig Dyfed)

The two kings meet ​again at the ford, each in his own ‌shape, and embrace and go home. Arawn's queen, ​rejoicing at his return, remarks that he has ​not spoken to her as he does tonight ‌for a whole year — and Arawn praises ‌God for a friend so true. In Dyfed, ‌Pwyll's court hears the story and tells ‌him he did well to make such a ​friend. From that time the two realms send ‌each other gifts every year: horses and greyhounds ​and hawks, and every kind of treasure. And ​because Pwyll had ruled Annwn and joined its ​two kingdoms in one, he was called from ‌then on Pwyll Pen Annwn — Pwyll Head ​of Annwn — in place of Pwyll lord ​of Dyfed. The friendship he bought back ​is the foundation on which the whole of ‌the First Branch, and the coming of Rhiannon, ​is built.[1]

The Spoils of Annwn (Book ​of Taliesin)

The Mabinogi never brings Arawn back ​on stage, but his world keeps growing. The ‌poem Preiddeu Annwfn, 'The Spoils of Annwn', preserved ​in the Book of Taliesin, sends Arthur's men ‌to raid the Otherworld for the cauldron of ​the Head of Annwfn — Pen Annwfn, the ‌very title the First Branch gives Pwyll's ​honour — and only seven come home. The ‌poem never names Annwn's king, and honest scholarship ‌leaves it unnamed; the echo of the title ​is the only thread between the two texts.[2] ​Later Welsh folklore knows the Cŵn Annwn, the ​Hounds of Annwn — white with red ears, ​exactly Arawn's pack — whose baying across the ‌night sky was heard as a death-omen; folk ‌tradition gave their mastery to Gwyn ap Nudd ​rather than to Arawn.[3]

Sources

  1. Davies, Sioned, trans., The Mabinogion (Oxford, 2007), 'Pwyll Prince of Dyfed'; Welsh text ed. Williams, Pedeir Keinc y Mabinogi (1930).
  2. Haycock, Marged, ed. and trans., Legendary Poems from the Book of Taliesin (Aberystwyth: CMCS, 2007), 'Preideu Annwfyn'.
  3. MacKillop, James, Dictionary of Celtic Mythology (Oxford, 1998), s.vv. Cŵn Annwn, Gwyn ap Nudd.

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

Arawn's ​comparisons are ‌almost all ‌inward, and ​the Welsh ​corpus itself ​supplies them. ​His clearest ​rival for ‌the kingship ​of the ​Otherworld ‌is Gwyn ​ap Nudd, ‌whom Culhwch ​ac Olwen ‌says God ​set over ​the demons ​of Annwn ​— a ​second king ‌of ​Annwn in ‌a second ​text, and ‌the folklore ​gave Gwyn ‌the hounds ​the Mabinogi ‌gave Arawn.[1] ​The structural ​reading runs ‌deeper: ‌commentators since ‌Mac Cana ​have read ​the Annwn ‌adventure as ​the proving ​of Pwyll's ‌character before ​the coming ​of Rhiannon ‌— ​the hero ‌tested in ​honor and ​restraint in ​the Otherworld ​before the ‌Otherworld's queen ‌chooses him ​in this ‌one.[2]

The ​name ​itself may ‌be the ‌tale's one ‌foreign guest: ‌Ifor Williams ​proposed that ‌Arawn is ​a learned ​borrowing of ​the biblical ‌Aaron, ​carried into ​Welsh by ​Latin learning, ‌and the ​handbooks register ​the possibility ​without deciding ‌it.[3] No ​Gaulish or ‌Irish counterpart ​of ‌Arawn has ​ever been ‌convincingly proposed; ‌the exchange-of-kings ​motif, with ​its unseen ​test and ​its kept ‌bed, is ‌the Mabinogi's ​own.

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

Lady ​Charlotte Guest's ‌translation of ‌the ​Mabinogion (1838–49) ​carried ​Arawn out ​of the ​White ‌and Red ​Books ​and into ‌English, and ​the ‌white red-eared ​hounds entered ‌the ​stock of ​Celtic ​imagery from ​that day.[1] ​In ‌the twentieth ​century ‌the figure ​found his ‌widest ​audience inverted: ‌Lloyd ​Alexander's Chronicles ‌of Prydain ​(1964–68) ​made 'Arawn ‌Death-Lord of ‌Annuvin' ‌the arch-villain ​of ​five children's ‌novels, and ​Disney's ​The Black ‌Cauldron ​(1985) kept ​the dark-lord ‌version ​on screen ‌— a ​long ​way from ​the ​most honorable ‌king in ‌Welsh ​prose, but ‌the ​reason the ​name is ‌known ‌worldwide.[2]

The ‌Cŵn Annwn ‌meanwhile ​never left ‌Welsh ​folklore, still ​baying as ​a ‌death-omen in ​folk ​memory into ​the modern ‌period.[3] ​Modern Druid ​and ​pagan practice ‌honours Arawn ​as ‌lord of ​the Otherworld ‌and ​patron of ‌kept ‌faith, and ​the name ​survives ​in Wales ​as ‌a rare ‌living given ​name.

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

Arawn ​is a ‌textual figure: ‌no inscription, ​votive deposit, ​or cult ​site names ​him, and ​the epigraphic ‌record of ​early Wales ​— the ‌Latin and ​ogham memorial ‌stones of ​roughly the ‌fifth to ​seventh centuries ​— records ​personal names ​in funerary ​formulae, not ‌theonyms.[1] His ​evidence is ‌manuscript evidence. ​The Four ‌Branches survive ​complete in ‌the White ​Book of ‌Rhydderch (Aberystwyth, ​NLW Peniarth ​MSS 4–5, ‌c. 1350) ‌and the ‌Red Book ​of Hergest ​(Oxford, Jesus ‌College MS ​111, c. ​1382–1410), with ‌earlier fragments ​in Peniarth ​6; the ‌tales' date ​of composition ‌is argued ​rather than ​documented — ​Ifor Williams ​placed them ‌in the ‌later eleventh ​century, and ‌current scholarship ​generally holds ​a range ‌around 1060–1120.[2]

‌The geography ‌of the ‌tale is ​real: Glyn ‌Cuch, where ​the insult ​happens, is ​identified with ‌the valley ​of the ​River Cych ​on the ‌Pembrokeshire–Carmarthenshire border, ​inside the ​Dyfed the ​story governs.[3] ‌The nearest ​material echo ‌of the ​Otherworld hunt ‌is literary: ​the Book ‌of Taliesin, ‌the codex ​that preserves ​Preiddeu Annwfn, ​is a ​fourteenth-century Welsh ‌manuscript (NLW ‌Peniarth MS ​2).[4]

Irish Mythological Cycles

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Arawn has ​no Irish namesake and ‌no Irish reflex — ‌but the world he ​rules is the same ​institution the Irish tradition ​calls the síd. Annwn ​and the síd are ​cognate constructions: a ‌world beside this one, ​richer and stranger, entered ​at its borders, with ‌kings of its own ​who hunt, feast, make ‌war on one another, ​and treat with the ‌kings of this ​world as equals.[1] The ​Irish parallels to Arawn ​himself are not names ​but offices: Manannán mac ​Lir, lord of Emain ‌Ablach and Mag Mell, ​who rides between worlds, ‌changes his shape ​at will, and lends ‌treasures and favors to ​mortal kings, is the ‌nearest structural counterpart — ​an Otherworld sovereign whose ‌dealings with this world ​are courteous, conditional, and ​always a test.[2]

‌The shape-exchange at the ‌heart of the First ‌Branch has its Irish ​cognates in the transformation-and-imposture ​machinery of the Mythological ‌Cycle: the disguises and ​shape-shifting contests of Cath ​Maige Tuired, whose ‌introduction reads the tale's ​Otherworld figures against the ​wider Celtic pattern, and ‌the sovereignty encounters in ​which an otherworldly woman ‌tests and confers kingship.[3] ​Mac Cana's structural comparison ​runs deepest: he ​reads the First Branch's ​economy of honor — ‌the debt at Glyn ‌Cuch, the year of ​the kept bed, the ‌single measured blow — ​against the Irish sovereignty ​tales, in which ‌a hero's worth is ‌proved in dealings with ‌the Otherworld before kingship ‌is confirmed, and finds ​the two traditions working ‌the same moral grammar ​from opposite sides.[4]

Even ​the hounds have ​their Irish hunt: the ‌Cŵn Annwn, white with ​red ears, match the ​Irish slua sí and ​the fairy hunt of ‌the síd-riders — the ​pale, red-eared Otherworld pack ​heard coursing at ​night in both insular ‌traditions, a shared Celtic ​index of the neighboring ‌world rather than a ​borrowing in either direction.[2] ‌Sims-Williams's survey of Irish ​influence on Welsh literature ‌cautions exactly here: ‌the parallels are real, ​but they are the ​residue of a common ​inheritance, and the First ​Branch's exchange-of-kings, with its ‌unseen test and kept ‌faith, remains the Mabinogi's ​own construction.[1]

Welsh Sources

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Arawn's whole ​narrative dossier ‌is the First ‌Branch of the ​Mabinogi, Pwyll ​Pendefig Dyfed — ​the hunting-debt at ​Glyn Cuch, ​the exchange of ‌shapes, the ​year of the ​untouched bed, the ‌single blow ​at the ford, ‌and the friendship ​sealed in ‌yearly gifts. He ​appears nowhere ​else in the ​Four Branches: after ​the title ​Pen Annwfn passes ‌to Pwyll, the ​tale turns ‌to Rhiannon, and ​Arawn's part ‌is done.[1]

Around ​that single spine ‌the wider ​Welsh tradition builds ‌the world he ​rules. Preiddeu ​Annwfn, 'The Spoils ‌of Annwn', ‌in the Book ‌of Taliesin, sends ​Arthur's men ​to raid the ‌Otherworld for the ​cauldron of ​the Head of ‌Annwfn — ​Pen Annwfn, the ​very title of ‌the First ​Branch — and ‌only seven come ​home; the ​poem never names ​Annwn's king.[2] ​Culhwch ac Olwen ‌gives Annwn a ‌second lord: ​Gwyn ap Nudd, ‌over whom God ​set the ​spirits of the ‌demons of ‌Annwn, and it ‌is Gwyn, not ‌Arawn, whom ​later folklore made ‌master of the ​Cŵn Annwn, ​the white red-eared ​hounds heard ‌baying as a ​death-omen.[3] The tales ​survive complete ​in the White ‌Book of Rhydderch ​(c. 1350) ​and the Red ​Book of ‌Hergest (c. 1382–1410), ​with earlier fragments ‌in Peniarth ​6; Lady Charlotte ‌Guest's translation (1838–49) ​first carried ‌them into English, ‌Ifor Williams's ​edition (1930) fixed ​the standard Welsh ​text, and ​Sioned Davies's translation ‌(2007) is the ‌current scholarly ​English version.[4]

Inscriptions & Seals

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

No ​inscription names ‌Arawn, ‌and none ​was ​to be ​expected. ​The early ​inscribed ‌stones of ​Wales ​— the ‌Latin ​and ogham ‌memorial ​stones of ‌roughly ​the fifth ​to ​seventh centuries ​— ​record personal ‌names ​in ‌funerary formulae, ​not ‌theonyms; votive ​epigraphy ‌of the ​Welsh ‌kind simply ​does ​not survive, ‌and ‌the theonymic ‌record ​of pre-Christian ​Wales ‌is known ​from ​literature and ‌place-lore ​rather than ​stone.[1]

‌There is ​no ‌continental contrast ​to ​set against ​the ​silence, as ‌there ‌is for ​some ‌Celtic figures: ​no ​Gaulish altar, ‌no ‌Romano-British dedication, ‌no ‌ogham stone ​has ‌ever been ​proposed ​as naming ​Arawn ‌or ​any plausible ​antecedent ​of the ‌name. ​The king ​of ​Annwn is ‌a ​creature of ‌the ​Welsh bookhand, ‌and ​his dossier ‌— ‌manuscript, tale, ​and ​folklore — ​is ​complete without ‌a ‌single carved ​letter.[2]

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

Arawn ​sets the ‌one test ‌that ​cannot be ​cheated: ​he gives ​a stranger ​his ‌face, his ​throne, and ​his ‌bed, and ​waits a ‌year ​to learn ‌what ​the stranger ​is. Everything ​Pwyll ​does in ​Annwn is ‌invisible ​— no ‌one in ​either ‌kingdom can ​tell ‌the kings ​apart, and ‌no ​one will ​ever know ‌what ‌passed in ‌the bed ​unless ​the doer ‌confesses ​it. That ​is exactly ‌the ​point. Honor, ​the First ‌Branch ​argues, is ‌what a ​man ​does when ​the ​only witness ‌is himself.[1]

‌The ​tale rewards ‌it without ​sentimentality: ​the kept ‌faith becomes ‌an ‌unbroken friendship, ‌an ​alliance across ‌the boundary ​of ​the worlds, ​the title ‌Pen ​Annwfn, and ​a court ​that ‌tells its ​lord ​he chose ​his friend ‌well. ​Arawn's kingdom ‌is the ​far ‌side of ​reality, but ‌his ‌question is ​asked ​everywhere — ​who are ​you ‌when no ‌one is ​watching?

Sources

  1. Davies, Sioned, trans., The Mabinogion (Oxford, 2007), 'Pwyll Prince of Dyfed'.
