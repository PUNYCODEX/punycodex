From Greek to Unicode: the journey of Éris

Long before it was a domain, this name traveled through scripts. Éris begins in Greek, passes through scholarly transliteration, and ends — for now — inside the punycode machinery of the global DNS. Each stage of that journey preserves some information and loses some, and the craft of restoration is knowing exactly which marks matter. This post follows the name stage by stage: the original script, the reconstructed pronunciation, the mythological record, the material evidence, and finally the Unicode form that carries all of it into the address bar. Think of it as a biography of a name, told through its spelling.

Overview

Éris (eris) — Strife, Discord · Strife — belongs to the Greek tradition, where it is catalogued under the domain "Strife, Discord". The name means "Strife".

Éris is strife personified — the quarrel, the feud, the rivalry that Greeks both cursed and quietly praised. Born of Nyx, Night herself, she is the one goddess who was never asked to the feast and who therefore changed the world. Yet Hesiod insists there are two Erises: the hateful one who sows war, and the praiseworthy one who stirs even the idle to work. She is the most honest deity in the pantheon — the Greeks' name for a force they could not escape and could not entirely disown.

The plain ASCII form eris survives as a modern convenience imposed by the early domain-name system; the restoration, not the fallback, is the form the project defends as philologically complete.

The Name

The name is attested in Greek as Ἔρις. Etymologically it means "Strife".

The ASCII form eris survives only because the early domain-name system could not carry diacritics; it is a technological compromise, not an ancient spelling. The Unicode restoration Éris recovers the stress accent of the original directly in the address bar. The original preserves one prosodic feature — stress or vowel length — rather than both, which places the name in Tier 2.

The letter-by-letter transformation runs:

  • e → É — Acute on e
  • r → r — r same
  • i → i — i same
  • s → s — s same

The project holds the domain éris.com (xn--ris-9la.com) as the canonical home of this name.

The Original Script

The name is written in Greek as Ἔρις. This original script is the form against which the ASCII fallback eris and the PuniCodex restoration Éris are measured: the restoration preserves its pitch accent of the written form, so that a reader typing the modern address still speaks the ancient name.

Pronunciation

The reconstructed pronunciation of the name is /é.ris/ — Attic Greek Reconstruction.

Phoneme by phoneme:

  • É- — the stressed opening syllable: short e bearing the acute accent — in the living accent system, a raised musical pitch rather than a heavy stress
  • -ris — trilled or tapped rhōtic r, short i, final voiceless s

For the modern speaker, the closest approximation is: 'EH-riss' — two quick syllables with the high note forward; nothing like the English slurring of 'heiress' or 'Eris' rhymed with 'ferrous'

Kindred and historical forms of the name:

  • Greek — ἔρις (eris), 'strife, quarrel, rivalry' — the goddess is the common noun made flesh, like Kháos or Némēsis
  • PIE — uncertain; older scholarship tied it to ὀρίνω (orínō), 'to stir, rouse', but Beekes judges the formation non-Indo-European — a Pre-Greek inheritance, fitting for a daughter of Night

Tier 2, accent-preserving. Ἔρις carries the acute accent on a short initial vowel but no long vowel (η/ω) and no circumflex; the restoration Éris preserves exactly one feature the ASCII 'Eris' loses — the stress — and so the temple stands at Tier 2.

Mythology

Éris has no temples and almost no cult — her mythology is her meaning. Every surviving story is an anatomy of strife itself: where it comes from, whom it feeds, and how it grows.

Born of Night, Mother of Calamities (Genealogy)

In the Theogony (224–232) Hesiod makes Éris a child of Nyx, Night, who bears her without father among the other dark abstractions — Death, Sleep, Blame, Woe. Éris then breeds her own terrible brood, the Kakodaimones: Toil (Ponos), Forgetfulness, Famine, Pains, Combats, Battles, Murders, Manslaughters, Quarrels, Lies, Disputes, Lawlessness, Ruin (Atē), and Oath, who 'troubles most of all men on earth when anyone deliberately swears falsely'. The genealogy is a single claim stated plainly: from one quarrel, every misery descends.

Hesiod's Correction (The Two Erises)

The Works and Days (11–26) opens by amending the poet's own Theogony: 'there was not one kind of Strife after all — on earth there are two.' The first, the elder, men blame, for she 'fosters evil war and slaughter'. The second, whom Zeus set in the roots of the earth, is much better for mortals: she rouses even the shiftless to work, for 'potter rivals potter, carpenter carpenter; beggar envies beggar, and poet poet'. Here Éris is split in two — envy as destroyer and emulation as the engine of all human striving — one of the earliest pieces of moral reflection in Greek literature.

The Golden Apple of the Hesperides (The Wedding Feast)

At the wedding of Peleus and Thetis, all the gods were feasted — except Éris, whose very nature barred her from the invitation list. The Cypria, as summarized in Proclus' Chrestomathy, tells that she came anyway and threw among the guests a golden apple 'to be a prize of beauty', inscribed kallistēi — 'for the fairest'. Hera, Athēnâ, and Aphrodítē each claimed it; the dispute was sent for judgment to Paris on Mount Ida, and from that judgment, Proclus says flatly, came the Trojan War. Hyginus (Fabulae 92) retells the same scene. It is the founding myth of discord: exclusion breeds the quarrel that topples a kingdom.

Small at First, Then Filling the Sky (On the Battlefield)

Homer's most haunting personification of war comes in Iliad 4 (440–445), as the two armies close: among them walks Éris, 'the sister and companion of man-slaying Ares — she rears only a small crest at first, but then she fixes her head in the heavens while her feet walk the earth', hurling bitterness into the midst of both hosts. She reappears on the shield of Achilles (Iliad 18.535–540), bloody-robed among the wounded and the dead. The image is precise: discord begins as a murmur and ends with its head in the clouds — and once she stands, no god can pull her down.

Symbols & Iconography

The iconography associated with Éris concentrates in a small set of recurring attributes, each a compressed statement about the name:

  • The Golden Apple — The apple of the Hesperides that Éris rolled among the wedding guests of Peleus and Thetis; the Cypria tradition (preserved in Proclus' summary) makes it the seed of the Trojan War — a single piece of fruit as the first cause of the greatest war in Greek memory.
  • The Inscription 'Kallistēi' — 'For the fairest' (τῇ καλλίστῃ) — the words cut into the apple itself. The inscription, not the fruit, is the weapon: a superlative with three claimants is a quarrel engineered in miniature.
  • Viper-Tressed Hair — Virgil gives the Roman Discordia serpent-entwined locks bound with blood-drenched fillets (Aeneid 6.280, 'Discordia demens, vipereum crinem vittis innexa cruentis'), kin to the Furies — strife wearing the visual grammar of vengeance.
  • The Swelling Stature — In Iliad 4 (440–445) Éris is 'small at first, but then she fixes her head in heaven while her feet walk the earth' — her growing body is her emblem: discord is always born tiny.
  • Place beside Árēs — Homer calls her 'sister and companion of man-slaying Ares' (Iliad 4.441), and on the shield of Achilles she strides through battle beside Tumult and Doom, clutching the wounded and the dead (Iliad 18.535–540). Her attribute is her company.

Éris is nearly invisible in archaic art — an absence that is itself the point of her myth.

  • The golden apple (χρυσοῦν μῆλον, 'for the fairest') — her defining attribute and the token she bequeaths to every Judgement of Paris scene from the sixth century onward.
  • The bloodstained attendant of the melee — on the shield of Achilles she is 'glutted with men's blood,' dragging one wounded man alive and one corpse by the feet through the throng (Iliad 18.535-538): the archaic blueprint for all later images of her.
  • The uninvited absence — on the Sophilos dinos (British Museum 1971,1101.1) and the François Vase (Florence, Museo Archeologico Nazionale 4209) the inscribed gods file in procession to the wedding of Peleus and Thetis; Éris alone is missing, because she alone was not asked.
  • The winged watcher — on the Attic red-figure hydria attributed to the Karlsruhe Paris Painter (Karlsruhe, Badisches Landesmuseum 259) a winged figure labelled ΕΡΙΣ hovers above the seated Paris, presiding over the contest she began.
  • A small corpus — her ancient dossier is thin and dominated by the Judgement; the standard catalogue is LIMC III.1 (1986), s.v. 'Eris.'

Epithets & Cult Titles

Éris carries no cult titles — she had no cult to mint them — but epic gives her a compact dossier of epithets that fixes her nature with unusual precision.

  • Ἄρεος ἀδελφή (Áreos adelphḗ) — 'sister of Árēs'; her standing relation, Iliad 4.440.
  • ἄμοτον μεμαυῖα (ámoton memauîa) — 'eager insatiably'; Iliad 4.440, the words that walk her onto the battlefield.
  • κρατερή (kraterḗ) — 'mighty'; Iliad 20.48, where she enters the theomachy paired with Enyṓ.
  • ἐΰς (eus) — 'baneful, hostile'; Iliad 11.4, as she goes down to the ships 'bearing the baneful portent of war.'
  • στυγερή (stygerḗ) — 'hateful, loathed'; Theogony 226, where Night's daughter bears Toil, Famine, and the Slaughters.
  • μεμπτή / ἐπαινητή (memptḗ / epainētḗ) — 'the blameworthy' / 'the praiseworthy'; the twin Erides of Works and Days 11-26, antiquity's only attempt to divide her.

The Homeric Hymns

No hymn to Éris survives — not in the Homeric collection, not among the Orphic hymns, not in Callimachus. The absence is itself a datum: the hymnic repertory honours gods who receive cult, and Strife received none. What she has instead is battlefield poetry. Homer gives her two of the Iliad's most arresting apparitions. At Iliad 4.440-445 she marches beside her brother Árēs, 'eager insatiably,' small at first until she props her head against the sky while her feet still walk the earth. At 11.3-14 Zeus posts her on Odysseus's black ship, where she looses the 'great piercing cry' that makes war sweeter to the Achaeans than the voyage home. Hesiod completes the portrait twice over: once as daughter of Night in the Theogony, once in the Works and Days as two Erides, the blameworthy and the praiseworthy. The silence of the hymnic tradition confirms what these passages say — Éris is a power the poets saw everywhere and worshipped nowhere.

Oracle Sites & Sanctuaries

Éris has no oracle, no altar, no temple — and Pausanias, who catalogues the shrines of Greece valley by valley, records no cult of Strife anywhere in his Description of Greece. Her only sanctuary is a sentence. Hesiod plants her in the fields of Ascra, where the bad Strife stirs wars and the good one stirs rivalry — 'potter resents potter, and builder builder' — a cult of emulation with ploughs instead of libations. The famous 'cult of Strife' is literary: the uninvited goddess of the Cypria who rolls the golden apple 'for the fairest' across the wedding feast of Peleus and Thetis, an episode preserved for us in Proclus's summary of the epic and in Apollodorus. The modern devotees who claim her — Renaissance allegorists of Discordia, twentieth-century Discordians — are heirs of that apple, not of any ancient rite. A temple that tells the truth about Éris must say it plainly: she was feared, described, and blamed, but never worshipped.

Archaeology & Evidence

Éris is the rare Olympian-circled figure with no attested temple, sanctuary, or cult anywhere in the Greek world — her existence is literary, and that absence is itself the archaeological fact. Her iconographic life is confined almost entirely to Judgment of Paris scenes: Pausanias (5.18–19), describing the Archaic chest of Cypselus at Olympia, records the wedding of Peleus and the judgment episode in which Éris and her golden apple were among the oldest identifiable figures of Greek myth in art. Roman Discordia survives mainly as the poets' personification; no temple of Discordia is recorded in the city of Rome.

Across Cultures

Rome adopted her as Discordia, and the Roman poets made her darker still. Ennius opened his account of the outbreak of the Second Punic War with her: 'Discordia taetra belli ferratos postis portasque refregit' — 'when foul Discord burst the iron-bound posts and gates of war' (Annales, fr. 225 Skutsch), an image Virgil deliberately echoes at the underworld's entrance in Aeneid 6.280, where Discordia demens stands with viper-entwined, blood-bound hair. She never acquired the civic cult that other translations did — Rome, like Greece, preferred to name strife rather than worship it. Her modern afterlife is stranger and more literal than most: the Discordian movement of the 1960s made her its patroness, and in 2006 the dwarf planet whose discovery provoked the reclassification of Pluto was formally named Eris — a fitting tribute, the IAU noted, to the goddess of strife and discord.

Within the Greek tradition, closely related names in the corpus include Achérōn, Achilleús, Adámas, Aḗr, Aithḗr, and Anánkē.

Cultural Legacy

Her Roman name survives everywhere in English: discord, discordant, discordance, and the idiom 'apple of discord' for the object of a quarrel — a phrase that walked straight out of the Cypria into the modern tongues of Europe. Discordianism's Principia Discordia canonized her as the counter-goddess of an orderly world, and the dwarf planet Eris carries her name in every astronomy textbook, so the 'tenth planet' that demoted Pluto keeps her quarrel alive in the heavens. Among the Olympian circle she remains what she always was: the goddess nobody worships and everybody obeys.

A Meditation

Éris exposes something unusual about Greek religious thought: its willingness to personify what it disapproved of. She has essentially no cult — no sanctuary, no festival, no priesthood — yet the poets cannot write a war without her. Hesiod's two Erises show the tradition arguing with itself: strife is the curse of mankind and, simultaneously, the condition of every human excellence, for without rivalry the potter never improves his pot. The apple myth extends the insight into politics: the quarrel was not caused by wickedness but by a superlative, by the mere existence of 'fairest' among three goddesses. To the Greeks, Éris was less a villain than a diagnosis — the recognition that conflict is not an interruption of the cosmic order but one of its permanent residents, daughter of the Night that precedes all things.

The Unicode Restoration

Éris is classified as Tier 2: the original carries only one of the two prosodic features — stress or vowel length — not both. The ASCII fallback eris still resolves everywhere, but it is the restored form that carries the name's full information. Across the 4 characters of the name, the restoration adjusts 1: 1 mark of stress (É). That is the whole thesis of this temple: the marks are the message.

The Domain Name

The restored name is live as a working domain: éris.com, which the DNS carries in punycode form as xn--ris-9la.com — an ASCII-compatible encoding that lets a non-ASCII name travel the global network without breaking older infrastructure. The visitor sees Éris; the machines see the encoding. That duality is the engineering compromise on which the entire restoration rests, and it is why a name written the way its own tradition wrote it in Greek can now be typed into any browser on earth.

Why This Restoration Matters

Every stage of the journey from Greek to Unicode was an act of care: the scribe who first wrote the name, the lexicographer who glossed it, the engineer who taught the DNS to carry it. The PuniCodex restoration is the latest stage, not the last word — the Scholarly Edition is revised as the evidence improves. What does not change is the principle: a name deserves to be written the way its own tradition wrote it. Éris in the address bar is that principle, made routable.
