Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Ceridwen (ceridwen) ​— Inspiration, Transformation ‌· bent or ‌crooked woman — is ​the keeper of ​the cauldron of ​awen in Welsh ​tradition: the enchantress ​of Llyn Tegid ‌who brews a ​year and a day ​for the three ‌drops of inspired ​knowledge, hunts the ‌servant-boy who steals ​them through every ‌shape in creation, ​swallows him as a ​grain of wheat, ​and gives him ​back to the ​world as Taliesin, ‌the greatest poet ​of the Welsh ‌tradition.[1]

Her whole dossier ​is a single ‌prose tale, Hanes ​Taliesin — the ‌Tale of Taliesin ​— preserved in ‌sixteenth-century Welsh manuscripts and ​customarily printed with ​the Mabinogion, though ‌it is none ‌of the Four ‌Branches. The name ​itself is older ​than the tale: medieval ‌Welsh poetry attests ​it in spellings ​such as Kerritwen ‌and Kyrridven, and ​the editors of ​the Welsh triads ‌record that the early ​sources concur on ‌her one fixed ​attribute — ownership ​of the cauldron ​that is the ​source of poetic ‌inspiration, the awen.[2]

The ‌lexicon glosses the ​name 'bent or ‌crooked woman', following ​the analysis Ifor ​Williams advanced for ‌an original form ‌Cyridfen; the dictionaries also ‌print the traditional ‌reading cerdd 'song, ​craft, poetry' + ‌gwen 'white, fair, ​blessed' — 'fair ​of song' — and ​the question remains ‌genuinely open.[3]

PuniCodex ​restores the name ​as Ceridwen — ​identical to the ‌ASCII input, because ​Welsh writes the name ​in plain Latin ​letters with no ‌diacritic for the ​restoration to recover; ‌the name is ​Tier 2 by ‌the mechanical rule. The ​plain-ASCII ceridwen.com is ‌held by a ‌third party outside ​the PuniCodex collection ​and is watched ​for release; no ​form of the name ‌is currently owned, ‌and this temple ​claims none.[4]

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

The name ​is attested in Welsh ‌as Ceridwen, with the ‌early spellings Kerritwen and ​Kyrridven preserved in medieval ​Welsh poetry — the ​name circulated in the ​verse tradition long before ​the sixteenth-century prose tale ‌that now defines her.[1] ​The etymology is one ​of the honest open ‌questions of Welsh onomastics, ​and the dictionaries register ‌two analyses rather than ​settling between them.[2]

The ‌first is the traditional ​reading: cerdd — 'song, ​craft, poetry', the very ​word for the poet's ​art — plus gwen, ​the feminine of gwyn, ‌'white, fair, blessed'. ​'Fair of song, blessed ‌of poetry' is the ​romantic analysis, and it ‌fits the keeper of ​the cauldron of awen ‌almost too well.[2]

The ​second is philologically harder ‌and is the one ​this lexicon prints: Ifor ​Williams, the greatest modern ‌editor of early Welsh ‌poetry, held that the ‌original and authentic form ​was Cyridfen, 'crooked woman' ​— an analysis the ‌early spellings with their ​double-r shapes (Kyrridven) do ​more to support than ‌the cerdd-reading can.[1] The ​gloss 'bent or crooked ​woman' therefore stands in ‌the entry not ​as a slur but ‌as the harder-won reading; ​the name's connection with ​poetry belongs to the ​figure, and need not ​be forced into her ‌etymology.

The ASCII form ‌is not a compromise ​here: Welsh writes the ‌name in plain Latin ​letters, and the normalized ​spelling is already Ceridwen. ‌There is no diacritic ‌and no distinctive letter ‌for the restoration to ‌recover; only the capital ​distinguishes the restored form ‌from the input, which ​places the name in ​Tier 2 by the ​mechanical rule.

The letter-by-letter ‌transformation runs:

  • ​c → C — ​Same, capitalized
  • e ​→ e — Same

‌- r → r ​— Same

  • i ​→ i — Same

​- d → d ‌— Same

  • w ​→ w — Same

‌- e → e ​— Same

  • n ‌→ n — Same

​The project holds no ‌domain for this name: ‌ceridwen.com is registered to ​a third party outside ​the PuniCodex collection and ​is watched for release. ​The temple is served ‌at /ceridwen/ and claims ‌no domain it does ​not hold.

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The pronunciation ​of the ‌name in ‌Modern Welsh ​is /kɛrˈɪd.wɛn/ — ​Modern Welsh; ​the medieval ​value is ​held to be ‌substantially the ​same.[1]

Phoneme ​by phoneme:

  • ‌Cer- — ​Hard velar ‌stop [k] ​(Welsh c is ‌never soft), ​short open ​[ɛ], and ​trilled [r]: the ​light, unstressed ​first syllable

‌- -id- ​— Short close ‌[ɪ] under ​stress with ‌a voiced ​dental [d]: Welsh ‌words are ​stressed on ‌the penultimate ​syllable, so the ​weight of ‌the name ‌lands here

  • ‌-wen — ​Labial-velar [w], ​open-mid [ɛ], ‌and alveolar [n]; ​the unstressed ​ultima closes ‌quietly

For ​the modern speaker, ​the closest ‌approximation is: ​'keh-RID-wen' — ‌the c hard, ​the r ​rolled, the ​stress on ​the middle syllable.

‌Kindred and ‌historical forms ​of the ‌name:

  • Medieval ​Welsh verse ​— Kerritwen, ‌Kyrridven, the early ‌spellings of ‌the poetry

‌- Ifor ​Williams (reconstruction) — ‌Cyridfen, 'crooked ​woman', the ​proposed original ​form

  • Traditional ‌analysis — ​cerdd + ​gwen, 'song' ​+ 'fair, blessed'

‌- Modern ​Welsh — ​Ceridwen, the ​unbroken living form

‌Ceridwen is ​Tier 2 ‌by the ​mechanical rule: the ‌Welsh form ​is already ‌plain ASCII, and ‌the restoration ​preserves nothing ​the ASCII ​form loses. The ​name's information-richness ‌is semantic ‌and mythic, ​not orthographic.

Sources

  1. Morris-Jones, John, A Welsh Grammar, Historical and Comparative (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1913), phonology and orthography.
  2. Geiriadur Prifysgol Cymru / A Dictionary of the Welsh Language (University of Wales Press), s.v. Ceridwen.

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

The name ​is preserved ‌in Welsh ‌manuscripts in ​the Latin ​alphabet as ​Ceridwen — ​the prose ​tale in ‌sixteenth-century Welsh ​(NLW MS ​5276D; the ‌Gellilyfdy copy), ​with earlier ‌poetic attestations ​in spellings ‌such as ​Kerritwen and ​Kyrridven.[1] The ​script is ​the medieval ​Welsh bookhand, ‌left to ​right.

The ‌scholarly rendering ​proceeds step ‌by step:

​- Welsh ‌writes the ​name in ‌plain Latin ​letters; no ​diacritic attaches ‌to it ‌in any ‌witness, and ​normalized orthography ​and the ‌ASCII spelling ​are identical.
​- The ‌early double-r ​spellings (Kyrridven) ​are the ‌evidence Ifor ​Williams read ‌in proposing ​the original ​form Cyridfen, ​'crooked woman' ​— the ‌analysis the ‌lexicon's gloss ​follows.[2]

  • ‌The traditional ​analysis cerdd ​+ gwen, ‌'song' + ‌'fair, blessed', ‌remains in ‌the dictionaries ​as the ‌alternative reading; ​the debate ​is registered, ​not settled.[3]

‌- The ​Unicode restoration ​Ceridwen therefore ​changes nothing ‌but the ​capital; the ​name stands ​in Tier ‌2 by ​the mechanical ‌rule, and ​the plain-ASCII ‌domain ceridwen.com ​— held ‌by a ‌third party ​outside the ​PuniCodex collection ​and watched ​for release ‌— is ‌the philologically ​complete form.

  2. Bartrum, Peter C., A Welsh Classical Dictionary (National Library of Wales, 1993), s.v. Ceridwen.
  3. Geiriadur Prifysgol Cymru / A Dictionary of the Welsh Language (University of Wales Press), s.v. Ceridwen; Koch, Celtic Culture (2006), s.v. Ceridwen.

Domains & Attributes

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sphere of influence, titles, epithets, domain cards.

Ceridwen is ​catalogued under ‌the domain ‌'Inspiration, Transformation' — ​and the ​tale earns both ​terms in ​their strictest ​sense.[1]

Inspiration

‌She is, ​in the words ​the triads' ‌editor preserves ​from the early ‌sources, the ​owner of ‌the cauldron that ​is the ​source of poetic ​inspiration — ​the awen, ​the flowing spirit ‌of Welsh ​poetry, the word ‌the bardic ​tradition still ‌uses for the ​muse itself. ‌Her whole design ​in the ‌tale is ​the industrial production ​of inspiration: ‌gathered ingredients, ‌the books of ‌the feryllt, ​a cauldron boiled ​a year ‌and a ​day, and the ​three drops ‌of the spirit ​of inspiration ​as its ‌yield.[1][2]

Transformation

​The pursuit ‌that follows ​the theft is ​the most ​complete transformation-sequence in ​Welsh literature: ‌hare and ‌greyhound, fish and ​otter, bird ‌and hawk, grain ​of wheat ​and high-crested ‌black hen. And ‌the swallowing ‌turns the ‌chase inside out ​— the ‌swallowed boy is ​carried nine ​months and ​born again, so ‌that the ​final transformation is ​not a ​shape at ‌all but a ​rebirth: Gwion ​Bach becomes Taliesin.[1]

​### The ‌Cauldron as ​Instrument of Both

‌The two ​domains meet ‌in the pot ​itself: the ‌cauldron brews the ‌inspiration and ​presides over ​the transformation, the ​vessel in ​which one kind ‌of being ‌is cooked ​into another.[3]

Symbols

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Iconography, attributes, and their meanings.

Ceridwen's attributes ​are the ‌working properties ‌of her ​single tale ​— the ​most functional ​regalia in ​the Welsh ‌corpus:[1]

  • ​The cauldron ​of awen ‌— Boiled ​a year ‌and a ​day from the ‌books of ​the feryllt ​until three ​drops of ​the spirit ​of inspiration ‌should rise: ​knowledge as ‌something cooked ​by art ‌and patience, ​not granted

‌- The ​three drops — ‌The whole ​virtue of ​the brew ‌in an ‌instant's splash: ‌inspiration that ​chooses its ​own vessel, ‌and falls ​on the ​wrong finger
‌- The ​high-crested black ​hen — ‌Her last hunting-shape: ​the swallowing ‌that is ​not death ​but the ​first cell ​of a ‌poet

  • ‌The hide ​bag — ‌The coracle-skin ​sewn round ​the child ‌she could ‌not kill, ‌given to the ‌sea on ​the twenty-ninth ‌of April: ​rejection that ​becomes deliverance

​- The ‌weir on ​the Dyfi ​— Gwyddno ​Garanhir's salmon-weir, ‌where the ​bag comes ​to rest ​on May Day ‌and the ​radiant brow ‌is named
​- Llyn ‌Tegid — ​Her lake, ‌modern Bala ‌Lake, which ​still carries ​her husband ​Tegid Foel's ​name: the ‌story's anchor ‌in real ​Welsh geography[2]

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

Ceridwen's mythology is ​one tale, Hanes Taliesin, told with ‌unusual economy — she holds ‌its first half absolutely and ​vanishes from its second.[1]

The ​Household by Llyn Tegid (Hanes ​Taliesin)

Ceridwen lived beside Llyn Tegid ​in Penllyn, the wife of ​Tegid Foel — Tegid the ‌Bald — whose name the lake ​still carries. Their children were ​weighed in opposite scales: Creirwy, the ‌fairest maiden in the world, ​and Morfran, a son so ‌dark and ill-favoured he was called ​Afagddu, Utter Darkness. What nature ‌had refused the boy, his mother ​resolved to supply from her ​arts: knowledge, and the gift ​of poetry.[1]

The Cauldron of ​a Year and a Day ​(Hanes Taliesin)

From the books of ‌the feryllt — the Welsh ​term for the alchemist's art ‌— she compounded a cauldron of ​Inspiration and Science, to be ‌boiled without ceasing for a year ​and a day, until three ‌drops of the spirit of ​inspiration were won. A blind man, ‌Morda, was set to kindle ​the fire; a servant-boy, Gwion Bach, ​son of Gwreang of Llanfair ‌in Powys, to stir. The ‌design was exact; everything that follows ‌is its unmaking.[1]

The ​Three Drops and the Flight of ​Shapes (Hanes Taliesin)

At the ‌year's end the three charmed ​drops leapt from the cauldron onto ​Gwion Bach's finger. He put ‌it in his mouth, and in ​that instant all knowledge of ​things past and to come ‌was his — and he fled, ​knowing Ceridwen would kill him. ‌She pursued through the great ​transformation-chase of Welsh tradition: he a ​hare, she a greyhound; he ​a fish, she an otter-bitch; he ​a bird, she a hawk; ‌every shape he took, she ‌took its hunter.[1]

The Black ​Hen and the Rebirth (Hanes ‌Taliesin)

Spent, the boy hid himself ​as one grain of winnowed ​wheat on a barn floor; ‌Ceridwen became a high-crested black hen, ‌found the grain, and swallowed ‌it. She carried him nine months, ‌and when she bore him ​he was so beautiful she ‌could not kill him: she sewed ​him in a hide bag ​and cast him into the sea ​on the twenty-ninth of April ‌— and passes out of ​the tale.[1]

The Radiant Brow ​(Hanes Taliesin)

On May Day ​the bag came to the salmon-weir ‌of Gwyddno Garanhir near the ​Dyfi's mouth, and Gwyddno's son ​Elffin found it. Seeing the boy's ​forehead he cried, 'Behold the ‌radiant brow' — tal iesin — ​and the child answered: 'Taliesin ‌be I called.' Years later ​the boy went to the court ‌of Maelgwn Gwynedd, shamed the ​royal bards with poems they could ‌not match, foretold the king's ‌death, and freed his imprisoned ​foster-father. The inspiration brewed for one ​son had become the making ​of another: the first poet of ​Wales is, in the end, ‌Ceridwen's child — swallowed, carried, ‌born again, and set adrift by ​her own hands.[1][2]

Sources

  1. Ford, Patrick K., ed., Ystoria Taliesin (Cardiff: University of Wales Press, 1992); Ford, trans., The Mabinogi and Other Medieval Welsh Tales (Berkeley: University of California Press, 1977).
  2. Wood, Juliette, 'The Elphin Section of Hanes Taliesin', Études Celtiques 18 (1981), on the tale's structure and the naming episode.

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

The strongest ​cross-border parallels are ‌Irish. The accidental ‌acquisition of ​a wisdom prepared ​for another is ​the plot of ​the Irish boyhood ​deeds of Finn ‌mac Cumaill: ​cooking the salmon ​of knowledge for ‌his master Finnéces, ​the boy burns ‌his thumb, puts ​it in his ‌mouth, and ​the knowledge intended ​for the teacher ​passes to the ​servant — the ​same economy as ‌the three ​drops and the ‌burning finger.[1] The ​swallowing that becomes ‌rebirth has its ​Irish twin in ‌Tochmarc Étaíne, ​where Étaín, transformed ‌into a fly, ​falls into a ​cup of wine, ‌is swallowed, and ‌is born again ‌into the ​mortal world.[2]

The ​cauldron itself is ‌pan-Celtic furniture: the ​Dagda's cauldron from ​Murias feeds every ‌company that ​comes to it, ​and the Old ‌Irish text The ​Cauldron of Poesy ‌locates a cauldron ​of poetry ​inside every human ​being — the ​closest Irish cousin ‌of the cauldron ‌of awen.[3] Folklorists, ​Juliette Wood ‌chief among them, ​have shown that ​Hanes Taliesin braids ‌these native strands ‌with international folktale ‌types, above all ‌the magic ​flight with its ‌transformation chase.[4]

The ​last syncretism is ​modern: Iolo Morganwg's ​Barddas set Ceridwen ‌and the ​awen at the ​centre of revival ​Druidry, and modern ‌Druids and pagans ​honour her as ​goddess of ​the cauldron — ‌a reception Ronald ​Hutton's histories of ‌the revival document ​and dissect.[5] Within ‌the Welsh corpus ​her nearest ‌kin are the ‌other figures of ​enchantment: Gwydion, the ​magician of the ​Fourth Branch, and ​Rhiannon, who ‌like Ceridwen outlasts ‌an unjust pursuit ​without breaking.

Sources

  2. Bergin, Osborn, and R. I. Best, ed. and trans., 'Tochmarc Étaíne', Ériu 12 (1938), 137–196.
  3. Breatnach, Liam, ed., 'The Cauldron of Poesy', Ériu 32 (1981), 45–93; Gray, Elizabeth A., ed. and trans., Cath Maige Tuired, Irish Texts Society 52 (1982).
  4. Wood, Juliette, 'The Elphin Section of Hanes Taliesin', Études Celtiques 18 (1981).
  5. Hutton, Ronald, The Druids (London: Hambledon Continuum, 2007), on Iolo Morganwg and the Barddas.

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

Lady Charlotte ​Guest carried ‌the Tale ‌of Taliesin ​into English ​in the ​nineteenth century, ​appended to ​her Mabinogion, ‌and the ​enchantress of ​Llyn Tegid ‌entered the ​English-speaking imagination ‌beside the ​Four Branches ‌she never ​belonged to.[1] ​Patrick K. ​Ford's edition ​and translation ​fixed the ‌tale's modern ​scholarly form.[2]

‌Robert Graves ​made her ‌a figure ​of international ‌consequence: The ​White Goddess (1948) ‌read Ceridwen's ​cauldron and ​her hunt ‌as the ‌grammar of ‌all true ​poetry, and ​though Celticists ‌have never ​accepted Graves's ​method, his ‌book set ​her at ​the centre ‌of twentieth-century ​writing about ‌the muse.[3] ​In Wales ​the awen ​she brewed ​never left ‌the tradition ‌— the ​word still ‌names poetic ​inspiration at ​the National ‌Eisteddfod, and ‌the Gorsedd ‌of Bards invokes ‌it in ​its rites ‌— while ​modern Druid ​orders and ​pagan practice ‌worldwide honour ​Ceridwen as ​keeper of ​the cauldron ‌of transformation.[4] ​The name ​itself, rare ​in the ‌medieval record, ​has come ‌back into ​use as ‌a Welsh ​given name, ‌carried by ‌the only ​myth in ​the language ​in which ​a mother's ‌failed plan ‌becomes a ​nation's poetry.

Sources

  1. The Mabinogion, trans. Lady Charlotte Guest, 3 vols. (London, 1838–49; the Tale of Taliesin appended in later editions).
  2. Ford, Patrick K., ed., Ystoria Taliesin (University of Wales Press, 1992).
  3. Graves, Robert, The White Goddess: A Historical Grammar of Poetic Myth (London: Faber and Faber, 1948).
  4. Hutton, Ronald, The Druids (Hambledon Continuum, 2007).

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

Ceridwen is ​a textual ‌figure: no ‌temple, votive ​deposit, or ​inscription names ​her, and ​pre-Christian Wales ​left no ‌theonymic epigraphy ​of the ​continental kind.[1] ‌Her witnesses ​are manuscripts. ‌The fullest ​early text ‌of Hanes ​Taliesin is ​embedded in ​the great ​world-chronicle of ​Elis Gruffydd ‌— a Welsh ​soldier and ‌chronicler of ​the Calais ‌garrison — ​compiled c. ‌1530–1552 and ​preserved in ‌Aberystwyth, National ​Library of ​Wales, MS ‌5276D; a ‌second version ‌was copied ​by the ​recusant scribe ‌John Jones ​of Gellilyfdy ​around 1607.[2]

‌The name ​is older ​than its ‌tale: medieval Welsh ​poetry preserves ‌it in ​spellings such ​as Kerritwen ​and Kyrridven, ​and the ‌editors of ‌the triads ​record that ‌the early ​sources agree ​on her ‌one fixed ‌attribute, the ‌cauldron of ‌awen.[3] The ​geography of ‌the story ​is real ​and still ​legible: Llyn ‌Tegid, her lake, ​is Bala ​Lake in ​Gwynedd, the ‌largest natural ​lake in ​Wales, which ​keeps her ‌husband Tegid ​Foel's name; ‌and Gwyddno's ​weir, where ‌the bag ​comes ashore, ‌stands in ‌the tradition ​at the ​Dyfi estuary. ​No sanctuary ​of hers ‌is known, ‌and none ​is claimed.

Irish Mythological Cycles

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The cauldron ​of awen has its ‌deepest cousins across ‌the Irish Sea, in ​an Irish cauldron-complex that ​between them maps ​nearly the whole range ​of what a ​cauldron can mean.[1] The ‌first cousin is the ​Dagda's cauldron, one ​of the four treasures ‌the Túatha Dé ​Danann brought from the ‌northern cities of ​the world — the ‌cauldron of Murias, from ​which no company ​ever went away unsatisfied; ​Cath Maige Tuired ​keeps its law, and ​the tradition holds ‌it as the cauldron ​of provision, the vessel ‌that feeds.[2] Where ​the Dagda's pot answers ‌hunger, Ceridwen's answers ​ignorance — and the ‌Old Irish tradition knows ​that second kind ‌too. The metrical treatise ​modern scholarship calls ​The Cauldron of Poesy ‌locates three cauldrons ‌within every human being, ‌among them the cauldron ​of poetry, born ​on its lips and ‌turned upright only ​by art, learning, and ​the warming of ‌divine and human inspiration ​— the closest Irish ​analogue to the ‌awen: inspiration as something ​positioned within a ‌person and ripened, not ​granted from outside.[3]

The ​sharpest parallel is ​the accident itself. In ​the boyhood deeds ‌of Finn mac Cumaill, ‌the youth cooks ​the salmon of knowledge ‌for his master Finnéces, ​who had waited ​seven years for the ‌fish; tending it, ‌Finn burns his thumb ‌on the hot ‌skin, puts it in ​his mouth, and the ‌knowledge intended for ​the teacher passes to ​the servant.[4] It ​is the exact economy ‌of Gwion Bach and ​the three drops: ​wisdom brewed for one, ​won by another ‌through a burning finger ​and an involuntary ​mouth — inspiration that ​chooses its own vessel. ‌Whether the Welsh ​tale borrowed from such ‌Irish models, or ​both drew independently on ‌a shared Celtic ​inheritance, is the standing ‌question of Irish–Welsh literary ‌relations; Sims-Williams's study ​of Irish influence on ​medieval Welsh literature ​shows how deep the ​channels run and how ‌rarely a single ‌direction of borrowing can ​be proved.[5]

  2. Gray, Elizabeth A., ed. and trans., Cath Maige Tuired, Irish Texts Society 52 (1982); Mac Cana, Proinsias, Celtic Mythology (London: Hamlyn, 1970), on the Dagda's cauldron of Murias.
  3. Breatnach, Liam, ed., 'The Cauldron of Poesy', Ériu 32 (1981), 45–93.

Welsh Sources

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Ceridwen's dossier ​rests on ‌a single prose ‌tale and ​a scattered poetic ​memory, and ​the distinction matters. ​Hanes Taliesin ​— the Tale ‌of Taliesin ​— is a ​sixteenth-century prose ‌text, preserved in ​the world-chronicle ‌of Elis Gruffydd ​(NLW MS ‌5276D, c. 1550) ​and in ​the version copied ​by John ​Jones of Gellilyfdy ​(c. 1607); ‌it is customarily ​printed with ‌the Mabinogion since ​Lady Charlotte ‌Guest's translation, but ​it is ‌none of the ​Four Branches, ‌and it does ​not stand ​in the White ‌Book of ‌Rhydderch or the ‌Red Book ​of Hergest with ​them.[1][2]

The ‌name, by contrast, ​is genuinely ​early. Medieval Welsh ‌poetry preserves ​it in spellings ​such as ‌Kerritwen and Kyrridven, ​and the ‌triads' editor records ​the consensus ​of the early ​sources: Ceridwen ​is the owner ‌of a ‌cauldron which is ​the source ‌of poetic inspiration, ​the awen; ​the early verse ‌also pairs ‌her with the ‌obscure Ogyrwen, ‌a name or ​word bound ‌to poetry.[3] The ​prose tale ​and the poetic ​memory agree ‌on the essential ​fact and ​differ on everything ​else — ‌the poems of ​the Book ​of Taliesin, attached ​to the ‌persona of her ​reborn son, ‌know nothing of ​Tegid Foel ‌or the weir. ​The standard ‌modern edition is ‌Patrick K. ​Ford's Ystoria Taliesin ​(1992); his ​English version appears ​in The ‌Mabinogi and Other ‌Medieval Welsh ​Tales (1977).[1]

Sources

  1. Ford, Patrick K., ed., Ystoria Taliesin (University of Wales Press, 1992), introduction, on the manuscripts and the relationship to the Taliesin poems.
  2. The Mabinogion, trans. Lady Charlotte Guest (1838–49, with the Tale of Taliesin appended in later editions).
  3. Bromwich, Rachel, ed., Trioedd Ynys Prydein, 3rd ed. (2006), p. 309; Bartrum, A Welsh Classical Dictionary (1993), s.v. Ceridwen.

Inscriptions & Seals

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

No ​inscription names ‌Ceridwen, ‌and none ​was ever ​to ​be expected. ​The ​early inscribed ‌stones ​of Wales ​— the ‌Latin ​and occasional ‌ogham ​memorial stones ‌of ​roughly the ​fifth to ​seventh ​centuries — ​record ‌personal names ​in ‌funerary formulae, ​not ‌theonyms; votive ​epigraphy of ‌the ​Gaulish kind ‌does ​not survive ​from ‌Wales, and ‌the divine ‌names ​of the ​Welsh ‌tradition are ​known ​from literature, ‌triads, and ​place-lore ​rather than ‌stone.[1]

​The record ‌to ​set beside ​her is ​therefore ​manuscriptal and ‌geographical: ‌the early ​poetic ‌spellings Kerritwen ​and Kyrridven, ​which ‌push the ‌name ‌centuries behind ‌its ​sixteenth-century tale, ‌and the ​lake ​itself — ​Llyn ‌Tegid, modern ​Bala ​Lake — ​which ‌carries her ​husband's name ​into ​the present ‌landscape.[2] ​The honesty ‌of ​the record ‌is the ​point: ‌her antiquity ‌rests ​on the ​verse ​citations the ​triads collect, ‌not ‌on any ​stone.

Sources

  1. Jackson, Kenneth H., Language and History in Early Britain (Edinburgh: Edinburgh University Press, 1953), on the early inscriptional record.
  2. Bromwich, Rachel, ed., Trioedd Ynys Prydein, 3rd ed. (2006), p. 309; Bartrum, A Welsh Classical Dictionary (1993), s.v. Ceridwen.

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

Ceridwen is ​the only ‌great figure ‌of Welsh tradition ​whose myth ​is a ​failure, and whose ​failure is ​a gift. ‌She plans everything: ​the books ​consulted, the fire ‌kept, the ​boy set ‌to stir, the ​year counted ‌to its ​day. And the ​plan is ​perfect except ​for the one ​thing no ‌plan commands ​— where the ‌drops fall.[1]

​The inspiration ‌she cooked for ​the son ‌she pitied goes ​to the ‌son she ​hunts, and everything ​she does ‌to recover ‌it only completes ‌the gift: ​the chase ​teaches him every ‌shape in ​creation, the ​swallowing carries him ‌through the ​longest transformation ​of all, and ‌the bag ​she sews to ‌drown him ​is the ​coracle that brings ​him to ​his name. ‌That is the ‌deepest thing ​the Welsh ‌tradition knows about ​poetry: it ​cannot be ‌inherited by arrangement. ‌It is ‌brewed in ‌patience and received ​by accident; ‌it belongs to ​whoever is ​standing by ​the pot with ‌a burning ​finger. Ceridwen ​loses the awen ​and gains ‌the poet ​— and the ​tale does ​not call ‌her defeated. It ​simply follows ‌the boy ​to the sea, ‌and lets ​the witch of ‌Llyn Tegid ‌rest, her ​cauldron empty, her ​work done, ​her name ​over the door ‌of every ‌song that ​comes unplanned.

Sources

  1. Ford, Patrick K., ed., Ystoria Taliesin (University of Wales Press, 1992).
