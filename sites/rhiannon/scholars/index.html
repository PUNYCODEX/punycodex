01

Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Rhiannon (rhiannon) ​— Horses, Sovereignty ‌· Great Queen ‌— is the ​central female figure ​of the Four ​Branches of the ​Mabinogi, the queen ​of Dyfed who ‌rides past Gorsedd ​Arberth on a ​pale horse at ‌a pace no ​mounted pursuit can ‌close, and who ​bears a false ‌accusation and an ​unjust penance without ​once surrendering her ​dignity.[1]

Her name ​is a scholarly ​reconstruction made flesh: ‌Celticists derive Middle ​Welsh Rhiannon from ‌Common Celtic Rīgantonā, ​'great queen, divine ‌queen', from rīgan- ​'queen' — Old ‌Irish rígan is ​the exact cognate ‌— and the ​figure matches the ​title.[2] She appears ‌in three of ‌the Four Branches: ‌in the First, ​Pwyll Pendefig Dyfed, ​she chooses Pwyll ‌for her husband, ​out-plots the unwanted ​suitor Gwawl, loses ‌her newborn son ​to a May-eve ​marvel, and endures ‌the penance of ​the horse-block until ‌the boy is ​restored and named ​Pryderi from her ​own word of ​relief; in the ‌Second, Branwen ferch ‌Llŷr, her birds ​— Adar Rhiannon ‌— sing to ​the seven survivors ​across the sea; ‌in the Third, ‌Manawydan fab Llŷr, ‌she is caught ‌in the enchantment ​of Dyfed and ‌comes home wearing ​the collars of ​asses, still a ​queen.[1]

PuniCodex restores ‌the name as ​Rhiannon — identical ​to the ASCII ​input, because Middle ‌Welsh writes the ​name in plain ​Latin letters with ​no diacritic for ‌the restoration to ​recover; the name ‌is Tier 2 ​by the mechanical ‌rule. The plain-ASCII ​rhiannon.com is held ‌by a third ‌party outside the ​PuniCodex collection and ​is watched for ​release; no form ​of the name ‌is currently owned, ‌and this temple ​claims none.[3]

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

The name ​is attested in ‌Middle Welsh ‌as Rhiannon, daughter ​of Hefaidd Hên ​(Heyfaidd the Old), ​in the Four ​Branches of ​the Mabinogi. The ‌standard etymology is ​a reconstruction: Common ​Celtic Rīgantonā, built ‌on rīgan- ​'queen' — the ‌exact cognate of ​Old Irish rígan ‌and relative of ​Welsh rhiain ​'maiden, noble lady, ​queen' — with ​a suffix marking ​the divine or ‌intensified form, ​hence the traditional ‌gloss 'Great Queen' ​or 'Divine Queen'.[1] ‌The reconstruction is ​the consensus ‌of the handbooks, ​but it is ‌a reconstruction: no ​ancient inscription preserves ​the longer ‌form, and honest ‌scholarship labels it ‌accordingly.[2]

The ASCII ​form is not ​a compromise ‌here: Middle Welsh ​writes the name ​in plain Latin ‌letters, and the ​normalized spelling ​is already Rhiannon. ‌There is no ​diacritic and no ‌distinctive letter ​for the restoration ​to recover; only ​the capital distinguishes ​the restored form ‌from the ‌input, which places ​the name in ‌Tier 2 by ​the mechanical rule.

​The letter-by-letter ‌transformation runs:

  • ‌r → R ‌— Same, capitalized

‌- h → ​h — ‌Same

  • i ​→ i — ​Same
  • a ​→ a — ‌Same
  • ​n → n ​— Same
  • ​n → n ‌— Same
  • ​o → ​o — Same

​- n → ‌n — Same

​The project holds ‌no domain ​for this name: ‌rhiannon.com is registered ​to a third ‌party outside the ‌PuniCodex collection ​and is watched ​for release. The ​temple is served ​at /rhiannon/ and ‌claims no ‌domain it does ​not hold.

03

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The pronunciation ​of the ‌name in Modern ‌Welsh is /r̥iˈa.nɔn/ ​— Modern ​Welsh; the Middle ​Welsh value ​is held to ​be substantially ‌the same.[1]

Phoneme ​by phoneme:

  • ​Rh- — ‌Voiceless alveolar trill ​[r̥]: the ‌Welsh digraph rh ​writes an ‌r sounded with ​breath, a phoneme ​English lacks; ​the usual approximation ​'hr' is ​only a crutch

‌- -i- — ​Short close ‌front [i] in ​the light, ‌unstressed first syllable
​- -an- ‌— Open [a] ​under stress: Welsh ‌words are ​stressed on the ​penultimate syllable, ‌so the weight ‌of the ‌name lands here
​- -non — ​Alveolar nasal ‌[n], the Welsh ​open-mid [ɔ], ​and the final ‌[n]; the unstressed ​ultima closes ​quietly

For the ‌modern speaker, ​the closest approximation ‌is: 'hree-AH-non' ​— a breathy, ​whispered r at ​the start, ​the stress on ‌the middle ‌syllable.

Kindred and ​historical forms of ‌the name:

​- Middle Welsh ​— Rhiannon, ‌daughter of Hefaidd ‌Hên, queen ‌of Dyfed

  • ‌Common Celtic (reconstructed) ​— Rīgantonā, ‌'great queen, divine ​queen'
  • ​Old Irish cognate ​— rígan, ‌'queen'
  • Modern ​Welsh — rhiain, ​'maiden, noble ​lady, queen', the ‌living relative

​Rhiannon is Tier ​2 by the ​mechanical rule: ‌the Middle Welsh ​form is ‌already plain ASCII, ​and the ‌restoration preserves nothing ​the ASCII form ‌loses. The ‌reconstructed etymon 'Great ​Queen' belongs ​to the philological ​record, not ​to the spelling: ‌the name's information-richness ‌is semantic, ​not orthographic.

Sources

  1. Morris-Jones, John, A Welsh Grammar, Historical and Comparative (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1913), phonology and orthography.
  2. Geiriadur Prifysgol Cymru / A Dictionary of the Welsh Language (University of Wales Press), s.v. Rhiannon.

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

The ​name is ‌preserved in ‌Middle Welsh ​manuscripts in ​the Latin ​alphabet as ​Rhiannon — ​Middle Welsh ‌(Brythonic Celtic), ​attested in ​the White ‌Book of ​Rhydderch (c. ‌1350) and ​the Red ‌Book of ​Hergest (c. ​1382–1410), ​recording material ​composed centuries ​earlier.[1] The ‌script is ​written left-to-right ‌in the ​medieval Welsh ‌bookhand.

The ​scholarly rendering ‌proceeds step ​by step:

‌- Middle ​Welsh writes ​the name ‌in plain ‌Latin letters; ‌the ​digraph rh ​represents the ‌voiceless trill ​[r̥] that ​the language ‌still keeps.
​- No ​diacritic attaches ‌to the ​name in ‌any witness: ​normalized Middle ​Welsh orthography ​and the ​ASCII spelling ‌are identical.
‌- The ​deeper ‌form Rīgantonā ​is a ​modern philological ‌reconstruction from ‌Common Celtic ‌rīgan- 'queen' ‌(Old Irish ​rígan, Welsh ‌rhiain), not ​an attested ​manuscript spelling.[2]
​- The ‌Unicode restoration ​Rhiannon therefore ​changes nothing ​but the ‌capital; ​the name ​stands in ​Tier 2 ‌by the ​mechanical rule, ‌and the ​plain-ASCII domain ‌rhiannon.com — ​held by ‌a third ‌party outside ​the PuniCodex ​collection and ​watched for ​release — ‌is the ‌philologically complete ​form.

Sources

  1. Williams, Ifor, ed., Pedeir Keinc y Mabinogi (University of Wales Press, 1930); Davies, trans., The Mabinogion (Oxford University Press, 2007).
  2. Geiriadur Prifysgol Cymru / A Dictionary of the Welsh Language (University of Wales Press), s.vv. Rhiannon, rhiain; Koch, Celtic Culture (2006), s.v. Rhiannon.

Domains & Attributes

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sphere of influence, titles, epithets, domain cards.

Rhiannon is ​the Great ‌Queen of the ‌Mabinogi: the ​Otherworld rider ​who chooses her ​own king, ​the mother who ​endures a ‌false charge, and ​the captive ​who survives the ‌enchantment of ​Dyfed.[1]

‌Sovereignty

She is ​the land's ‌queen in the ​oldest sense: ​she appears unbidden ​at the ​mound of Arberth, ​names the ‌man she ​will marry, and ‌her union ​with Pwyll stands ‌in the ​long Celtic pattern ‌in which ​kingship is conferred ‌by the ​woman of ​the territory.[2]

‌Horses

Every ‌turn of her ‌story is ​bound to the ​horse: the ‌pale mount no ​rider can ​overtake, the ‌foal born the ​night her ​son is stolen, ‌the horse-block ​of her penance, ‌and the ​collars of asses ​she wears ​out of ​the enchantment. Scholars ‌have long ‌read her against ​the Gaulish ‌horse-goddess Epona.[2]

​Endurance

Twice ​she is sentenced ‌to humiliation ‌she did ‌not earn — ‌the false ​accusation of the ‌serving women, ​the spell of ​Llwyd — ​and twice she ‌endures it ​without breaking, ​until the truth ​arrives on ‌its own legs.[1]

​### The ​Otherworld's Music

The ​Birds of ‌Rhiannon, Adar Rhiannon, ​sing to ‌the survivors ​of the Irish ‌war: a ​song far over ‌the sea ‌yet clear as ​if near, ​which later tradition ​credits with ​waking the ‌dead and lulling ‌the living ​to sleep.[3]

Sources

  1. The Mabinogi, First and Third Branches, trans. Sioned Davies (Oxford University Press, 2007).
  2. Mac Cana, Proinsias, The Mabinogi, 2nd ed. (Cardiff: University of Wales Press, 1992).
  3. The Mabinogi, Second Branch (Branwen ferch Llŷr), trans. Davies (2007); MacKillop, James, Dictionary of Celtic Mythology (Oxford, 1998), s.v. Adar Rhiannon.

Symbols

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Iconography, attributes, and their meanings.

The iconography ​of Rhiannon ‌is narrative ‌rather than cultic ​— medieval ​Welsh prose ​preserves no temple ​regalia, but ​the Four ‌Branches fixed a ​small set ​of attributes ‌around her:[1]

​- The pale ‌horse — ​The great ‌pale mount she ​rides past ​Gorsedd Arberth ​at an even ​pace no ​pursuit can ‌close: sovereignty that ​arrives when ‌it chooses, ​not when it ‌is chased
​- Gorsedd ‌Arberth — The ​mound above ‌the court ​where whoever sits ​receives blows ‌or sees ‌a wonder: ‌the threshold between ​Dyfed and ​the Otherworld
‌- The bottomless ​bag — ​The small ‌bag that will ​not fill ​until a ‌true lord treads ​it down: ‌her counter-plot ​against Gwawl, and ​the origin ​of the ​game of Badger ‌in the ‌Bag

  • ​The horse-block — ‌The marble ​mounting-block at ​the gate ‌of Arberth where ‌she sits ‌out her ‌penance and offers ​to carry ‌guests to ​court on her ​back: dignity ​under a ‌false sentence
  • ​The collars ​of asses ​— What she ‌wears about ​her neck ​when the enchantment ​of Dyfed ‌releases her: ​the humiliation of ‌the Third ​Branch, endured ‌like a ​queen
  • The ‌three birds ‌— Adar ​Rhiannon, whose song ​over the ​sea is ​clearer than any ‌earthly music: ‌the Otherworld ​made audible[2]

Sources

  1. The Mabinogi, First and Third Branches, trans. Davies (2007).
  2. The Mabinogi, Second Branch, trans. Davies (2007); MacKillop, Dictionary of Celtic Mythology (1998), s.v. Adar Rhiannon.
07

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

Rhiannon's mythology is the strongest sustained ​portrait of a woman in medieval Welsh prose, and it is ‌told almost entirely in the First and Third Branches of ‌the Mabinogi, with one luminous cameo in the Second.[1]

The ​Rider No Horse Can Catch (First Branch, Pwyll Pendefig Dyfed)

Pwyll, ​prince of Dyfed, goes up to Gorsedd Arberth — the ​mound where a man either suffers blows or sees a wonder ​— and sees a lady in shining gold brocade riding past ​on a great pale horse, at a slow and even ‌pace. He sends his swiftest rider after her; the faster the ​pursuit, the farther she draws ahead. It happens again, and again, ​until Pwyll himself spurs his horse nearly to foundering and ‌at last calls out: 'Maiden, for the sake of the man ​you love best, wait for me.' She stops at once: 'I ‌will wait gladly — and it would have been better ​for the horse had you asked me that long ago.' She ‌is Rhiannon, daughter of Hefaidd Hên, and she has come of ​her own will: she is being given to a man ​against her desire, and she has chosen Pwyll.[1]

The Bag ​That Would Not Fill (First Branch)

At the wedding feast Pwyll ​grants a request before hearing it, and the petitioner is ​Gwawl fab Clud, the suitor Rhiannon refused, who claims her by ‌the promise. Rhiannon devises the counter-plot: a year later Pwyll comes ​to Gwawl's feast as a beggar asking only that a ‌certain small bag be filled with food. The bag cannot be ​filled until a true lord treads the food down and declares ‌it enough; Gwawl rises to do so, Pwyll draws the ​bag shut over him, and his war-band falls on the hall, ‌striking the bag and asking what is in it — 'a ​badger' — the origin of the game called Badger in ‌the Bag. Gwawl renounces his claim and gives sureties; Rhiannon marries ​the man she chose.[1]

The May-Eve Vanishing and the Horse-Block ​(First Branch)

After three childless years the nobles press Pwyll ‌to take another wife; he begs one year more, and Rhiannon ‌bears a son. On the birth-night the six watching women all ‌fall asleep, and the child vanishes. Fearing blame, they kill ​a hound's pups, smear the blood on Rhiannon's face, lay the ​bones beside her, and swear she destroyed her own child. Pwyll ‌will not put her away, but a penance is imposed: ​to sit beside the horse-block at the gate of Arberth, tell ​her story to every traveller, and offer to carry guests and ‌strangers to court on her back. She accepts a sentence ​she did not earn, and bears it.[1]

The child has fallen ​into the strangest rescue in Welsh prose. Teyrnon Twrf Liant loses ‌a foal every May-eve; keeping watch, he strikes off a ​great clawed hand that reaches through the window for the newborn ‌foal, and outside the door finds a baby wrapped in silk ​brocade. He and his wife rear the boy as Gwri ​Wallt Euryn, Gwri of the Golden Hair, who grows with uncanny ​speed into the very likeness of Pwyll. When Teyrnon hears of ​Rhiannon's penance he brings the boy to Arberth; Rhiannon says ‌that if this is true, her pryder — her care, her ‌anxiety — is lifted from her, and from that word the ​boy is named Pryderi. The penance ends.[1]

The Enchantment ‌of Dyfed (Third Branch, Manawydan fab Llŷr)

After Pwyll's death Rhiannon ​is given in marriage to Manawydan fab Llŷr, and with Pryderi ​and his wife Cigfa the four return to Dyfed. A ‌peal of thunder and a fall of mist empty the land ‌of people, dwellings, and beasts. They live by hunting, then by ‌saddlery, shield-making, and shoemaking in England, driven from each town ‌by craftsmen who cannot match their work. Back in empty Dyfed, ​a shining white boar leads Pryderi and Manawydan to a fortress ‌that was never there; Pryderi takes hold of a golden ​bowl hanging by chains above a marble fountain and is rooted, ​dumb, to the spot. Rhiannon goes in after him, grasps the ​same bowl, and is trapped beside him — and fort, ‌bowl, and prisoners vanish.[1]

Manawydan undoes the spell by patience: he ​catches one slow mouse among the hordes stripping his wheat, condemns ​it to hang as a thief, and refuses the ransom ​offered in turn by a clerk, a priest, and a bishop ‌until his price is met — Pryderi and Rhiannon released, the ​enchantment lifted, no vengeance. The mouse is the wife of ​Llwyd fab Cilcoed, transformed with his household; Llwyd had laid the ​spell to avenge his friend Gwawl. Pryderi comes home with the ‌gate-hammer about his neck, and Rhiannon with the collars of ​asses about hers — the second humiliation she wears like a ‌queen, and the second she survives.[1]

The Birds of Rhiannon ​(Second Branch, Branwen ferch Llŷr)

Rhiannon never steps on stage ‌in the Second Branch, but her birds do. The seven survivors ​of the Irish war, bearing the wounded head of Brân the ‌Blessed, feast at Harlech for seven years, and three birds ‌— Adar Rhiannon — begin to sing to them: every other ​song seems unlovely beside it, and though the birds are far ​out over the sea, their singing is as clear as ​if they sang in the hall. The song accompanies the company ​through the eighty years at Gwales in Penfro, until the forbidden ‌door toward Cornwall is opened and sorrow returns. Later Welsh ‌tradition says the song of the Adar Rhiannon wakes the dead ​and lulls the living to sleep.[2]

Sources

  1. The Mabinogi, First Branch (Pwyll Pendefig Dyfed) and Third Branch (Manawydan fab Llŷr), trans. Sioned Davies (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2007); Welsh text ed. Ifor Williams, Pedeir Keinc y Mabinogi (Cardiff: University of Wales Press, 1930).
  2. The Mabinogi, Second Branch (Branwen ferch Llŷr), trans. Davies (2007); MacKillop, James, Dictionary of Celtic Mythology (Oxford, 1998), s.v. Adar Rhiannon.

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

The great ​comparative debate ‌pairs Rhiannon with ‌Epona, the ​Gaulish horse-goddess whose ​two hundred ​and more dedications ​make her ​the best-attested Celtic ‌deity of ​the Roman west. ​A queenly ‌rider whose every ​story is ‌bound to horses, ​whose name ‌the handbooks reconstruct ​as 'Great ​Queen', looks very ​like an ​insular reflex of ​the mare-goddess; ‌Mac Cana argues ​the parallel ‌and Koch registers ​it as ‌the standard comparison ​— but ‌no inscription names ​Rhiannon or ‌*Rīgantonā, and the ​identification remains ​typological, not epigraphic.[1]

‌The second ‌frame is sovereignty. ‌Mac Cana ​reads the First ​Branch against ‌the Irish pattern ​in which ​the land's queen ‌chooses and ​validates the king, ​so that ‌Pwyll's marriage to ​Rhiannon is ‌his investiture as ​much as ​his wedding.[2] The ​third comparison ​is with the ‌Irish Macha, ‌the sovereignty figure ​forced to ‌race the king's ​horses: horse, ​humiliation, and queenship ‌cluster around ‌both women, and ‌the handbooks ‌have compared them ​for a ‌century. None of ​these parallels ​is an identity; ​together they ‌map the kind ​of goddess ​the Welsh prose ​is remembering.

‌Within the corpus, ​closely related ​figures include Pryderi ​(her son, ‌the only child ​of the ‌Four Branches whose ​story spans ‌all four), Manawydan ​(her second ‌husband, the patient ‌undoer of ​the enchantment), and ​Arawn (the ​Otherworld king of ​Annwfn whose ‌friendship with Pwyll ‌opens the ​First Branch).

Sources

  1. Koch, John T., ed., Celtic Culture: A Historical Encyclopedia (ABC-CLIO, 2006), s.vv. Rhiannon, Epona; MacKillop, Dictionary of Celtic Mythology (1998), s.v. Rhiannon.
  2. Mac Cana, Proinsias, The Mabinogi, 2nd ed. (University of Wales Press, 1992).

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

Lady ​Charlotte Guest's ‌translation of ‌the Mabinogion ​(1838–49) carried ​Rhiannon out ​of the ​Welsh manuscripts ​and into ‌the ​English-speaking world, ​and the ‌Victorian Celtic ​revival made ‌her one ​of the ‌best-known figures ​of Welsh ​tradition.[1] ​Evangeline Walton's ​twentieth-century retellings ​of the ‌Four Branches, ​beginning with ‌The Virgin ​and the ‌Swine ​(1936) and ‌completed in ​the Mabinogion ‌tetralogy, kept ​her before ​modern readers ‌as the ‌proud queen ‌of ​Dyfed.[2]

In ​1975 Fleetwood ‌Mac's 'Rhiannon' ​— written ​by Stevie ‌Nicks, who ​took the ​name from ‌a ​novel and ‌only afterwards ​learned that ​it belonged ​to a ​Welsh goddess ‌— made ‌the name ​famous ‌worldwide; the ​given name ​Rhiannon, already ‌living in ‌Wales, spread ‌across the ‌English-speaking world ​in the ‌song's ​wake.[3] In ​modern Druid ​and pagan ‌practice she ​is honoured ​as a ​goddess of ‌horses, ​sovereignty, and ​endurance under ​injustice, and ‌scholars of ​the Mabinogi ‌rank her ​among the ‌most fully ​realized ‌women in ‌medieval European ​literature — ​a figure ​whose dignity ​the prose ‌never once ‌allows to ​slip.

Sources

  1. The Mabinogion, trans. Lady Charlotte Guest, 3 vols. (London, 1838–49).
  2. Walton, Evangeline, The Virgin and the Swine (1936); the Mabinogion tetralogy (Ballantine, 1970–74).
  3. Fleetwood Mac, 'Rhiannon (Will You Ever Win)', Fleetwood Mac (Reprise, 1975), written by Stevie Nicks.

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

No ​cult site, ‌votive deposit, ‌or inscription ​of Rhiannon ​is known; ​her attestation ​is ​entirely literary, ‌and the ​reconstructed *Rīgantonā ​has no ‌epigraphic witness ​anywhere in ‌the ​Celtic world.[1] ‌Her evidence ​is manuscript ​evidence. The ​Four Branches ​survive ​complete in ‌the White ​Book of ‌Rhydderch (Aberystwyth, ​National Library ‌of Wales, ​Peniarth ‌MSS 4–5, ​c. 1350) ‌and the ​Red Book ​of Hergest ‌(Oxford, Jesus ‌College ‌MS 111, ​c. 1382–1410), ​with earlier ‌fragments in ​Peniarth 6; ​the tales ‌themselves ​are dated ​by debate ‌rather than ​document, most ‌often to ​the ​later eleventh ​or early ​twelfth century.[2]

‌The geography ‌of her ​story is ‌real: ​Arberth, the ​chief court ‌of Dyfed, ‌stands by ‌modern Narberth ‌in Pembrokeshire, ​and ‌the mound ​tradition attaches ​to the ​country around ‌it — ​but no ​sanctuary ​of hers ‌is known. ​The nearest ​material horizon ​is comparative, ‌not ​hers: the ‌dedications to ​the Gaulish ‌horse-goddess Epona ​recovered by ‌the hundred ‌across ​the Roman ​west, against ​which scholars ​have long ‌read the ‌rider of ​Arberth.[1]

Sources

  1. Koch, John T., ed., Celtic Culture: A Historical Encyclopedia (ABC-CLIO, 2006), s.vv. Rhiannon, Epona.
  2. Davies, Sioned, trans., The Mabinogion (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2007), introduction, on manuscripts and dating.

Irish Mythological Cycles

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Rhiannon belongs to ​Welsh rather than Irish ‌literature, but the Irish cycles ‌supply her closest mythic kin, ​and the comparison has ​been a fixture of Mabinogi ​scholarship for over a ​century. The nearest analogue is ​Macha — above all the ‌Macha of the Noínden ​Ulad, the Debility of the ​Ulstermen: the otherworldly woman ‌who comes unbidden to the ​mortal Cruinniuc as his wife, ‌is boasted of by ​her husband before the king ‌of Ulster, and is ​forced, heavily pregnant, to race ​the king's horses. She wins, ​delivers twins on the ​field, and dies cursing the ​men of Ulster with ‌the pangs of childbirth in ​their hour of need. Horse-swiftness, ‌a marriage across the ​world's boundary, a humiliation imposed ‌by a king, and ​endurance that outlasts the sentence ‌— the cluster is Rhiannon's ​own, and Mac Cana ‌sets the two figures side ​by side as reflexes ​of one sovereignty-and-horse complex.[1]

The ‌second Irish bridge is sovereignty ‌itself. Irish literature knows ‌the goddess of the territory ​who chooses and validates ​the king: Étaín of the ‌Tochmarc Étaíne, the woman the ​gods and kings of ​Ireland contend over, and above ‌all the sovereignty of ​Tara, whose kingship is conferred ​in a bridal — the ‌banais rígi — with ​the woman of the land.[2] ‌The First Branch runs on ​the same economy: Rhiannon ​appears at the mound, names ​the man she will ​marry, and Pwyll's union with ‌her reads as his investiture ‌in Dyfed as much ​as his wedding.[1] Sims-Williams's survey ‌of Irish influence on ​Welsh literature registers the comparative ​frame while warning against over-reading ‌borrowing into parallel inheritance.[4]

‌One Irish figure is best ‌read as contrast rather ‌than parallel. At the ford ​of the Unius on Samain ‌night the Mórrígan — ​the war-goddess as washer at ​the ford — meets ​the Dagda before the battle ‌of Mag Tuired, and the ​washer-at-the-ford figure of Irish ​and Scottish tradition stands at ​the water's edge to ‌prophesy death to those who ​pass.[3] Rhiannon stands at the ​same kind of threshold ​— the mound of Arberth, ‌where a man either ​suffers blows or sees a ‌wonder — but her economy ​is the opposite: she ‌does not announce doom, she ​endures a false one, ‌at the horse-block and under ‌the collars of asses, until ​the sentence collapses of ​its own weight. The Irish ​woman at the ford ​foretells what will be; the ‌Welsh woman at the mound ‌waits out what never ​should have been.[1]

  1. Mac Cana, Proinsias, The Mabinogi, 2nd ed. (Cardiff: University of Wales Press, 1992), on the Macha parallel and the sovereignty reading of the First Branch.
  2. Koch, John T., ed., Celtic Culture: A Historical Encyclopedia (Santa Barbara: ABC-CLIO, 2006), s.vv. Macha, Étaín, sovereignty, Tara.
  3. Gray, Elizabeth A., ed. and trans., Cath Maige Tuired, Irish Texts Society 52 (1982), the meeting at the ford of the Unius.
  4. Sims-Williams, Patrick, Irish Influence on Medieval Welsh Literature (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2011).

Welsh Sources

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Rhiannon's whole ​dossier is ‌the Four ‌Branches of ​the Mabinogi ​— Pedeir ​Keinc y Mabinogi ​— and ​she appears ‌in three ​of the ​four. The First ‌Branch, Pwyll ​Pendefig Dyfed, ‌is her ​great narrative: ‌the uncatchable ​ride, the choice ​of Pwyll, ​the defeat ​of Gwawl, ​the May-eve ‌loss, the penance ​of the ‌horse-block, and ​the restoration ‌of Pryderi. ​The Second ‌Branch, Branwen ferch ​Llŷr, knows ‌her only ​by her ​birds, the ‌Adar Rhiannon whose ‌song attends ‌the survivors ​at Harlech ​and Gwales. ‌The Third ​Branch, Manawydan fab ​Llŷr, gives ‌her the ​second marriage, ​the enchantment ‌of Dyfed, and ​the collars ‌of asses.[1]

​The tales ​survive complete ​in two ​manuscripts: the White ‌Book of ‌Rhydderch (c. ​1350) and ‌the Red ​Book of Hergest ​(c. 1382–1410), ‌with earlier ‌fragments in ‌Peniarth 6. ‌Their date ​of composition is ‌argued rather ​than documented ​— Ifor ​Williams placed ‌them in the ​later eleventh ​century, and ​current scholarship ‌generally holds ​a range ​around 1060–1120 — ​and their ‌author is ​unknown.[2] Lady ‌Charlotte Guest's ​translation (1838–49) first ‌carried them ​into English; ‌Ifor Williams's ‌edition (1930) ​fixed the ​standard Welsh text; ​Sioned Davies's ​translation (2007) ‌is the ‌current scholarly ​English version.[3]

Sources

  1. The Mabinogi, trans. Sioned Davies (Oxford University Press, 2007), introduction and texts.
  2. Williams, Ifor, ed., Pedeir Keinc y Mabinogi (University of Wales Press, 1930); Davies, The Mabinogion (2007), introduction, on date and manuscripts.
  3. The Mabinogion, trans. Lady Charlotte Guest, 3 vols. (London, 1838–49).

Inscriptions & Seals

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

No ​inscription names ‌Rhiannon, and ‌none names ​the ​reconstructed *Rīgantonā. ​The early ​inscribed ​stones of ‌Wales — ​the Latin ​and ‌ogham memorial ​stones of ‌roughly the ​fifth ‌to seventh ​centuries — ​record ​personal names ​in funerary ​formulae, not ‌theonyms; ​votive epigraphy ‌of the ​Welsh kind ‌simply ​does not ‌survive, and ​the ‌theonymic record ​of pre-Christian ​Wales is ‌known ‌from literature ‌and place-lore ​rather than ​stone.[1]

‌The epigraphic ​contrast that ​matters ‌is continental ​and comparative: ​the Gaulish ‌horse-goddess ​Epona is ‌named in ​more than ​two ​hundred Roman-period ​dedications from ‌Britain ‌to the ​Balkans, making ‌her the ​best-attested ​Celtic deity ‌of the ‌ancient world ‌— ‌precisely the ​epigraphic abundance ‌that ​Rhiannon's dossier ​lacks. Scholars ​read the ‌rider ​of Arberth ​against that ​record; they ‌do ​not, and ​cannot, cite ​an ‌inscription for ​her.[2] The ‌honesty of ​the ‌record is ​the point: ‌'Great Queen' ‌is ​a philological ​reconstruction of ​the ​name, not ‌a title ‌recovered from ​stone.

Sources

  1. Jackson, Kenneth H., Language and History in Early Britain (Edinburgh University Press, 1953), on the early inscriptional record.
  2. Koch, John T., ed., Celtic Culture: A Historical Encyclopedia (ABC-CLIO, 2006), s.v. Epona.

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

Rhiannon ​cannot be ‌caught because ‌she has ​already ​decided where ​she is ​going. ​The fastest ‌horses in ​Dyfed gallop ​themselves ‌to exhaustion ​behind a ‌rider who ​is ‌barely trotting ​— and ​the ​moment she ​is asked, ​she stops, ‌because ​stopping was ‌always hers ​to give. ‌That ​is the ‌secret the ​First ‌Branch teaches ​about power: ​the pace ‌of ‌a sovereign ‌is not ​set by ​her ‌pursuers.[1]

The ​rest of ​her ‌story tests ​the lesson. ​Falsely accused, ‌she ​does not ‌rage or ​plead; she ​sits ​down by ​the horse-block ‌and ‌carries her ​guests, one ‌by one, ​until ​the truth ‌walks home ‌on its ‌own ‌legs. Trapped ​in the ‌enchantment, ​she endures ​the collars ​of asses ‌and ​comes out ​still the ​Great Queen. ‌Endurance, ​in her ​telling, is ​not ‌passivity — ​it is ‌the refusal ​to ‌let an ​unjust sentence ‌write your ‌name. ​She waits, ​she carries, ​she ​outlasts; and ‌the land ‌is hers ​again.

Sources

  1. The Mabinogi, First Branch, trans. Davies (2007).
