How Honshū got its accent back

The ASCII form honshu is missing something. Honshū restores the marks the source language used to distinguish this name from a thousand others — and those marks change how the name is read, pronounced, and understood. This post explains, with the full scholarly record behind it, what each restored mark preserves: the Japanese characters evidence, the reconstructed sound, the myths the name carries, and the classification logic that separates Tier 1 restorations from Tier 2. By the end, the marks in Honshū will look less like ornaments and more like what they are — recovered evidence, pinned back in its proper place.

Overview

Honshū (honshu) — The Heart of the Islands — belongs to the Japanese tradition, where it is catalogued under the domain "Center, Mainland, Heart". The name means "'Main island' (本州)".

Honshū is the island that became a civilization: largest of the Japanese archipelago, home of every historic capital from Nara to Kyōto to Edo-Tōkyō, and the ground on which the Jōmon, the Yayoi, and the Yamato state built the longest continuous imperial tradition on earth.

PuniCodex restores the name as Honshū and serves its temple at honshū.com. The restoration preserves the long vowel ū — a distinctive feature the ASCII form loses — which places the name in Tier 1. The plain ASCII form honshu survives as a modern convenience imposed by the early domain-name system; the restoration, not the fallback, is the form the project defends as philologically complete.

The Name

No indigenous written attestation survives for this name; Honshū is a scholarly transliteration of the reconstructed spoken form. Etymologically the name means "'Main island' (本州)".

The ASCII form honshu survives only because the early domain-name system could not carry diacritics; it is a technological compromise, not an ancient spelling. The Unicode restoration Honshū recovers the vowel length of the original directly in the address bar. The restoration preserves the long vowel ū — a distinctive feature the ASCII form loses — which places the name in Tier 1.

The letter-by-letter transformation runs:

  • h → H — Same letter
  • o → o — Same letter
  • n → n — Same letter
  • s → s — Same letter
  • h → h — Same letter
  • u → ū — Macron marks the long vowel

The project holds the domain honshū.com (xn--honsh-pfb.com) as the canonical home of this name.

The Original Script

The form Honshū is therefore a scholarly transliteration rather than an attested ancient spelling; it encodes the reconstructed sound of the name for modern use, and no mark in it is decorative.

Written 本州: hon (root, main, origin) plus shū (province). The Kojiki's mythic name for the island is Ōyashima, "the great eight islands" — the archipelago's whole body, of which Honshū is the spine.

Pronunciation

The reconstructed pronunciation of the name is /hon.ɕuː/ — Japanese (Hepburn).

For the modern speaker, the closest approximation is: HON-shoo, with a long final ū.

Kindred and historical forms of the name:

  • Japanese — 本州
  • Mythic name — Ōyashima
  • Owned form — Honshū

Honshū 本州 — "Main Province" — closes with the long ū of shū, preserved in the macron. The name is descriptive geography elevated to a proper name: the largest of Japan's islands, holding Tokyo, Kyōto, Ōsaka, and some three-quarters of the nation. Both syllables carry long vowels in kana (ほんしゅう); the macron on the final ū marks the doubled u.

Mythology

Honshū's mythology is the national charter itself: the Kojiki's island-birth, the sun goddess's gift of the land, and the descent of her grandson to rule it.

The Islands from the Spear (The Birth)

The Kojiki opens on the heavenly bridge: Izanagi and Izanami stir the ocean with the jewelled spear, and from the brine dripping from its point the first island coagulates. Descending, they wed and give birth to the islands of Japan — the "great eight islands" culminating in Ōyashima — and then to the kami of wind, sea, mountain, and tree. The archipelago is not created but born: the land is kin.

Amaterasu's Realm (The Sun)

When the sun goddess Amaterasu withdrew into the Heavenly Rock-Cave — outraged by her brother Susanoo's rampage — the world went dark until the gods' comic dance lured her out. Her mirror, hung outside the cave, caught her own light back to her; that mirror rests at Ise on Honshū's Kii coast, and her shrine there has been ritually rebuilt every twenty years for thirteen centuries: the island's heartwood, renewed.

Ninigi and the Rice Land (The Descent)

Amaterasu sent her grandson Ninigi down from heaven to rule the "Land of Fair Rice-ears of the Fertile Reed-plain" — the Kojiki's name for Honshū's heartland — bearing the three regalia. His great-grandson Jimmu conquered eastward to Yamato and became the first emperor: the mythic bridge from island-birth to imperial genealogy, anchored to real ground in Nara's basin.

Symbols & Iconography

The iconography associated with Honshū concentrates in a small set of recurring attributes, each a compressed statement about the name:

  • The three regalia — Mirror, sword, and jewel — enshrined on Honshū at Ise, Atsuta, and the palace
  • The rice paddy — The Yayoi inheritance: wet-rice civilization as the island's second foundation
  • The cedar avenue — The planted forests of the pilgrim roads and shrine precincts

Archaeology & Evidence

Sannai-Maruyama near Aomori preserves a Jōmon city of 5,500 years ago — great cedar pillars, longhouses, and chestnut stores that rewrote the era's reputation from "primitive" to prosperous. The Yayoi's moated villages and the keyhole-shaped kofun tombs (Daisenryō in Ōsaka is among the largest tombs on earth by area) trace the state's rise. Nara's Tōdai-ji holds the world's largest bronze Buddha in the world's largest wooden building; Ise's shikinen sengū — the twenty-year rebuilding — is living archaeology, a Bronze Age rite performed on schedule into the present.

Across Cultures

The island is the great syncretist of Asia: Buddhism arriving from Korea in the 6th century was fused with kami-worship into shinbutsu-shūgō — the dual-identity system that ran Japanese religion for a millennium; Zen and the tea culture of Kyōto fused with warrior aesthetics; and the Meiji restoration engineered State Shinto on top of it all. Honshū's mountains hold the shugendō tradition — Yamabushi ascetics practicing the oldest fusion of all: kami, buddha, and mountain as one path.

Within the Japanese tradition, closely related names in the corpus include Fūjin, Hokkaidō, Jizō, Kōbe, Kyōto, and Kyūshū.

Cultural Legacy

Honshū carries roughly 104 million people — the largest island population on earth — and its corridor from Tōkyō to Ōsaka is the most studied urban megalopolis in geography. Its cultural exports need no list: the world's first novel (The Tale of Genji) was written here, Zen gardens and ukiyo-e and kabuki were born here, and the shinkansen that shrank the island in 1964 taught the world high-speed rail.

A Meditation

Honshū is the island that kept everything: the oldest pottery beside the fastest trains, the goddess's mirror beside the parliament. Its lesson is continuity as craft — the shrine rebuilt every twenty years so that it never ages and never changes. It asks the heartland's question: what in your tradition is worth rebuilding, exactly as it was, forever?

The twenty-year rebuilding of Ise is the island's deepest answer to modernity: continuity is not preservation but repetition — the shrine is never old and never new, the cypress always fresh, the craft always taught. Honshū holds its megacities and its mirror with the same hands, and its lesson to every anxious age is the carpenter's: what you rebuild on schedule, you never have to save.

And the island keeps answering in both registers at once: the shinkansen bows to the mountain as it passes; the morning news follows the shrine calendar; the world's largest city ends, an hour out, in cedar valleys where the kami-names predate the state. Honshū's genius was never to choose between the shrine and the station — it scheduled them. The heartland's final lesson is that depth and scale are not enemies: an island can hold a hundred million lives and still keep, at Ise, an empty plot waiting for the next rebuilding, exactly as it was.

Stand at Ise on a rebuilding year: two shrines, one old, one new, standing side by side for a season — the island's whole philosophy visible at a glance. The past is not kept by freezing it but by making it again, carefully, on schedule, forever.

The main island does not boast; it simply holds — the capitals, the shrines, the mountains, and the memory — the spine on which everything else hangs.

The Unicode Restoration

Honshū is classified as Tier 1: the restoration preserves at least one distinctive feature — a diacritic or a distinctive letter — that the ASCII form loses. The ASCII fallback honshu still resolves everywhere, but it is the restored form that carries the name's full information. Across the 6 characters of the name, the restoration adjusts 1: 1 mark of length (ū). That is the whole thesis of this temple: the marks are the message.

The restored name is live as a working domain: honshū.com, which the DNS carries in punycode form as xn--honsh-pfb.com — an ASCII-compatible encoding that lets a non-ASCII name travel the global network without breaking older infrastructure. A Closer Look at the Marks

A restored name is a small map. In Honshū, the journey from the ASCII fallback honshu to the Unicode form can be read letter by letter, and each letter tells a story. Of the 6 characters, 5 remain unchanged; they anchor the name to its modern shape. The remaining adjustments recover marks the source tradition used to distinguish this name from every other word built from the same letters.

The h stays as it is — Same letter.

The o stays as it is — Same letter.

The n stays as it is — Same letter.

The s stays as it is — Same letter.

The h stays as it is — Same letter.

The u becomes ū — Macron marks the long vowel.

Together these changes are not decoration. They are the minimum set of marks needed to keep the name legible in its own tradition. Remove them and you still have a string of letters; you no longer have the name as it was written.
