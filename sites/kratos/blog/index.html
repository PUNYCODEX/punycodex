Mythology

Primary Attestation (Sources)

Name and Meaning (Etymology)

The name means "Strength, power" and reflects the figure's role in the tradition. Krátos is the personification of strength or sovereign power in Greek poetry. In Aeschylus's Prometheus Bound he is the pitiless agent who compels Hephaestus to bind Prometheus, embodying the coercive force that even the craftsman-god cannot refuse. Unlike his brother Bía (Violence), Krátos represents institutionalized, legitimate might. His pairing with Bía in the binding of Prometheus shows that Greek tragedy could personify abstract forces and set them in dialogue with the gods.

Krátos is shown as a muscular, beardless youth wielding a hammer or club, often beside Hephaistos at the crucifixion of Prometheus. His attributes are bonds, anvil, and the reluctant tools of punishment.

The figure blends personification with the aesthetic of the divine craftsman's assistant.

Epithets & Cult Titles

Krátos is paired with Βία (Bia, Force) and called κραταιός (krataios, mighty), καρτερός (karteros, staunch), and δεσμοφύλαξ (desmophylax, guard of bonds). His name becomes an abstract noun for sovereign power, used of gods, kings, and armies.

The absence of cult epithets reflects his near-abstract role as the executive arm of Zeus's will.

The Homeric Hymns

Krátos appears in the opening of Aeschylus's Prometheus Bound as the personification of Strength, dragging the Titan to his punishment at Hephaistos's forge. While not the subject of a Homeric Hymn, his speech in the prologue is itself a hymnic assertion of Zeuss irresistible power.

The fragmentary Prometheus Unbound and Hesiod's Theogony name him a son of the Titan Pallas and the river Styx.

Oracle Sites & Sanctuaries

Krátos had no independent cult or oracle; his presence is felt wherever Zeus's authority is enforced. The Sicyonian temple of Zeus had an altar to Kratos and Bia, honouring the powers that underpinned oath and obedience.

He is therefore a theological personification rather than a sanctuary god.
