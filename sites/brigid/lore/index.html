The Authentic Name

Unicode restoration and ASCII comparison

Scholarly Transliteration

Brigid

The name survives in scholarly transliteration. Brigid is the standard Celtic romanisation, documented in academic sources — “The Exalted One (from Old Irish Brigit)”. Because the spelling uses only Latin letters, the form is the same in both ASCII and Unicode.

No indigenous writing system is securely attested for individual celtic names. The form shown is a modern scholarly transliteration.

ASCII Constraint

brigid

The plain brigid form is identical to the Unicode restoration. Because this name is already written in Latin letters, no diacritics, stress, or script information were lost — only capitalization differs.

Unicode Restoration

Brigid

The Unicode restoration does not need to recover lost marks for Brigid. Its value is canonical spelling and consistent cataloguing, not the reconstruction of erased orthography. The domain is readable as-is to both DNS and humanity.

Pronunciation

How Brigid was spoken

/ˈbrʲiɣʲitʲ/ Old Irish Reconstruction
Br- Voiced bilabial stop [b] and slender (palatalized) trill [rʲ]; Old Irish r was always palatalized after an initial b in this name, as in modern Irish.
-i- Short front [i], held brief; in the later language the vowel lengthened, giving modern Irish Bríd.
-g- Voiced velar fricative [ɣ] between vowels — not a hard stop but a soft continuant, the sound Old Irish wrote g in medial position.
-id Short front [i] and slender dental [dʲ]; the final d was devoiced and palatalized, surviving as the -d of modern Bríd /bʲrʲiːdʲ/.

Modern Approximation

'BRIJ-id' — like 'bridge' split in two, the g softened to a voiced fricative (a gargled g), both r and d slender; the modern Irish form Bríd is simply 'breed'.

Etymological Kin

  • Old Irish Brigit (genitive Brigte), daughter of the Dagda in the Mythological Cycle.
  • Proto-Celtic *Brigantī, 'the Exalted One', source also of the tribal name Brigantes and the Romano-British goddess Brigantia.
  • Modern Irish Bríd, /bʲrʲiːdʲ/, the living form of the name and of the saint.
  • Scottish Gaelic and Manx Brìde and Breeshey, the name carried into the sister Gaelic languages.
  • Watched form brigid.com — held outside the PuniCodex collection and watched for release; no form of the name is currently owned.

The Accent / Script Rule

Brigid is Tier 2 by the mechanical rule: the normalized form is already plain ASCII — the Old Irish spelling Brigit carries no diacritic and no distinctive letter for the restoration to recover. Only the capital distinguishes the temple's form from the input; the name's fame, like Baldr's, vastly outruns its orthography.

05

The Exalted One

Poetry, Smithcraft, Healing — and the Undying Flame of Kildare

Brigit, daughter of the Dagda, is the triple hearth of the Irish gods: Cormac's Glossary gives her two sisters of the same name — Brigit the smith and Brigit the leech beside Brigit the poet — so that the one name covers the forge, the well, and the word. Her feast is Imbolc, the first of February, when the ewes come into milk; and at Kildare a fire burned for her, Giraldus says, that no man might approach and no night ever saw go out.

The Sacred Flame

The perpetual fire of Kildare, tended by nineteen nuns in turn — on the twentieth night, Giraldus says, it was left to Brigid herself.

The Anvil

Smithcraft, one of the three provinces Cormac assigns to the sisterhood of Brigits — the forge where Goibniu's art and mortal craft meet.

The Poet's Harp

The first of her provinces in the Glossary — Brigit banfhiled, Brigit the woman-poet, patroness of the learned craft of filidecht.

The Rush Cross

St Brigid's cross, woven of rushes on Imbolc eve — the small loom on which the goddess's feast was carried into the saint's day.

Sacred Symbols

The flame of Kildare The fire that neither dies nor accumulates ash — patronage that outlasts the night-watch of its keepers
The anvil and the forge Smithcraft, the making province — skill that transforms raw metal the way poetry transforms raw speech
The holy well Healing drawn from the ground — the countless wells of St Brigit where the leech-sister's province is still visited
Fea and Femen, the two oxen The Dagda's daughter's beasts, who gave their names to plains of Ireland — wealth and the working land
Imbolc The first of February, the milk returning to the ewes — the year's first hinge, kept as her day
06

Mythology

Stories of Brigid

Irish tradition gives Brigit few long tales but a peculiar density of testimony: the great pseudohistory of the Lebor Gabála knows her genealogy, the battle-tale Cath Maige Tuired gives her the first keening heard in Ireland, and Cormac's Glossary preserves the triad that made her three goddesses in one name.

Lebor Gabála Érenn

The Dagda's Daughter

The Book of Invasions places Brigit firmly in the family of the Túatha Dé Danann: Brigit the poetess, daughter of the Dagda, the good god who owns the cauldron and the club. The same tradition marries her to Bres, the half-Fomorian king whose beautiful face and niggardly rule nearly undid the Túatha Dé Danann, and gives her a son, Ruadán — so that the exalted one stands, by marriage and blood, on both sides of the great conflict of Irish myth. The Lebor Gabála is pseudohistory, written by Christian scholars ordering the gods into an ancestry, but its genealogies are the tradition's own filing system: Brigit belongs to the Dagda's house.

Sanas Cormaic

Three Sisters, One Name

Cormac mac Cuilennáin's glossary, compiled around 900, preserves the tradition's clearest statement of her triple nature. Brigit, it says, is the poetess, daughter of the Dagda — and she had two sisters, Brigits also: Brigit the woman of smithcraft and Brigit the female physician, from whose names all the Irish used to call a goddess Brigit. Three crafts, three sisters, one name: from this single entry comes the familiar picture of the triple goddess of poetry, smithcraft, and healing. Whether the triad records three genuinely distinct figures or one goddess's three provinces arranged in the Irish love of threes, the Glossary is the witness every later account follows.

Cath Maige Tuired

The First Keening in Ireland

The Second Battle of Mag Tuired gives Brigit her one moment of action, and it is grief. Her son Ruadán, sent by his Fomorian kin to spy on the Túatha Dé Danann, hurls a spear at the smith Goibniu; the smith draws it from his own wound and casts it back, and Ruadán goes to his father to die. Brigit comes to the field and bewails her son — and the text says she wept and shrieked over him, the first keening and the first whistling for grief that was heard in Ireland. The inventor of lamentation is a terrible dignity: the goddess of the word is the one who first taught Ireland what a mother says at a grave.

Giraldus Cambrensis, Topographia Hiberniae

The Fire at Kildare

Writing about 1188, Gerald of Wales describes the wonder of Kildare with a traveller's precision: a fire hedged round with a circular fence of brushwood, which no male may cross — any man who tries, he says, does not escape Brigid's vengeance. Nineteen nuns tend the flame, one each night, each adding fuel; on the twentieth night the last nun lays the wood and says, 'Brigid, guard your fire, it is yours.' In the morning the fire is found still burning, and its ashes, Gerald marvels, never increase though the wood is always consumed. He records it as a marvel of the saint's church; scholarship has never doubted that the hedge, the number nineteen, and the unashing flame belong to a far older hearth.

Sanas Cormaic · the quarter days

Imbolc, the Ewe's Milk

Her feast stands at Imbolc, the first of February, one of the four great hinges of the Irish year beside Beltaine, Lugnasad, and Samain. Cormac's Glossary explains the word itself as oimelc, 'ewe's milk' — the season when the sheep come into milk and the year's hunger begins to turn. The old texts keep the day as Brigit's own; the keening, the forge, and the word all gather at the one date, when the light is just beginning to return and the flocks give their first milk. It is the oldest continuously observed fact about her: whatever else changed, the first of February stayed hers.

Extended Lore

Brigit is the rare goddess whose three provinces are one act seen from three sides. The smith and the poet do the same thing: they take what is raw — ore, breath — and by heat and pressure give it a form that outlasts them. The healer does it too, coaxing the broken body back toward the shape it was made for. Cormac's three sisters are really a single lesson: that making is sacred wherever it happens, at the anvil, on the tongue, or at the sickbed. Her fire at Kildare, hedged and watched and never consumed, is the emblem of that lesson — a flame tended by nineteen keepers that still belongs, on the twentieth night, to the name itself. Craft, kept in common, does not go out.
