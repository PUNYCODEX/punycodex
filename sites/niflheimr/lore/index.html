The Authentic Name

Unicode restoration and ASCII comparison

Original Script

ᚾᛁᚠᛚᚼᛁᛘᚱ

The name in its original Norse form. Niflheimr (ᚾᛁᚠᛚᚼᛁᛘᚱ) is attested in the source tradition — “Mist-home”. Its original diacritics and script distinctions carry the full phonetic and orthographic weight of the source tradition.

ASCII Constraint

niflheimr

The plain niflheimr form is identical to the Unicode restoration. Because this name is already written in Latin letters, no diacritics, stress, or script information were lost — only capitalization differs.

Unicode Restoration

Niflheimr

The Unicode restoration does not need to recover lost marks for Niflheimr. Its value is canonical spelling and consistent cataloguing, not the reconstruction of erased orthography. The domain is readable as-is to both DNS and humanity.

Punycode Encoding
Niflheimr.com → niflheimr.com

The non-ASCII characters in Niflheimr are encoded while the ASCII remains visible. To the DNS, it is Punycode. To humanity, it is Niflheimr.

Pronunciation

How Niflheimr was spoken

/ˈnivlˌhɛimr̩/ Old Norse Reconstruction
Nifl- Dental nasal [n], short close [i], then the f voiced to [v] between voiced sounds — Old Norse wrote f but heard v, as in modern Icelandic Niflheimur — and a clear [l].
-heimr Voiceless [h], the falling diphthong [ɛi] (Old Norse ei, as in modern 'hay' gliding down), bilabial [m], and the final syllabic nominative [r̩] — the masculine -r ending, sounded as its own light beat.

Modern Approximation

NIVL-haym-r — the f is soft, sounding as v; the second element glides like 'aim' with an h in front; end with a light trilled r that carries its own syllable.

Etymological Kin

  • Old Norse niflheimr, the masculine compound 'mist-world'; the manuscript form, unchanged since the Eddas.
  • Modern Icelandic Niflheimur, the learned word for the primordial mist-world, kept alive from the medieval corpus.
  • Old Norse near-kin niflhel 'mist-Hel', the poetic word for the world below Hel — Vafþrúðnismál 43 and Baldrs draumar 2 use it where the prose says Niflheimr.
  • Indo-European kin Latin nebula, Greek nephélē 'cloud', Sanskrit nábhas 'cloud, mist' — the first element descends from the inherited root *nebh- 'cloud, fog'.

The Accent / Script Rule

Niflheimr is Tier 2 by the mechanical rule: the normalized Old Norse form is already plain ASCII — no diacritic and no distinctive letter for the ASCII alphabet to lose. Only the capital distinguishes the restored form from the input; the mist-world was never hard to type, only hard to imagine.

The Mist-World Before the World

Hvergelmir, the Élivágar, and the Northern Pole of Ginnungagap

Niflheimr is the oldest address in the Norse cosmos: the world of fog and rime that existed, Snorri says, many ages before the earth was made — the cold northern pole of the yawning gap, from whose roaring well every river in creation flows and from whose poisoned ice the first living thing quickened.

Hvergelmir

The roaring kettle: the well in Niflheimr's middle, whence Grímnismál says all waters have their course.

The Élivágar

The ice-waves: eleven rivers older than the earth — Svöl, Gunnþrá, Fjörm, Fimbulþul, Slíðr, Hríð, Sylgr, Ylgr, Víð, Leiptr, and Gjöll, which runs nearest the gate of Hel.

The Rime and the Venom

Frost fog layered across the gap and the yeasty eitr that hardened like dross from a fire — creation's raw material, and its warning.

Níðhöggr

The malice-striker: the serpent that gnaws Yggdrasill's third root from below, where it stands over Niflheimr above the well.

Sacred Symbols

Hvergelmir, the roaring kettle The source beneath all sources: every river in the world is one of its children
The Élivágar The ice-waves older than the earth — water carrying the venom that froze into the first flesh
The rime fog Cold as a substance, not an absence: the raw material the warm south breathed on until it lived
06

Mythology

Stories of Niflheimr

Niflheimr's mythology is the mythology of before: it stars in no adventure and houses no hero, because its story is over before the gods' stories begin — and yet every river, every frost giant, and every corpse that sinks past Hel is still paying its tribute to the mist-world.

Gylfaginning 4

The World That Came First

Snorri's chronology begins not with a god but with a climate. Niflheimr, he says, was made many ages before the earth was created, and in its middle is the well called Hvergelmir, from which flow the rivers: Svöl the cool, Gunnþrá the battle-defiant, Fjörm the swift, Fimbulþul the loud-babbling, Slíðr the fearsome and Hríð the storming, Sylgr and Ylgr, Víð the broad, and Leiptr the flashing — and Gjöll, the resounding, which runs nearest the gate of Hel. Before there was a world to water, the mist-world already had its watershed.

Gylfaginning 5–7 · Vafþrúðnismál 21

The Yeasty Venom and the Quickening

The Élivágar ran so far from their source that the yeasty venom in them hardened, like dross running out of a fire, and turned to ice; rime and fog rose layer upon layer across Ginnungagap's northern half, while sparks and molten flecks flew out of Muspellsheimr in the south. Where the warm breath met the rime, the ice thawed and dripped — and the dripping drops quickened into the likeness of a man, named Ymir by the gods and Aurgelmir, the mud-yeller, by the rime-giants who descend from him. The Poetic Edda keeps the same recipe in three lines: out of Élivágar spurted venom-drops, Vafþrúðnismál says, and grew until a giant was made. The first life in the North was brewed from poisoned ice thawed by a fire it could not see.

Gylfaginning 15–16 · Grímnismál 26

The Root Over the Well

When the world was finally built, Niflheimr kept the oldest seat in it. Yggdrasill's third root, Snorri says, extends over Niflheimr, and beneath that root is Hvergelmir, and the serpent Níðhöggr gnaws the root from below — the ash is always being eaten at its oldest corner. Grímnismál completes the water-cycle: the hart Eikþyrnir stands on Valhöll's roof cropping the leaves, and from his horns it drips into Hvergelmir, whence all waters have their course. The rivers of gods and giants alike — the Fjörm and the Slíðr, the Gjöll and the Leiptr — are one well's outflow, and the well sits in the mist.

Vafþrúðnismál 43 · Baldrs draumar 2 · Gylfaginning 3

Niflhel, Down in the Ninth World

In the poems the mist-world darkens into something lower. Óðinn, proving his wisdom to the giant Vafþrúðnir, says he journeyed through nine worlds below Niflhel; in Baldrs draumar he saddles Sleipnir and rides down to Niflhel to wake a dead seeress and wring the prophecy of Baldr's death from her. Snorri states the grim geography plainly: evil men go to Hel, and thence down to Niflhel — that is down in the ninth world. The world that was first becomes the world that is lowest: creation's old north pole, demoted to the cellar of the dead.

Extended Lore

Niflheimr is the patron of what was here first. Before the gods, before the giants, before the earth itself, there was a world of mist with a well in the middle of it, and rivers already running. The Norse imagination refused to let creation begin with a decree; it began with weather — with frost accumulating in a silence no one had yet named, thawed, at last, by a warmth it never asked for. Every river still runs from that well, which is the myth's way of saying that every living thing is downstream of the cold it rose from.
