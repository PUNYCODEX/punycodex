01

Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Loki (loki) ​— Trickery, Chaos, ‌Shape-shifter · possibly 'knot' ‌or 'lock' — ​etymology uncertain — ​is the trickster of ​the Norse Æsir: ​not a god ​by birth but the ‌giant Fárbauti's son ​by Laufey, admitted ​to the fellowship of ‌the gods through ​an oath of ‌blood-brotherhood with Óðinn, ​and the author of ‌both their finest ​treasures and their ​mortal wound.[1]

The Eddas ​give him more ​narrative than any ​figure save Þórr and ‌Óðinn. He cuts ​off Sif's hair ‌and wagers his own ​head against the ‌dwarfs' craft, from ​which the gods ‌receive the spear Gungnir, ​the ship Skíðblaðnir, ‌the ring Draupnir, ​the boar Gullinbursti, and ​Þórr's hammer Mjǫllnir.[2] ‌He accompanies Þórr ‌to Þrymr's hall disguised ‌as a bridesmaid ​and talks the ​party through the wedding ‌feast.[3] And he ​finds the one ​thing that never ‌swore Frigg's oath — ​the mistletoe — ​and guides the ‌blind Hǫðr's hand, killing ​Baldr.[4] The Lokasenna ‌stages the rupture: ​a full flyting against ​every god in ​Ægir's hall, then ​the flight to ‌Franangr, the salmon-shape, the ‌net of his ​own devising, and ‌the binding with the ​entrails of his ​son Nari beneath ‌a venom-dripping serpent, where ‌Sigyn holds her ‌bowl until Ragnarök.[1] ‌At the end he ​steers the ship ‌Naglfar against the ​gods and falls ​with Heimdallr, each slaying ​the other.[5]

PuniCodex ‌catalogs the name ​as Loki and serves ​its temple at ​/loki/. The name ‌is Tier 2: the ​normalized Old Norse ​spelling is Loki, ​with a short o ‌and no distinctive ​letter, so the ‌plain ASCII form ​loses nothing — the ‌restoration and the ​ASCII form are ‌the same word. The ‌plain-ASCII domain loki.com ​is registered to ​a third party, held ​outside the collection, ​and watched for ‌release; no owned domain ‌is claimed for ​this entry.

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

The name ​is attested ‌in normalized Old ‌Norse as ​Loki (nominative; genitive ​Loka), spelled ​throughout the manuscript ​tradition with a ​short o ‌and no diacritic.[1] ​Its etymology ​is genuinely uncertain, ‌and honest ​lexicography says so: ‌the most ​discussed derivation connects ‌it to ​the Germanic luk- ​family — Old ​Norse lok ​'cover, lid, bolt, ​lock' and ‌the verb lúka ​'to close, ‌to end' — ​whence the ‌proposed senses 'knot', ​'lock', 'loop', ‌or 'the one ​who closes', the ‌last reading ​attractive for the ​god who ‌brings the world's ‌end.[2] The ‌nineteenth-century equation of ​Loki with ​Logi 'flame' is ‌abandoned: the ​o of Loki ​is short, and ‌Logi appears ​in Gylfaginning as ​a separate ‌figure, the personified ​wildfire who ‌defeats Loki in ​an eating ​contest.[3]

Because the ​Old Norse ​spelling is identical ‌to the ASCII ‌spelling, the ​name is classified ‌Tier 2: ​the restoration preserves ​no distinctive ‌feature that ASCII ‌loses. The ‌lexicon also records ‌an attested ​variant Lóki (alt-stress, ‌acute on o), ​sourced to ​Cleasby-Vigfusson and the ​Poetic Edda; ‌the normalized form ​Loki remains ​primary.

The letter-by-letter ​transformation runs:

‌- l → ​L — ​Same, capitalized

  • ​o → o ‌— Same

​- k → ‌k — ​Same

  • i ‌→ i ​— Same

The ‌project holds ‌no domain for ​this name: ​loki.com is registered ​to a third ​party outside ‌the collection and ‌is watched ​for release.

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The reconstructed ​pronunciation of ‌the name ‌is /ˈlo.ke/ ​— Old ​Norse Reconstruction.[1]

​Phoneme by ​phoneme:

  • ​Lo- — ‌Voiced alveolar ​lateral [l] ​followed by ‌short mid ​back [o], ‌as in ​English lot; ‌stressed on ​the first ​syllable, as ​in all ​Old Norse ​words
  • ‌-ki — ​Unaspirated velar ‌stop [k] ​followed by ‌short close ​front [i]; ‌the second ​syllable falls away ‌lightly

For ​the modern ​speaker, the ‌closest approximation ‌is: 'LOH-kee' ‌— two ​even syllables, ​the o ‌short and ​round, never ​the diphthong ‌of English ​low.

Kindred ​and historical ‌forms of ​the name:

‌- Old ​Norse — ​Loki, the ​manuscript form; ​also by ‌the byname ‌Loptr, 'the ​airy one'[2]
‌- Modern ​Icelandic — ​Loki, unchanged, ‌and still ‌current as ‌a given name
‌- Faroese ​ballad tradition ‌— Lokki, ​the benevolent ​helper of ​the Lokka ‌táttur[3]

  • ​Distinct figure ​— Útgarða-Loki, ​the giant ‌king of ​Útgarðr, who ​shares the ​name but ‌not the ​identity[1]

Loki ‌is Tier ​2 because ‌the ASCII ​form is ‌philologically complete: ‌English 'Loki' ​loses no ​sound, no ​length, and ​no letter ‌that the ‌Old Norse ​original carries.

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

The name ​is preserved ‌in medieval ‌Icelandic manuscripts ​in the Latin ​alphabet as ​Loki — ​Old Norse ​(North Germanic), attested ‌13th c. ​CE in ​the Codex Regius ‌of the ​Poetic Edda ‌and the ​principal Snorra Edda ‌codices, recording ​material centuries ​older.[1] Before ​the Latin bookhand, ​Old Norse ​names were ‌carved in ​the Younger Futhark; ‌no runic ​attestation of ‌this theonym ​survives, so the ‌manuscript form ​is the ‌primary witness, ​and the epigraphic ​rendering ᛚᚢᚴᛁ ‌is a ‌reconstruction only.

The ‌scholarly rendering ​proceeds step ​by step:

‌- The name ​is a ​single uncompounded ‌word of ​uncertain etymology; the ​luk- family ‌('lock, knot, ​closing') is ‌the most discussed ​derivation, the ​logi 'flame' ​equation being ​abandoned.[2]

  • Old ‌Norse orthography ‌writes it ​Loki, with ‌a short o ​that takes ​no length ‌mark and no ‌distinctive letter.

‌- Because ‌the normalized ​spelling and the ‌ASCII spelling ​are identical, ​the name ​is Tier 2: ‌the restoration ​preserves no ​feature ASCII ​loses. The lexicon ‌records the ​attested variant ​Lóki (alt-stress) ​alongside the primary ‌form.[3]

  • ​No domain ‌is owned ​for this name: ‌the plain-ASCII ​loki.com — ‌the philologically complete ‌form — ​is registered ​to a ​third party, held ​outside the ‌PuniCodex collection, ‌and watched ​for release.

Domains & Attributes

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sphere of influence, titles, epithets, domain cards.

Loki ​is catalogued ‌under Trickery, ‌Chaos, ​Shape-shifter — ​and ​the tradition ​earns every ​element ‌of it.[1]

​### ​The Trickster

‌He is ​the ‌mind that ​finds ‌the loophole: ​in Frigg's ​oath, ​in the ​wall-builder's ​contract, in ‌the dwarfs' ​wager. ‌Skáldskaparmál's kenning-list ​names ‌him 'slanderer ​of the ‌gods' ​and 'thief ‌of ​the gods'.[2]

​### The ‌Shape-Shifter

‌Salmon in ‌the ​falls of ​Franangr, mare ‌to ​the stallion ​Svaðilfari, ‌fly at ​the dwarfs' ​bellows, ‌old woman ​drawing ‌secrets from ​Frigg: his ​body ​is the ​one ‌thing about ‌him that ​never ‌holds still.[1]

​### ​The Companion

‌On the ‌road ‌he is ‌indispensable ​— the ‌wit that ​recovers ​Mjǫllnir in ​Þrymskviða ‌and talks ​Þórr's bridal ​party ​through Þrymr's ‌feast.[3]

​### The ​End-Bringer

By ​Angrboða ‌he fathers ​Fenrir, ‌Jǫrmungandr, and ​Hel; at ‌Ragnarök ​he breaks ‌his ‌bonds, steers ​Naglfar against ​the ​gods, and ​falls ‌with Heimdallr ‌in mutual ​killing.[4]

Symbols

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Iconography, attributes, and their meanings.

Loki's iconography ​is narrative ‌rather than ‌cultic — ​the corpus ​gives him ​no temple ​regalia, but his ​myths fixed ‌a tight ​set of ​attributes:[1]

  • ‌The sewn ​lips — ‌Brokkr's needle ​after the lost ‌wager: the ​trickster's own ​weapon, speech, ​bound shut; ​the scars ​are the ‌one permanent mark ​the myths ‌leave on ​him
  • ‌The mistletoe ​dart — ‌the one ​thing too young ‌to swear ​Frigg's oath, ​shaped into ‌the weapon ‌that kills ‌Baldr: the ​loophole made ​lethal
  • The ‌net of ​Franangr — ​the first ‌net ever ​devised, invented ​by Loki ‌himself and then ​used by ‌the Æsir ​to take ​him from ​the falls: ​the snare ‌that turns on ‌its maker

​- The ‌salmon shape ​— the ​slippery, upstream ‌form he ‌takes in ‌flight; even his ‌hiding-place is ​a thing ‌of currents
​- The ​serpent and ​the bowl ‌— Skadi's venom-dripping ​snake above ​his face, ​and Sigyn's ‌bowl that ​catches the ​drops until ​she must turn ‌to empty ​it: the ‌machinery of ​his binding[2]
‌- The ​mare's shape ‌— the ‌form in which ​he bore ​Sleipnir, Óðinn's ​eight-legged horse: ​parenthood across ‌the line ‌of species ​and sex

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

Loki's mythology is the largest ​of any single figure in the Norse corpus: ‌he is the gods' companion, their supplier of wonders, ‌their betrayer, and their doom, and the Eddas ​never reconcile these into one character.[1]

The Blood-Brother ​(Lokasenna 9)

When Óðinn moves to silence him in ​Ægir's hall, Loki invokes the oldest bond in ​the mythology: 'Do you remember, Óðinn, how in days ​of old we two blended our blood together? ‌You said you would never taste ale unless it ​were brought to us both.' The oath explains ​everything structural about him — why a giant's son ‌sits among the Æsir, and why his betrayal, when ​it comes, is kin-slaying rather than warfare.[2]

‌The Flyting at Ægir's Hall (Lokasenna)

Uninvited to Ægir's ​feast, Loki kills the servant Fimafeng, is driven ‌out, returns on the strength of the oath, and ​then insults every god and goddess in turn ​— Bragi, Iðunn, Gefjon, Óðinn, Frigg, Freyja, Njǫrðr, Týr, ​Freyr, Byggvir, Heimdallr, Skadi, Sif — finding for each ​the wound that will not close. Only Þórr's ​arrival ends it: three vows with Mjǫllnir, and Loki ‌yields the floor. The prose epilogue carries him ​straight to Franangr and the binding.[2]

The Companion ‌on the Road (Þrymskviða)

When Þrymr steals Mjǫllnir ​and demands Freyja, Loki borrows her feather-coat, learns the ‌terms, and rides beside the disguised Þórr as his ​maidservant — supplying the ready answers ('Freyja has ‌not eaten for eight nights, so eager was she ​for Jǫtunheimr') that keep the disguise alive until ‌the hammer is in the bride's lap. The poem ​preserves the benevolent Loki the later tradition needed ​to explain.[3]

The Wager of the Head (Skáldskaparmál)

‌For mischief he cuts off Sif's hair; to escape ‌Þórr's hands he procures from the sons of ‌Ívaldi the golden hair, the ship Skíðblaðnir, and the ​spear Gungnir — then wagers his own head ​that the dwarf Brokkr's brother Eitri cannot match them. ‌In the smithy he stings the bellows-worker as ​a fly, yet the work holds: Gullinbursti, Draupnir, and ​Mjǫllnir, short-handled from the sting but judged the best ‌of all. Loki concedes his head — and ​points out that the neck was never in the ​bargain. The dwarfs sew his lips shut instead.[4]

‌### The Death of Baldr (Gylfaginning 49)

In a ​woman's shape he draws from Frigg the single ‌omission in her oath — the mistletoe, too young ​to swear. He pulls it up, puts it ​in the blind Hǫðr's hand, and guides the throw; ​Baldr falls dead. When Hermóðr wins the condition that ​all things must weep Baldr back from Hel, ‌all things weep except the cave-giantess Þǫkk — 'let ‌Hel hold what she has' — and the ​Æsir believe Þǫkk was Loki.[5]

The Binding (Gylfaginning ‌50; Lokasenna prose)

Hiding on Franangr in a ​house with four doors, Loki takes salmon-shape in the ​falls and, brooding on how he might be caught, ‌devises the net — the first ever made. ‌Óðinn spies him from Hliðskjálf; the gods read the ‌ash-pattern of the burnt net, remake it, and ‌drag the falls until the salmon is taken. Then ​the punishment: his son Váli is changed into ‌a wolf and tears apart his brother Nari (Narfi ​in the Lokasenna prose), and with Nari's entrails the ​Æsir bind Loki across three stones; Skadi fastens ​a venomous serpent above his face. Sigyn, his wife, ‌holds a bowl beneath the drops, and only ​when she turns to empty it does the venom ​strike him — his writhing, Snorri says, is ​what men call earthquakes.[6]

Ragnarök (Gylfaginning 51; Vǫluspá)

‌At the end the bonds break. Loki steers Naglfar ​— the ship built of dead men's nails ​— against the gods, while his children by the ​giantess Angrboða close in: Fenrir with jaws from ‌earth to heaven, Jǫrmungandr blowing venom, Hel's host behind ​him. On the field of Vígríðr he meets ‌Heimdallr, and they kill each other. The sibyl of ​the Vǫluspá saw it centuries before Snorri: 'the kinsman ‌of Býleistr' steers the ship from the east.[7]

​### A Necessary Distinction: Útgarða-Loki and Logi

Two figures ‌in Gylfaginning share his name or his old ‌reputation and are neither of them Loki Laufeyjarson. Útgarða-Loki ​is the giant king of Útgarðr whose illusions ​humiliate Þórr on the eastern journey — a separate ​being, and Snorri never confuses them. And Logi, 'wildfire', ​who beats Loki in the eating contest at ‌Útgarðr, is the fire itself in person — the ‌figure with whom the nineteenth century wrongly fused ​the god into a fire-deity.[8]

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

The standing ​comparative frame ‌for Loki ‌is the ​Greek Promētheús: ​a trickster-benefactor ​chained to ​rock, torn ​daily — ‌the eagle ​and the ​dripping serpent were ‌paired as ​early as ‌the Romantic ​mythographers, and ‌Rooth's monograph ​remains the ​standard weighing ​of the ​parallel. The ​comparison illuminates ‌the punishment but ​not the ‌god: Promētheús ​suffers for ‌helping mankind, ​Loki for ‌destroying his ​own fellowship.[1]

‌The nineteenth ​century dissolved ​Loki into ‌a fire-god on ‌the strength ‌of his ​name's resemblance ​to Logi ‌'flame' — ​the conflation ​Wagner's Loge ‌made operatic ​— but ​modern philology ‌rejects it: the ​o of ‌Loki is ​short, and ​the texts ​keep Logi ​and Loki ‌apart as ‌separate figures.[2] ​A second ‌honest distinction ​concerns Útgarða-Loki: the ​cunning giant-king ‌of the ‌eastern journey ‌shares the ‌name but ​not the ‌identity, and ​scholarship since ​de Vries ​has treated ‌the coincidence as ​onomastic, however ​suggestive the ​parallel mastery ‌of illusion.[3] ​Within the ​corpus, closely ​related figures ‌include Óðinn ​(his blood-brother), ‌Þórr (his ​traveling companion), Baldr ‌(his victim), ​and Heimdallr ‌(his last ‌opponent), with ​Sigyn (his ​wife) and ​the monstrous ​children Fenrir, ‌Jǫrmungandr, and ‌Hel completing ​the household.

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

Loki's afterlife ​forked early ‌and never rejoined. ‌The folk ​record kept ​a kinder god: ​the Faroese ​ballad Lokka táttur ​makes Lokki ‌the clever helper ​who, with ​Óðinn and Þórr, ‌saves a ​farmer's son ‌from a giant ​— a ‌benevolent trickster surviving ​in oral ​tradition long after ​the Eddic ​damnation.[1] Learned Europe ​remade him ‌as fire: ​Wagner's Loge in ‌the Ring ​is the nineteenth ‌century's conflation ​of Loki with ‌Logi rendered ​as opera, the ‌cynical flame ​who talks ​the gods into ‌their bargains.[2]

‌The twentieth and ‌twenty-first centuries ​restored his ambiguity ​at mass ‌scale: Neil Gaiman's ​'Low Key ​Lyesmith' in ‌American Gods (2001) ​hides the ​bound god in ‌a prison ​cell, and Marvel's ‌Loki — ​Þórr's adopted brother ​and rival, ​on page ​from 1962 and ‌on screen ‌from 2011 — ​has carried ‌the name further ​than the ​Eddas ever traveled, ‌at the ‌price of ‌thinning the mythology ‌to a ​family quarrel. Modern ‌heathenry remains ​honestly divided: many ​Ásatrú kindreds ​refuse to hail ‌Loki at ​blót, reading ​him as the ​oath-breaker the ‌Eddas describe, while ​others honor ​the necessary disturber ​without whom ‌nothing in Ásgarðr ​changes.[3] The ‌quietest survivals ​are idiomatic: Scandinavian ‌folk usage ​long attached his ‌name to ‌the shimmering air ​over hot ​ground and to ​knot-tying spiders ​— small, ‌unsettled things that ‌refuse to ​hold still.[4]

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

One ​carved stone ‌is nearly ‌the whole ​candidate corpus ​for Loki's ​image. ​The Snaptun ​stone (Denmark, ‌c. 1000), ​a hearth-stone ​or bellows-shield, ‌bears ​a bearded ‌face with ​scarred, sewn-shut ‌lips and ​is commonly ​identified ​as Loki ​from the ​Brokkr episode ‌— an ​identification ‌widely repeated ​but unprovable, ‌since the ​stone carries ‌no inscription.[1] ​The ‌Kirkby Stephen ​stone in ​Cumbria, showing ‌a bound ‌horned figure, ‌has ​likewise been ​read as ‌the bound ​Loki, though ​other scholars ‌read ​it as ​a bound ‌devil of ​Christian iconography.[2]

‌Beyond these ​disputed ​stones the ​attestation is ​textual: the ‌Poetic Edda ‌poems survive ​chiefly ‌in the ​Codex Regius ​(GKS 2365 ‌4to, c. ‌1270) and ‌the ‌Hauksbók, and ​Snorri's account ‌is transmitted ​by the ​principal Snorra ​Edda ‌manuscripts (GKS ​2367 4to, ​Codex Wormianus, ​Codex Upsaliensis). ‌No ​runic inscription ​names the ​god, and ‌no Scandinavian ​place-name is ‌securely ​his — ‌a silence ​that matches ‌his standing: ‌the one ​major ​figure of ​the pantheon ​with no ‌temple, toponym, ‌or votive ​deposit.[3]

Scholarly Sources

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Poetic Edda

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The Poetic ​Edda knows ‌Loki at full ‌length, and ​its two great ​witnesses pull ​in opposite directions. ​The Lokasenna ​is his ‌tragedy in one ​night: barred ​from Ægir's feast, ‌he kills ​the servant Fimafeng, ‌returns on ​the strength of ‌the blood-oath ​with Óðinn (st. ​9 — ​'we two blended ​our blood ​together; you said ‌you would ​never taste ‌ale unless it ​were brought ‌to us both'), ​and works ‌down the bench ​insulting every ‌god and goddess ​until Þórr's ​hammer ends the ‌night. The ‌prose epilogue carries ‌him to ​Franangr, the salmon-shape, ​the net, ‌and the ​binding, naming Narfi ​and the ‌wolf-changed Váli.[1] The ​Þrymskviða shows ​the companion: Loki ‌in Freyja's ​feather-coat, Loki as ‌the quick-tongued ​bridesmaid whose lies ​('she has ​not eaten for ​eight nights') ‌hold Þrymr's ‌hall together until ​Mjǫllnir is ‌in Þórr's lap.[2]

​Around these, ​the mythological poems ‌fix his ‌cosmic role. The ‌Vǫluspá's sibyl ‌sees him bound ​— 'there ‌sits Sigyn, not ​at all ​glad over her ​husband' — ‌and sees ​him free at ​the end, ​steering the ship ‌from the ​east as 'the ​kinsman of ​Býleistr'.[3] Hyndluljóð gives ‌the genealogy ​in compressed form: ‌Loki fathered ​the wolf on ‌Angrboða, bore ​Sleipnir by Svaðilfari, ‌and is ‌brother to ​Býleistr — the ​poem's one ​stanza holding his ​whole family, ‌monstrous and equine, ‌in a ​single breath.[4]

Sources

  1. Lokasenna (Poetic Edda), with prose epilogue, trans. Larrington (2014).
  2. Þrymskviða (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).
  3. Vǫluspá (Poetic Edda), the bound-Loki and Ragnarök stanzas, trans. Larrington (2014).
  4. Hyndluljóð (Poetic Edda), the Loki stanza, trans. Larrington (2014).

Prose Edda

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The Prose ​Edda is ‌Loki's fullest witness, ‌and Snorri's systematic ​frame lets ​the whole arc ​be read in ​order. Gylfaginning ​34–35 introduces him ‌in the catalog ​of the ​Æsir with his ‌pedigree — ​son of Fárbauti ‌and Laufey, brother ​of Helblindi ‌and Býleistr — ​and his monstrous ​parenthood by ​Angrboða: Fenrir, Jǫrmungandr, ​and Hel, the ​three the ‌gods bind, drown, ​and banish ‌in advance.[1] The ​Útgarðr journey (Gylfaginning ‌44–47) gives ​him the eating ‌contest against Logi, ​wildfire itself, ‌and surrounds him ​with the name-double ​Útgarða-Loki — ‌the giant king ‌whose illusions ‌humiliate Þórr, and ​whom Snorri is ​careful never ‌to identify with ​Loki Laufeyjarson.[2]

The ​Baldr cycle ‌(Gylfaginning 49) is ​Snorri's great narrative: ​the oath ‌taken from all ​things, the ‌overlooked mistletoe, Loki's ​woman-shape prying the ​secret from ​Frigg, the guided ​hand of the ‌blind Hǫðr, ‌and — after ​Hermóðr's ride — ‌the giantess ​Þǫkk's dry tears, ​'and this ‌people believe was ‌Loki'.[3] The binding ‌(Gylfaginning 50) ‌and Ragnarök (Gylfaginning ​51) complete the ‌arc: the ​entrails of Nari, ​the three stones, ​Skadi's serpent, ‌Sigyn's bowl, the ​earthquakes; then ​Naglfar, the steersman, ​and the mutual ‌killing with ​Heimdallr.[4] Skáldskaparmál preserves ​the poets' Loki: ​the kenning-list ‌with its verdicts ​('slanderer of the ‌gods', 'thief ​of the gods'), ‌the Sif-hair ​tale, the wager ‌of the head ‌with Brokkr ​and Eitri, the ​theft and recovery ​of Iðunn, ​and the capture ‌by Geirröðr that ‌sends Þórr ​out unarmed.[5]

Runic Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The ​runic corpus ‌contains ‌no ​attestation of ​Loki: ​no runestone ​invokes ​him, ‌no amulet ​bears ​his name, ‌and ​no ‌inscription links ​a ‌dedicant to ​any ​cult ​of his ​— ​a silence ‌consistent ​with ‌the broader ​absence ‌of Loki ​from ‌cult ​and toponymy.[1]

‌What ​can be ​offered ‌is ‌the epigraphic ‌reconstruction. ​In the ​Younger ‌Futhark, ​the sixteen-rune ​row ‌of the ​Viking ​Age, ‌the name ​would ‌have ​been carved ​approximately ​ᛚᚢᚴᛁ (l-u-k-i): ​the ‌ᚢ-rune, ‌which covered ​the ‌u- and ​o-family ​vowels ‌alike, would ‌write ‌the short ‌o, ​and ‌the runes ​would ​underspecify nothing ​the ‌name ​actually contains. ​The ​reconstruction is ‌a ​frame, ​not a ​witness ‌— the ​normalized ‌manuscript ​spelling Loki ‌remains ​the primary ‌attestation, ‌and ​it is ​identical ​to the ​ASCII ‌form ‌the restoration ​carries.[2]

Sources

  1. Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide (Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. Loki.
  2. Gordon, E. V., An Introduction to Old Norse, 2nd ed. rev. Taylor (Clarendon Press, 1957), on the Younger Futhark.

Sagas & Medieval Sources

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Outside ​the two ‌Eddas, Loki's ‌medieval footprint ​is thinner ​than his ​modern fame ​suggests. The ​skaldic corpus ‌preserves him ​chiefly ​through the ‌mythological shield-poems: ​Þjóðólfr of ‌Hvin's Haustlöng ​(c. 900), ‌quoted extensively ​in Skáldskaparmál, ​knows the ​Þjazi episode ​— ​Loki captured, ‌Iðunn stolen ​and recovered, ‌the giant ​burned at ‌Ásgarðr's wall ​— and ‌names Loki ​as ‌Þórr's companion ​at the ​duel with ‌Hrungnir; Þjóðólfr's ‌verses are ‌among the ​oldest datable ​witnesses to ‌the god.[1] ​Eilífr ​Goðrúnarson's Þórsdrápa ‌continues the ​companion-role into ​the Geirröðr ‌journey.[2]

Saxo ​Grammaticus, who ‌euhemerizes the ​gods at ​length in ​the ​Gesta Danorum, ‌gives no ‌corresponding narrative ​role to ‌a Loki ​figure — ​a reminder ‌that the ‌flyting-and-binding cycle ‌is ‌specifically the ​Eddic tradition's ‌property. The ​post-medieval folk ​record then ​reverses the ‌polarity: the ​Faroese Lokka ​táttur ​ballad casts ‌Lokki as ​the clever ​helper who ​saves a ‌farmer's son ​from a ‌giant where ​Óðinn and ‌Þórr ​cannot — ‌evidence, as ‌Rooth stressed, ​that a ​benevolent Loki ​survived in ​oral tradition ‌independent of ‌the Eddic ​damnation.[3]

Sources

  1. Þjóðólfr of Hvin, Haustlöng, as preserved in Skáldskaparmál, trans. Faulkes (1987).
  2. Eilífr Goðrúnarson, Þórsdrápa, as preserved in Skáldskaparmál, trans. Faulkes (1987).
  3. Rooth, Anna Birgitta, Loki in Scandinavian Mythology (CWK Gleerup, 1961), on the Lokka táttur.

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

Loki is ​the god ‌of the ‌loophole, and ​the tradition ​built his ​ending out ​of his ​own materials. The ‌net he ​invented catches ​him; the ‌sons he ​fathered supply ‌the rope; ​the wife ‌he wronged with ​his shape-shifting ​stands over ​him with ​the bowl; ​and the ‌earthquakes his ​writhing causes ‌are the world's ​flinch at ‌what it ​cannot quite ‌punish away.[1]

​If the ‌uncertain etymology ​is even ​half right — ‌the luk- ‌root, the ‌knot, the ​lock, the ​closing — ‌then the ​name carried ​the whole story ‌from the ​start: the ​mind that ‌unties every ​oath, and ‌the god ​who ends ​tied in the ​one knot ​he cannot ‌pick. The ‌Eddas never ​decide whether ‌he saved ​the gods ​or damned them, ‌because he ‌did both, ‌with the ‌same wit, ​in the ‌same lifetime. ​The honest ​question the myth ​leaves is ‌not whether ​to love ​him or ​hate him, ‌but whether ​a fellowship ​can survive its ​own indispensable ‌saboteur — ​and the ‌Eddic answer, ​written in ‌Baldr's blood ​and the ‌nails of Naglfar, ‌is that ​it cannot, ​and keeps ​him at ​the table ‌anyway, until ‌the hall ​itself burns.[2]

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 50, trans. Faulkes (1987).
  2. Lokasenna (Poetic Edda); Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 49–51, trans. Larrington (2014) and Faulkes (1987).
